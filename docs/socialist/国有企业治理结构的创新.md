# Chapter 5: Innovation in the Corporate Governance Structure of State-Owned Enterprises

> **Core question of this chapter**: After an enterprise is restructured, who controls the managers? "Shareholder primacy" or "stakeholder co-governance"? When banks, employees, and minority shareholders have all invested in specific assets, how should the company's control rights move "contingently" with the state of operations?

## Overview of This Chapter

Chapter 4 resolved who the enterprise "belongs to"; this chapter resolves who manages the enterprise and how. The starting point is the principal-agent relationship: the separation of capital ownership from control is the primary cause of its emergence — the owner hands operations to professional managers, and the agent, besides monetary income, also pursues non-monetary goods (consumption items that are usually not bought or sold for money but likewise yield utility: status, power, comfort, prestige), so the agency problem arises — the agent uses the authorization granted by the principal and, in pursuing the maximization of his own utility, takes actions that harm the principal (the typical form of moral hazard). This framework was opened empirically by Berle and Means in 1932 with their study of the "separation of ownership and control" in American joint-stock companies, and was given its definitive form by the agency-cost theory of Jensen and Meckling in 1976; the Austrian school and information economics (Hayek 1945, Akerlof 1970, the signaling theory of Spence and Stiglitz) supplied the analytical tools of information asymmetry. The entire design of governance structure is a search, under conditions of information asymmetry, for the lowest-cost mechanism to "keep the agent under control": the principal needs to obtain information about the agent's behavior (three channels — the measurement of profit indicators, measurement by means of the stock market, and direct measurement of behavior), mechanism design must satisfy the two basic conditions of incentive compatibility and individual rationality (participation) constraints, the structure of managerial remuneration (salary, bonus, shares and share purchase options, pension plans) is the core incentive instrument, while the labor market for managers, the product market, and the capital market (the threat of takeover) constitute the three external constraints.

But the traditional governance view of "shareholder primacy" meets challenges in reality: the enterprise is not the private property of shareholders, but a nexus of a series of contracts — the equality and independence of the property-rights holders implicit behind the contracts require a pluralization of the subjects of governance structure. Two theoretical supports back this turn. The first is a complete understanding of the connotation of property rights: property rights, while recognizing the reasonableness and legality of one person's pursuit and protection of one's own property-rights interests, emphasize that the exercise of property rights must respect and protect the interests of others — the pluralization of governance subjects is the logical extension of the modern connotation of property rights. The second is the property-rights character of human capital: bounded rationality and the tendency toward opportunism constrain the rational choice of the economic man, and human capital is not wholly separable from its owner (the difficulty of incentives lies precisely here); the degree of pledgeability of human capital indicates that its owner may be able to share in enterprise ownership, and the conditions that turn possibility into reality are specificity (the accumulation of specific human capital begets future bargaining power, which can change an established distribution of interests) and the team character (the essence of the enterprise is team production or a bundle of long-term contracts, and human capital and non-human capital depend on each other). Since creditors and employees have both invested specific assets in the enterprise, the arrangement of enterprise ownership (claim rights and control rights) should be opened to them — from this follows the design of co-governance and contingent governance. International comparison also provides a reference: the Anglo-American model is dominated by external governance through market constraints (a developed securities market exerts enormous external constraint, and the internal structure is shareholders' meeting — board of directors — management), while the German-Japanese model is more deeply constrained by law and by government intervention in the economic structure, with internal governance centered on the separation and balance of powers. This chapter then analyzes the real form of governance in Chinese SOEs — manager control under administrative intervention — and offers two innovative designs: the co-governance mechanism and the contingent-governance mechanism.

## Core Concepts

### The Corporate Governance Structure

The corporate governance structure is also called the legal-person governance structure of the company; it refers to the unified mechanism through which owners, operators, and supervisors form relations among themselves — with clearly defined rights and responsibilities, mutual checks, coordinated operation, and scientific decision-making — via the company's organ of power (the shareholders' meeting), the organs of operating decision and execution (the board of directors and the manager), and the supervisory organ (the supervisory board), and which is institutionalized in accordance with laws, regulations, rules, and the company's articles of association.

Why is a specialized structure needed to "govern" the enterprise? Because after the separation of the two rights, the enterprise faces two kinds of problems at once: the agent may harm the principal (the agency problem), and the interests of multiple stakeholders need to be protected simultaneously. The governance structure is an in-enterprise replica of the idea of the separation of powers — decision rights (the board of directors), executive rights (the management level), and supervisory rights (the supervisory board) separated and mutually balanced — and the root of it is that "who has the say under what state of affairs" is written into the institutions in advance, so that every conflict does not have to be renegotiated from scratch. The governance structure is therefore a device for allocating enterprise ownership, not merely a diagram of institutional arrangements.

### Moral Hazard and Agency Costs

Moral hazard refers to the situation in which, owing to information asymmetry, a person engaged in economic activity takes actions that are detrimental to others while maximizing his own utility; it not only harms the party at an informational disadvantage but also disrupts the existing market equilibrium and leads to inefficient allocation of resources. Agency costs refer to the costs inevitably induced by the introduction of an agency arrangement, under conditions of separation between ownership and control, because the utility functions of principal and agent are not entirely consistent; they include: the expenses such as salaries and bonuses paid to the agent; the rise in costs and reduction in profits caused by the agent's pursuit of non-monetary goods; and the loss of enterprise efficiency caused by the difference between the agent's decisions and the optimal decisions that would maximize the principal's profit.

Why are this pair of concepts the "cost accounting" of governance design? Because every governance mechanism spends money to buy a reduction in agency costs: supervision and constraint themselves have costs, and a rational principal achieves an equilibrium of external constraints at the level where the marginal cost of constraint equals agency cost, minimizing the sum of the two — not stricter the better, but a marginal trade-off. There are two routes to lowering agency costs: internally designing an optimal incentive-constraint mechanism (remuneration structure, board supervision), and making use of market competition — competition in the labor market for managers (the market computes the future value of a manager's human capital from his past performance, so a manager concerned with long-run interests must exert effort), competition in the product market (a fully competitive output market exerts pressure on the manager), and competition in the capital market (whose substance is the contest for control of the company, the main form of which is takeover — takeover is regarded as the last weapon against managers harming shareholders' interests).

### Incentive Compatibility and Participation Constraints

Incentive compatibility refers to the condition that, since the agent is the acceptor of the contract, the incentives provided by the mechanism must be sufficient to induce the agent to choose voluntarily the contract set according to his own type. The participation constraint (individual rationality constraint) requires that the utility the agent gets from accepting the contract be no less than his reservation utility — accepting the contract is economically more worthwhile than refusing it, thereby guaranteeing the agent's interest motivation to take part in the mechanism-design game.

These two conditions are the mathematical formulation that mechanism-design theory (the Hurwicz–Myerson tradition) gives to the governance problem. Why is either one indispensable? Satisfy only the participation constraint and the agent will show up, but may "work while lying" — hiding his type or shirking; satisfy only incentive compatibility and the choice is honest, but the agent may not participate at all. The design of an optimal incentive-constraint mechanism must satisfy both constraints simultaneously, and its core idea is to convert the agent's pursuit of personal utility into a pursuit of maximizing enterprise profit — the mechanism does no moralizing, but makes honest effort the agent's optimal strategy.

### Enterprise Ownership

Enterprise ownership refers to the claim rights and control rights over the enterprise's residual — relative to the fixed returns (wages, interest) agreed in advance by contract, to whom the residual income belongs and to whom the residual control (the decision rights not specified in the contract) belongs. Why is enterprise ownership different from "property ownership"? Property ownership answers to whom the assets belong; enterprise ownership answers "who fills the gaps of the enterprise as a contract": the enterprise faces an uncertain world, contracts cannot exhaust all states, and the decision rights and residual income in unspecified states must be claimed by somebody — and the core of the governance structure is precisely this scheme of claiming. The traditional logic of "shareholder primacy" assigns the entire right of claim to shareholders; stakeholder theory instead argues for sharing according to the investment of specific assets and the bearing of risk. The arrangement of enterprise ownership is state-contingent — different states of operation correspond to different arrangements: shareholders dominate in normal operations, creditors take over when assets fall short of debts — and this is exactly the theoretical foundation of contingent governance.

### Insiders Control and Manager Control under Administrative Intervention

Insiders control refers to the situation in which the operators of state-owned enterprises gradually seize most of the control rights during the economic transition, and in which the acquisition of this control is often accomplished through a "collusion" with employees — it is a description of enterprise behavior during the Soviet and Eastern European transition: insiders pursue their own interests at the expense of the owners of capital, and the accompanying "weak government" that has lost control of the enterprise becomes the "prey" for rent-seeking by various interest groups. Manager control under administrative intervention is the form of governance that formed in China's SOE reform: reform is accomplished mainly by redistributing rights, responsibilities, and interests through the marketization and contractualization of the government-enterprise relation, with the government granting operators large operating rights and monitoring their behavior.

Why did China not replicate the Eastern European form of insiders control? Because its formation mechanism is different: the acquisition of control rights by managers of Chinese SOEs does not depend on "collusion" with employees, but on the government's authorization following one-to-one negotiation between the manager and the government department; after the separation of the two rights or the dispersal of shares, the decision rights of managers do not come from authorization by internal employees; the growth of employees' wage income does not necessarily mean erosion of enterprise profit; and the judgment of a "weak government" does not match China's reality — the government always retains the means of appointment, removal, and intervention. Manager control under administrative intervention has five features: the distribution of enterprise ownership is determined through one-to-one negotiation between the enterprise's government department and the operator, and the operator's autonomy is granted directly by the government; after receiving authorization the operator exercises decision rights independently, and the government incentivizes mainly through promotion in post, moral encouragement, and linking the operator's income to enterprise economic performance; although the operator grants employees certain rights through the employees' congress and the trade union, as legal representative he remains dominant in the distribution of enterprise ownership; the enterprise's government department acts as the operator's supervisor in order to protect state property rights; and the government's main instruments of supervision are the appointment and removal of operators and participation in or intervention in major enterprise decisions. Its defects are four: operators receive only incentives without corresponding constraints and penalties (the state still bears unlimited liability for the enterprise); there is only one-directional authority without multi-party checks; the consequence of individual decision-making and collective responsibility is that there is no genuine risk-bearer inside the enterprise; and information resources and the decision and supervision resources derived from them are wasted, while the legitimate rights of creditors and employees are ignored. These four defects are precisely the targets of governance innovation.

### Enterprise Co-Governance and the Contingent-Governance Mechanism

Enterprise co-governance emphasizes joint participation in decision-making and mutual checks in supervision: the board of directors and the supervisory board must include representatives of stakeholders other than shareholders (employee representatives, bank representatives, and so on). The core of co-governance is economic democratization — ensuring through formal institutional arrangements such as the company's articles that every property-rights holder has equal opportunity to participate in the distribution of enterprise ownership, relying on mutual supervision mechanisms to check the behavior of the various property-rights holders, and stabilizing the foundation of cooperation by means of appropriate voting mechanisms and interest-constraint mechanisms. The contingent-governance mechanism is the institutionalization of the state-contingency of enterprise ownership: if there is no pre-existing legal provision, the transfer of the dominant right in the distribution of enterprise ownership must be accomplished through voluntary negotiation among the property-rights holders; different states of operation reflect different patterns of interest distribution, and when one stakeholder's rights suffer serious injury, there must be a demand to change the established pattern and renegotiate — and the design purpose of the contingent-governance mechanism is to ensure that, under abnormal states of operation, the stakeholder who has suffered a loss has an appropriate institution in which to complete a willingness to renegotiate, changing the established pattern of interests mainly through the contest for control rights and constituting an external constraint on the behavior of enterprise decision-makers.

Why does governance innovation need contingent governance in addition to co-governance? Because the two solve problems of different periods: co-governance solves "who participates in ordinary times" — bringing the various stakeholders into the board of directors and the supervisory board, realizing democratic decision-making and balanced supervision; contingent governance solves "who takes over when things go wrong" — not disturbing the operator under normal conditions, but letting subjects such as creditors take over control rights by procedure when signals of abnormal states such as financial deterioration appear. A complete contingent-governance mechanism contains three elements: a subject able to use the mechanism, signals, and the contingent-governance procedure; the procedure divides into three stages — ex ante supervision (making a reasonable assessment before investing specific assets of the investment target's production capacity, development prospects, management and organizational ability, rate of return, and potential risks, in order to prevent, under information asymmetry, adverse selection and signaling manipulation through overestimation of returns and underestimation of risks), concurrent supervision (closely watching the enterprise's operating conditions and the managers' behavior, preventing moral hazard, generally accomplished by the supervisory board), and ex post supervision (judging the enterprise's future through company performance and preventing financial crisis: not interfering when operations are good, but applying penalties when signs of financial crisis appear — penalties must be credible, for lenient treatment encourages irresponsibility, shirking, or embezzlement, and the most credible penalty is enterprise restructuring, that is, adjusting the leadership team, resorting to the law when necessary).

## Questions and Analysis

### What is the agency problem? How is an incentive-constraint mechanism designed?

The agency problem refers to the situation in which, after owners and operators form a principal-agent relationship because of the separation of the two rights, the two parties' objective functions are not entirely consistent, and the agent, holding control of the enterprise, may use the principal's authorization to engage in activities that harm the principal's interests. The universal difficulty of design lies in information asymmetry: when the principal asks the agent about the information of his type, the agent will not tell the truth unless there is a monetary payment or some instrument of control serving as incentive and price — and therefore obtaining information about the agent's behavior is an important condition for designing an optimal incentive-constraint mechanism.

There are three channels of information: the measurement of profit indicators (determining the effort and ability expended by the agent by directly observing the magnitude of enterprise profit, the drawback being that profit is disturbed by market noise), measurement by means of the stock market (measuring the agent's behavior by observing fluctuations in the share price of a listed company, bringing market expectations into the assessment), and direct measurement of behavior (the principal directly observes the agent's behavior, which is costly and may induce window dressing). Mechanism design must satisfy two constraints: the incentive-compatibility constraint — the incentives provided by the mechanism must be able to induce the agent to choose voluntarily the contract set according to his type; and the participation constraint — the utility from accepting the contract must be no lower than reservation utility. The core of incentives is to convert the agent's pursuit of personal utility into a pursuit of maximizing enterprise profit; to address the manager's pursuit of monetary income, the owner can achieve incentive and constraint by setting an optimal remuneration plan — managerial remuneration generally consists of salary, bonus, shares and share purchase options, and pension plans, and each form has its own advantages and disadvantages (salary is stable but provides no incentive, bonus is linked to current profit but induces short-term behavior, shares and options tie the manager's wealth to the company's long-run value, and a pension is "golden handcuffs"), and the optimal remuneration design should be the optimal combination of all the different forms of remuneration.

### Why must governance-structure innovation move beyond the predicament of "shareholder primacy"?

The logic of "shareholder primacy" meets four predicaments in state-owned enterprises. First, the absence of the owner makes "shareholder" itself a nullity: state assets must have a representative who exercises their rights — if the government department is the representative, it will face the problem of "cheap talk rights" (the voter does not bear the consequences of voting, and constraint degenerates into form); if the operator is the representative, then rights expand without limit, leading to unconstrained free disposal and injury to owners' equity. Second, shareholder primacy requires that most operating and production decision rights be delegated to the operator, while the democratic centralism of state-owned enterprises again requires collective decision-making and collective responsibility — the consequence of combining the two is that the operator often decides in the name of the collective while bearing no consequences himself. Third, the participation and supervision rights that ordinary employees should have are reduced under the logic of shareholder primacy to formalistic tools, wasting human resources. Fourth, the combination of state capital with government power allows the operator to arbitrarily strip the rights of other minority shareholders and creditors.

The direction out of the predicament is to recognize the logic of stakeholder cooperation: the company's goal is to serve stakeholders, not merely to maximize shareholders' interests; the cooperation logic does not deny each property-rights holder's self-interested pursuit, but emphasizes that rational property-rights holders regard the company's adaptive capacity as the source of their own interests, giving every property-rights holder the chance to participate in the distribution of enterprise ownership, so that the actual structure of ownership distribution is the result of mutual negotiation among the property-rights holders. The theoretical basis was already given in the overview section: the reciprocity of the exercise of property rights requires respect for others' interests; and the specificity and team character of human capital make employees and creditors de facto risk-bearers. SOE governance innovation thereby unfolds into two mechanisms: co-governance within the board of directors — establishing a system of employee directors (employee directors entering the board of directors is an important part of perfecting co-governance), with banks able to enter the board of directors lawfully through proxy voting or a trust system (protecting the rights of minority shareholders and banks); and co-governance within the supervisory board — employee representatives and bank representatives entering the supervisory board in appropriate fashion, ensuring that all property-rights holders equally enjoy supervisory rights and achieve mutual checks.

### What does the empirical analysis of the efficiency of SOE governance structures show?

International comparison of governance models shows two prototypes: in the Anglo-American model external governance is dominated by market constraints, and a very developed securities market exerts enormous external constraint on enterprise operations, with the internal structure composed of the shareholders' meeting, the board of directors, and the management level; in the German-Japanese model the government intervenes more deeply in changes in the economic structure and often uses special industrial and technology policies to influence development, internal governance is constrained by law, and the central content is a balancing separation of powers (bank shareholding, cross-shareholding, and main-bank supervision). Neither model is absolutely superior — the model with strong market constraints protects investors directly but easily induces short-term behavior; the model with strong relational constraints favors long-run investment but may protect backwardness.

Judgments about SOE behavior in our country's transition period need careful distinction. Describing transition-period SOEs in terms of "insiders control" is not entirely applicable (the concepts section gave four reasons: control rights come from government authorization rather than collusion with employees, decision rights do not come from employee authorization, wage growth does not equal profit erosion, and the judgment of a "weak government" does not match reality). A more accurate characterization is manager control under administrative intervention: it has the double character of administrative intervention (the government retains the right of appointment and removal and of intervening in major decisions) and of operator control (after authorization it independently exercises decision rights and occupies a dominant position in the distribution of ownership) — it is neither the Eastern European style of managerial revolution nor genuine owner control. Its four defects (only incentives without constraints or penalties, only one-directional authority without multi-party checks, individual decision-making with collective responsibility, and the waste of information resources with stakeholders' rights ignored) explain the tenacity of governance problems after SOE restructuring: the shareholding reform changed the enterprise's form, but did not automatically change its governance logic. This is precisely where the necessity of institutional innovation lies — co-governance brings multi-party checks into the picture, contingent governance establishes credible penalties, and only the combination of the two can make up the governance gap of "too much incentive, too little constraint."

## Comprehensive Discussion

### The Plurality of Governance Subjects from the Property-Rights Characteristics of Human Capital

(1) The connotation of property rights implies the pluralization of subjects. Modern enterprise theory regards the enterprise as a combination of a series of contracts; the equality and independence of the property-rights holders implicit behind the contracts require that the subjects of the corporate governance structure stand in a relation of equality and independence with one another, and these interconnected subjects together compose the "stakeholders." The basic connotation of property rights, while recognizing the reasonableness and legality of one person's pursuit and protection of one's own property-rights interests, emphasizes that due consideration be given to the respect for and protection of others' interests in the exercise of property rights — the pluralization of the subjects of corporate governance structure is the logical extension of the modern connotation of property rights.

(2) The pledgeability of human capital implies the possibility of sharing ownership. Bounded rationality and the tendency toward opportunism constrain the economic man's rational choice behavior; the agent faces constraints when directing the human capital that belongs to him in order to maximize, which means that human capital is not wholly separable from its owner. The degree of pledgeability of human capital can only indicate that its owner may possibly share in enterprise ownership, and does not necessarily acquire that right — whether possibility becomes reality depends on a series of conditions that affect the bargaining power of the parties to the contract, of which the specificity and team character of human capital are most important. First, specificity is an important basis for participation in governance: the accumulation of specific human capital begets future bargaining power, and can thereby change an established distribution of interests — an engineer who possesses skills unique to a given enterprise will see his human capital depreciate sharply if he leaves that enterprise, and he therefore has both motive and standing to care about the enterprise's long-run fate. Second, the essence of the enterprise is team production or a bundle of long-term contracts, and the team essence manifests as mutual dependence between human capital and non-human capital — output is the result of joint action, and no party can easily claim all the rights to the outcome alone.

(3) From theory to institution. Since employees bear risk with specific human capital and creditors bear risk with specific financial assets, the governance structure should provide them with channels of participation: the system of employee directors and employee supervisors, and the creditor's proxy-voting or trust system. This argument also answers "why SOE restructuring cannot simply copy shareholder primacy": employees of state-owned enterprises have made a historical investment in the enterprise over the long run in the form of high welfare for low wages, and their specificity is stronger, so co-governance for them is not a favor but the recognition of property rights.

### The Logic of Stakeholder Cooperation and the Realization of "Co-Governance" in SOEs

The essential difference between the logic of stakeholder cooperation and the logic of shareholder primacy lies in the company's goal: the former holds that the company should serve stakeholders, not merely pursue the maximization of shareholders' interests. The cooperation logic does not deny each property-rights holder's self-interested pursuit, but emphasizes that rational property-rights holders regard the company's adaptive capacity as the source of their own interests — a governance structure embodying the cooperation logic must give every property-rights holder the opportunity to participate in the distribution of enterprise ownership, so that the actual structure of ownership distribution is the result of mutual negotiation among the property-rights holders.

The governance structure that implements the cooperation logic is the co-governance mechanism. Its core is economic democratization: ensuring through formal institutional arrangements such as the company's articles that every property-rights holder has equal opportunity to participate in the distribution of enterprise ownership; relying on mutual supervision mechanisms to check the behavior of the various property-rights holders; and stabilizing the foundation of cooperation by means of appropriate voting and interest-constraint mechanisms, so that the behavior of the property-rights holders is unified in the common goal of improving the enterprise's adaptive capacity. The co-governance model includes two parallel mechanisms: co-governance within the board of directors, ensuring that property-rights holders have equal opportunity to participate in the company's major decisions; and co-governance within the supervisory board, ensuring that all property-rights holders equally enjoy supervisory rights and achieve mutual checks.

The concrete realization in SOE governance innovation: at the board level, establish a system of employee directors — employee directors entering the board of directors is an important part of perfecting the co-governance mechanism; and a system of bank directors — banks entering the board of directors and the supervisory board lawfully through proxy voting or a trust system, realizing the protection of the rights of minority shareholders and banks. At the supervisory board level, employee representatives and bank representatives enter the company's supervisory board in appropriate fashion. Taken together, the two mechanisms turn "multi-party checks" and "protection of stakeholders' rights" from slogans into operable institutional arrangements, addressing three of the four defects of operator control under administrative intervention — one-directional authority, nobody bearing responsibility, stakeholders being ignored — while the first (only incentives without constraints or penalties) must be filled in by the contingent-governance mechanism: credible penalties under crisis conditions.

### The Basic Meaning and Basic Procedure of the Contingent-Governance Mechanism

Basic meaning: viewed dynamically, the diversification of the forms of enterprise ownership arrangements is precisely their state-contingency — different states of enterprise operation correspond to different arrangements of enterprise ownership. If there is no pre-existing legal provision, the transfer of the dominant right in the distribution of enterprise ownership must be accomplished through voluntary negotiation among the property-rights holders, and this institution is the contingent-governance mechanism. Its foundation is the state-contingent character of enterprise ownership: different states of operation reflect different patterns of interest distribution, and when one stakeholder's rights suffer serious injury there must be a demand to change the established pattern of interests and renegotiate; the design purpose of the mechanism is to ensure that, under abnormal states of operation, the stakeholder who has suffered a loss has an appropriate institution in which to complete a willingness to renegotiate. The mechanism changes the established pattern of interests mainly through the contest for control rights, and who holds control depends on credible knowledge of a certain random variable (a signal of the state of operation); it is directed at enterprise decision-makers and constitutes an external constraint on their behavior. The complete mechanism contains three elements: the subject of contingent governance (the person able to use the mechanism), signals, and the contingent-governance procedure.

The basic procedure divides into three stages. Ex ante supervision: before each subject of contingent governance invests specific assets in the enterprise, it makes a reasonable assessment of the investment target — the content covering the enterprise's production capacity, development prospects, management and organizational ability, rate of return, and potential risks, with the aim of preventing, under conditions of information asymmetry, the adverse-selection behavior of the party with the informational advantage (overestimating the rate of return, underestimating risks, manipulating signals). Concurrent supervision: closely watching the enterprise's operating conditions and the behavior of the managers, preventing the occurrence of moral-hazard behavior — the managers' interest objectives are not entirely consistent with those of other stakeholders, and they may sacrifice the latter's rights to satisfy their own; concurrent supervision is generally accomplished by the supervisory board. Ex post supervision: judging the enterprise's future through company performance (generally its financial condition), with the aim of preventing financial crisis; according to different performance levels the subject of contingent governance takes corresponding action — not interfering when operations are good and leaving managers free to dispose of company business, but applying penalties when signs of financial crisis appear. The key is that penalties must be credible: if conduct that should be punished is treated leniently or merely criticized and made subject to reflection, it will encourage managers' irresponsibility, shirking, or embezzlement; the most credible penalty is enterprise restructuring — what is usually called adjusting the leadership team, resorting to the law when necessary. The three stages together form a complete chain of risk management: screening out bad projects before entry (ex ante), watching behavior while in post (concurrent), and replacing and taking over when things go wrong (ex post) — control rights thereby become a real constraint hanging over the operator, and not merely a moral condemnation after the fact.

## Limitations and Follow-Ups

Four coordinates of governance innovation need supplementary record. First, **the practical gap of co-governance**: the system of employee directors was written into law after the 2005 revision of the Company Law (employee directors in wholly state-owned companies are a statutory requirement; democratic management through the employees' congress was an existing provision in every text of the Company Law, and the 2018 amendment touched only the share buy-back clauses), but in practice the representativeness and substantive participation of employee directors have long been questioned as formalistic; the system of bank directors, in China where the main-bank system has not taken shape and the banks' own marketization is incomplete, has not become widespread — in contingent governance the creditors' actual takeover manifests mainly as banks restructuring the debts of troubled enterprises rather than transferring control, and the low rate of enforcement of the bankruptcy system makes "ex post penalties" often absent. Second, **the updating of governance tools**: the system of independent directors introduced in 2001 became the Chinese variant of Anglo-American external governance (disputes over its independence and effectiveness have continued to the present), and institutional investors, cumulative voting, electronic voting, and ESG disclosure have gradually entered the governance framework; since 2017, "Party building in the articles" at SOEs has formed another governance arrangement — Party organizations embedded in the corporate governance structure, and the articulation of this with the board's statutory powers is a new topic for governance research. Third, **the sluggishness of the control-rights market**: the threat of takeover in the capital market (which the textbook calls "the last weapon against managers harming shareholders' interests") has long been blunted in the A-share market by institutional constraints such as the split share structure (the split-share reform was only completed in 2005) and the approval of transfers of state shares, and hostile takeover cases remain rare even after 2010 — this shortcoming in external governance shows that governance-structure reform cannot draw its blueprints only at the board level, but also depends on the overall development of the capital market and the rule-of-law environment. Fourth, **the theory's own evolution**: stakeholder theory, since being systematized by Freeman in 1984, has experienced a crescendo from "how do stakeholders influence governance" to "stakeholder capitalism" (the 2019 statement of the U.S. Business Roundtable), and has also met the criticism that "plural objectives = no objective"; for our country, a more operable direction is to ground co-governance in verifiable institutional details (nomination and voting rules for employee directors, the information rights of creditors' committees) and to ground contingent governance in the rigidity of enforcement of the bankruptcy law and the delisting system — the Chinese page of governance theory is still being written.
