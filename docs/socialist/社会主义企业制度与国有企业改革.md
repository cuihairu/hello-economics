# Chapter 4 · The socialist enterprise system and the reform of state-owned enterprises

> **The central question of this chapter**: for an enterprise owned by the whole people, who exactly is 'the' owner? Why do state property rights come into conflict with the market economy? What surgery on the property-right system is needed before the enterprise can become a genuine subject of the market?

## The arc of this chapter

The theoretical starting point of state-owned-enterprise reform is a set of questions about the relation of law to economics. Marx distinguished two senses of ownership: ownership as an economic relation, expressed in the activities of production, distribution, exchange and consumption under a given mode of production; and ownership as a legal relation, embodying relations of will and legal right, denoting the possessing subject's right of arbitrary disposal over the object possessed. A given mode of production gives rise to a given mode of possession; once economic relations of possession arise, a corresponding legal ownership arises to match them — ownership is the legal form of ownership relations, and the nature and content of ownership relations determine the nature and content of ownership. Marx also analysed the separation of the several powers of ownership (possession, use, income, disposition): as production socialises, the separation broadly takes two forms — the separation of the legal ownership of capital from its economic ownership (the money capitalist transfers economic ownership and obtains interest, the functioning capitalist obtains entrepreneurial income; in the joint-stock form this appears as the division of powers between shareholders and the corporate legal person, or as a principal-agent relation), and the separation of the function of capital from the functions of management and supervision (the joint-stock company separates the power to manage from the ownership of capital). These two kinds of separation supply the prototype of the modern enterprise system: ownership need not equal operation, and the owner need not manage personally.

Once the theoretical instruments are in place, the problem falls on the specialness of state property rights. Public property has four features: the indivisibility of the property right, the non-exclusivity of the right of use, externality, and the non-transferability of the residual claim; the state agency of public property is state ownership. The state, as an abstract subject, cannot exercise the owner's functions itself, and can only act through the government as agent, and the government in turn delegates down through a chain of administratively subordinate agencies — this forms the administrative-agency model of state property rights. Administrative agency does guarantee the state's hold on the residual claim and avoids the transaction costs of the market, but it pays heavy organisational and informational costs: the incentive cost is high — the principal's supervision is not a purely profit-making act but a public choice with high supervisory expense, and it is hard to design an incentive-constraint mechanism approximating market standards, while suppressing opportunism by lower-level agents costs incentives that rise as the scale of the state sector grows; the informational cost is high — information is lost cumulatively in transmission, and lower-level agents may also block it or pass on false information; the efficiency of constraint may fall — transactions inside a hierarchical organisation face strict barriers to entry and agency contracts are long-term in character, which breeds tendencies to forgive errors, to shield one another, and to shun risky innovation, together with the politicisation of the agency relation, softening the supervision and punishment of opportunistic behaviour; and influence costs are hard to avoid — agents at every level spend large amounts of time and energy lobbying superiors and building networks of personal relations so as to win a larger share of internal resource allocation, wasting resources and leading the principal into mistaken decisions. Add to this that under administrative agency government and enterprise are not separated and the enterprise lacks any mechanism of independent operation bearing its own profits and losses, and reform becomes inevitable: only by deepening the reform of state-owned enterprises can a micro-foundation be provided for the transition of the institutional model and the growth mode, market conditions can be created for the institutional switch, and the mode of macro control can be switched over. And the core means of reform is property-right system reform — clarifying property rights so that state-owned enterprises become genuine market subjects that operate autonomously and bear their own profits and losses.

The direction of property-right reform was already set out in Chapter 3 (from the hierarchy rule to the property-right rule); this chapter goes further to answer three questions. First, where exactly do state property rights conflict with the market economy? The essence of market transaction is a property-rights transaction in which transactors, on a basis of equality and voluntariness, pursue the maximisation of their utility functions; the conflict between state property rights and the market economy concentrates on two points: the conflict between the non-personified capital behaviour of the ownership subject and the demand that the enterprise operate state assets with profit as its goal — the government, as ownership subject, bears social responsibility and need not take the preservation and appreciation of state assets as its sole goal, whereas market competition demands that the enterprise maximise profit; and the conflict between the non-transferability of the residual claim and the demand that enterprise property rights be tradable — for the enterprise to become a genuine subject of market competition it must be able to dispose of its assets autonomously, to go bankrupt and to be taken over, and this depends on the transferability of the residual claim, but once the residual claim is transferred the state character of the enterprise becomes hard to maintain. Second, how to classify? According to the nature of the product the state-owned enterprise supplies and the differences between the industries it occupies, there are competitive enterprises and non-competitive enterprises (the latter in turn divided into enterprises supplying public goods and monopolistic enterprises engaged in basic industry and infrastructure), with policy applied by category: enterprises supplying public goods are state-owned and state-operated, natural-monopoly enterprises are state-owned and state-controlled, large and medium competitive enterprises undergo shareholding conversion, and small state-owned enterprises are opened up completely. Third, how is shareholding conversion carried out? Through the four-tier authorised-operation model — separation of government and enterprise on the basis of the separation of government functions from state-asset ownership, dispersal of the equity of state shares, independence of the company's property rights, and an efficient internal governance structure within the enterprise — so that property rights are clarified under the condition of public ownership as the mainstay.

## Core concepts

### Ownership and its powers

By Marx's definition, ownership is the property right that fixes the final attribution of a thing and denotes the subject's exclusive possession and monopoly of the definite thing, a property right existing on the same thing independently, not dependent on any other right. It summarises and confers the four powers the owner actually enjoys: the power of possession — the possibility of directly controlling the property, the rights-and-obligations relation between the owner and others arising from actual control of the property; the power of use — the possibility of employing the property according to its purpose without changing the nature of its ownership or possession, presupposing possession; the power of income — the possibility of obtaining the economic benefit arising from the owner's property, which in the capitalist economy shows chiefly as uncompensated appropriation of surplus value (the functioning capitalist's claim to entrepreneurial income, the money capitalist's claim to interest, the landowner's claim to rent); and the power of disposition — the legally guaranteed possibility of carrying out acts aimed at changing the economic use or state of the property, of which consumption is disposition in fact and transfer is disposition in law.

The definition of ownership carries three qualifications: the owner may exercise the right only within the limits the law permits; within the limits the law permits each right may be exercised freely; and others may not infringe it without permission. Why take the trouble to distinguish the four powers? Because the whole of the governance difficulties of the state-owned enterprise is hidden in the 'separation of powers': whole people (the ultimate owner) — state — government — enterprise operator, with the four powers of possession, use, income and disposition split apart and granted to different subjects, and any organisational form in which two rights are separated generates a principal-agent problem — Marx's remark on the joint-stock company that 'the power to manage is separated from the ownership of capital' is precisely the forerunner of modern principal-agent theory.

### Property rights and the property-right system

A property right is a right, realised through social coercion, to choose among the multiple uses of some economic good — not a single right but a bundle of rights, including the right of use, the right of income and the right of transfer; its effectiveness depends on the probability that the right is coerced into realisation and on the price paid for that coercion, which may rest on legal norms, contract and governmental coercion, or on ethical and moral norms. The property-right system is an institutional arrangement formed by the combination of established property-rights relations and property-right rules, able to organise, adjust and protect property-rights relations effectively, reflecting the legal and economic constraints of property interests and responsibilities among the owners, operators and users of property.

Why must economics forge a concept of 'property rights' beyond 'ownership'? Because ownership answers 'whose is the property', while property rights answer 'who may do what with the property' — the latter is the practical operating unit of transaction and incentive. By the Coase theorem, when transaction costs are positive the initial assignment of rights bears on the efficiency of resource allocation: the arrangement of property rights is not an inconsequential legal form, but the mechanism deciding whether resources flow to those best able to use them. The four conditions for clarifying property-rights relations — exclusivity, divisibility, transferability, effective protection — bring five benefits in return: they clearly delimit the boundary of the transaction, they standardise transactional behaviour, they let transactors form stable expectations of income, they raise the efficiency of cooperation, and they raise the efficiency of resource allocation. The five benefits jointly support one judgement: clarification of property rights is a necessary condition for the market mechanism to work effectively, and this is the theoretical basis of the proposition that 'the core of state-owned-enterprise reform is property-right reform'.

### Public goods and basic industries

Public goods are goods whose consumption is not exclusive (anyone's consumption of the good does not preclude others' simultaneous consumption) and not rival (it cannot be divided among consumers), and for which charging encounters difficulty; they divide into pure public goods supplied free to all citizens (national defence, the public-security system, public roads, and so on) and quasi-public goods supplied to consumers against some charge (public schools, public hospitals, and so on). Because everyone can free-ride, public goods cannot be adequately supplied by the market and must be acted on by the government. Basic industries are the groups of industries occupying a foundational position in the national economy and exerting a constraining and decisive influence on the development of other industries; they comprise basic industry (energy industry, mining, basic raw-material industry) and infrastructure (transport, airports, ports, communications, water conservancy, and so on), whose products are inputs indispensable to the processing of downstream industries, and whose investment is enormous in scale and marked by 'indivisibility'.

Why are these two concepts the yardstick for the classified reform of state-owned enterprises? Because 'what kind of product the enterprise supplies' fixes where market failure sits, and so fixes how the owner's functions are to be arranged: the free-rider problem of public goods makes private supply inadequate, so the government must supply them directly (state operation); the increasing returns to scale of a natural monopoly make multi-firm competition inefficient, so the government must control while a corporate form of operation may be introduced (state control); in competitive fields the market mechanism is effective, so the enterprise should be converted into a joint-stock company and compete on equal terms (shareholding conversion) — the nature of the product decides the institutional arrangement, and this is the logical skeleton of the classified reform strategy.

### The joint-stock company and the legal-person asset system

A joint-stock company is a company founded and organised by shareholders above a certain number, with all its capital divided into shares of equal value, in which shareholders bear limited liability to the company for the shares they subscribe and shares may be publicly issued and freely transferred; it is the principal organisational form of the legal-person asset system. The core features of the legal-person asset system include: the enterprise has independent legal-person status, and the legal person's assets consist of the capital the shareholders invest and the reserves set aside over the years; investors bear limited liability and risk only the amount of capital they invest; free transfer of shares maintains the integrity and independent movement of the legal person's assets — securities trading is separated from the movement of the company's assets, so that the shareholders' transactions on the stock market do not directly affect the legal person's assets; the relation between operator and company is defined — the operator's income, promotion and reputation are tied to the efficiency of asset operation and to the state of appreciation; sound and effective ownership constraint operates at two levels — shareholders impose equity constraint by 'voting with their hands' at the shareholders' meeting and 'voting with their feet' on the stock market, while the company's corporate organs impose legal-person ownership constraint on the operator by controlling the right to make major strategic decisions, the right to appoint and remove managers, and the right of supervision; and a high-quality team of entrepreneurs is required (the two kinds appearing as directors and as managers respectively) together with external constraints (hard market constraint and hard legal constraint).

Why is the shareholding form the decisive leap of state-owned-enterprise reform? Because it unties three dead knots at once: limited liability breaks the old pattern of 'the state bearing unlimited liability for the enterprise'; the transferability of shares gives a technical breakthrough to the state property-right attribute that 'the residual claim is not transferable'; and the legal person's property right lets the enterprise escape administrative control by governmental organs and operate independently. Of course, all this is 'technically feasible'; how in practice to prevent the state share from holding a single majority in the equity structure, and how to keep administrative interference from returning in another guise, are the entry point to the governance-structure questions of Chapter 5.

### Competitive state-owned enterprises

A competitive state-owned enterprise is one built with state investment, in which barriers to entry and exit are largely absent, in which numerous enterprises exist within the same industrial branch, whose product is broadly homogeneous and divisible, and which operates with profit as its goal; most are distributed across processing industry, construction, commerce and services. For such enterprises the four identifying features mean the state has no reason to maintain a monopoly — barrierless entry and exit and homogeneity of product mean that market competition is fully effective, and a monopoly position would only manufacture distortion; a profit goal means the public goal does not make majority holding necessary. The direction of reform is therefore shareholding conversion, turning them into legal-person organisations with clear property rights, separation of government and enterprise, diversified ownership subjects, scientific management, limited liability borne by the owner, and bearing of their own profits and losses — and the six requirements for clarifying property rights (defining the subject to whom the property right belongs, requiring the ownership subject to behave as an economic man, making rights and responsibilities explicit, exclusivity of property rights, effective incentive and constraint, and legal protection of property rights) are implemented one by one in the conversion.

## Problems and analysis

### Why must the property-right reform of state-owned enterprises be deepened?

The first reason is the high cost of the administrative-agency model (unfolded above in the arc section): high incentive cost, high informational cost, possibly falling efficiency of constraint, and influence costs that are hard to avoid. All four costs rise with the scale of the state sector and the lengthening of the agency chain, and they share one root — administrative agency substitutes hierarchy for market in transmitting information and incentives, and hierarchy is by nature inefficient at both tasks.

The second reason is the systemic significance of reform. To transition toward the socialist market economy system, subjects of market competition that operate autonomously and bear their own profits and losses must be cultivated: only by deepening state-owned-enterprise reform can the necessary micro-foundation be provided for the transition of the institutional model and the growth mode — if market subjects are not independent, neither price reform nor the competition mechanism can be discussed; only so can the necessary market conditions be created for the switch of institutional model — if enterprises do not buy and sell facing the market and do not bear the consequences of profit and loss, the market system has no real subjects of demand and supply; and only so can the mode of macro control be switched over — when the government is at once owner and regulator, macro control inevitably slides into direct administrative interference with enterprises, and only with government separated from state-asset ownership and from enterprise can macro control turn to indirect instruments as the mainstay.

The third reason goes straight at the two conflicts between state property rights and the market economy (already stated in the arc section): the conflict between the non-personified behaviour of the ownership subject and profit-oriented enterprise operation demands the separation of government and enterprise; the conflict between the non-transferability of the residual claim and the requirement that enterprise property rights be tradable demands that the flow and reorganisation of state shares be solved in the shareholding conversion. The two conflicts decide that the old road of 'reforming nothing in property rights and only adjusting management' (delegation of authority and concession of profit, the contracting system) cannot cure the inefficiency of state-owned enterprises at its root — the contracting system partly yields the residual claim to the operator but solves neither the absence of the owner nor the soft budget constraint, and instead induces short-run behaviour; this is exactly the lesson of state-owned-enterprise reform in the late 1980s. Conclusion: to change the low efficiency of state-owned enterprises as quickly as possible, property-right system reform must be deepened and property rights clarified so that state-owned enterprises become genuine subjects of market competition.

### Why must state-owned enterprises be reformed by category?

The reason for classification is a single one: public-service demands of different natures call for different optimal institutional arrangements. Enterprises supplying public goods adopt the state-owned, state-operated model: the social goal holds absolute dominance in enterprise operation, and the profit goal subordinates itself to the maximisation of social welfare; the degree of monopoly is high; in an industry made up of many enterprises such as banks, information asymmetry exists and consumers find it hard to choose among the varied services and prices. The features of the state-operated model are: the enterprise is created with government-raised funds and generally finances only indirectly, and enterprise efficiency is measured by macro efficiency or the maximisation of social welfare; the government directly appoints or dismisses the enterprise's head and issues term targets, and the enterprise chooses the optimal mode of operation under the constraint of targets; the government controls entry and exit (permitting one or a few firms to enter a monopolistic industry, limiting the waste of resources caused by over-competition); effective regulation of monopolistic enterprises prevents administrative monopoly from harming consumers; income distribution is controlled (the proportion of retained profit, the direction of its use, and the rate of growth of total wages are all controlled by the government); and the enterprise holds no pricing power — prices and the amount of fiscal subsidy are fixed by the government, to prevent an excessively high monopoly price from harming consumers.

Natural-monopoly state-owned enterprises adopt the state-owned, state-controlled model. Basic industries have natural-monopoly characteristics — increasing returns to scale make production by one or a few large firms use resources more effectively than production by many small ones; the operating goal is two-sided — pursuing profit and the preservation and appreciation of state capital while also bearing social obligation; investment is enormous and marked by 'indivisibility'; and the industry needs to develop moderately in advance. Government control must reach four goals — optimising the allocation of resources, raising the enterprises' efficiency of resource use, controlling income redistribution, and stabilising enterprise finances; control appears in five respects — making the ownership subject and the mode of constraint explicit, giving regard both to the profit goal and the social goal, supervising investment activity, supervising price formation, and supervising financial activity. The difference between state control and state operation is that the government is no longer the operator, and in many enterprises is not the sole owner either (other investment subjects are permitted to enter, so that the subjects of possession are diversified), but state capital is in control; ownership and the right to operate are separated to varying degrees, and the government exercises ownership constraint and sets entry-permit rules to sustain economies of scale.

Large and medium competitive enterprises undergo shareholding conversion (the identifying criteria and the six requirements are given in the concepts section) and afterwards become genuine subjects of market competition. Small state-owned enterprises are opened up completely: small firms suitable for opening up are generally small in scale, highly competitive, facing fast-changing market demand, of low industrial importance, traditionally little controlled by the state, and impose greater risk on the state than the revenue they hand in; the forms of opening up are varied — contracting, leasing, auction, transfer of shares, reorganisation, association, merger, and bankruptcy for those long insolvent. State capital withdraws, which not only lets the enterprise break completely free of governmental interference and seek survival and development in market competition, but also lets the government set down the burden of endless 'blood transfusion' and concentrate its resources on the industries it must control; building an exit mechanism for ownership in fact helps hold to public ownership as the mainstay — rewriting 'mainstay' from a numerical advantage into an advantage of quality and control.

### The four-tier authorised-operation model of the shareholding conversion of state-owned enterprises

To clarify property rights under the condition of public ownership as the mainstay, and to make competitive state-owned enterprises subjects of market competition that operate autonomously and bear their own profits and losses, the conversion may proceed by the four-tier authorised-operation model.

First tier of authorised operation: separation of government and enterprise on the basis of the separation of government functions from state-asset ownership. The problem it solves is to construct the institutional conditions of that separation, so that the subject acting for the state in exercising ownership behaves as capital — taking the preservation and appreciation of state capital as its operating goal — and so that an effective incentive-constraint mechanism is built to monitor that subject. The points include: decomposition of government functions, with ownership of assets separated from administrative power and from macro-control power (ownership exercised by a state assets supervision and administration commission as agent, administrative power belonging to administrative departments, and control power to the government's functional departments); the commission standing apart from the governmental system, with the powers and responsibilities of its agency over state property rights made explicit; the National People's Congress monitoring its management of state assets; and appraisal of state assets. The substance of this tier is to answer 'who is to be the owner' — stripping the owner's function out of the sequence of governmental departments and cutting the path by which administrative power interferes with the enterprise through the channel of ownership.

Second tier of authorised operation: dispersal of the equity of state shares. The problem it solves is to establish a set of competitive holding companies, separating the income right of state shares from their control right, so as to create the conditions for the independence of enterprise property rights. A holding company is a financial investment company specialised in operating state assets, its chief duty being to operate the state's shares as the state's agent; the state assets supervision and administration commission should transfer the control right of state shares by competitive tender rather than by administrative transfer; and the holding company, as an independent legal person, may — once it has fulfilled its obligations to the commission — exercise state equity, enjoy equity benefits and bear holding risk like any shareholder, operating on the principle of maximising income with as little risk as possible. The substance of this tier is the 'professionalisation of the owner': letting a specialist body, with capital return as its goal, exercise equity by commercial logic and insulating it from administrative preference.

Third tier of authorised operation: independence of the company's property rights. Once the income right of state shares and their control right are separated and gain transferability, the dispersal of equity becomes possible; the subjects holding shares are diversified; and when equity is dispersed to a certain degree, each shareholder, the cost of control being too high, cannot or will not directly command the operation of the company's assets, so that the company can establish the legal-person asset system — the enterprise with independent legal-person status holds the legal person's property right. The holding company bears limited liability to the amount of capital it invests and enjoys, by law, the shareholder's rights in common (aimed at taking part in the company's business) and rights in its own interest (drawing economic benefit directly from the company). This tier opens up the fundamental proposition of the modern enterprise system that 'the enterprise belongs to the enterprise': the company's assets are independent of any single shareholder, the state included.

Fourth tier of authorised operation: an efficient internal governance structure within the enterprise. The aim is to have the principal set an optimal incentive-constraint mechanism for the agent, to perfect the internal governance structure of the joint-stock company, and to raise the efficiency of asset operation: the shareholders' meeting is the supreme power organ; the board of directors, as the company's legal representative organ, exercises the duty of business decision-making; the supervisory board permanently supervises the company's finances and the board's execution of business; and the general manager is engaged by the board, answers to the board, executes resolutions and accepts supervision and inspection. This tier solves 'how to keep the operator under control once the owner is in place' — it is the direct prelude to the governance-structure innovation of Chapter 5.

## Synthetic essay

### Marx's theory of ownership and its implications for the reform of state-owned enterprises

**(1) Property ownership is the legal expression of ownership relations**. Marx treated the category of ownership in two senses: the category of ownership as an economic relation, embodying the possessing relation of economic subjects to the objective conditions of production, expressed in the activities of production, distribution, exchange and consumption under a given mode of production; and the category of ownership as a legal relation, embodying relations of will and legal right, denoting the possessing subject's right of arbitrary disposal over the object possessed. A given mode of production gives rise to a given mode of possession, and once economic relations of possession arise there is a corresponding legal ownership to match them; monopoly or exclusivity in ownership necessarily appears in law as certainty of property attribution, while at the same time denying to anyone outside that subject exclusive possession of the same property — ownership is the legal form of ownership relations. In actual life the nature and content of ownership relations determine the nature and content of ownership; changes in ownership relations are necessarily reflected in the legal institutions of the superstructure, and changes in the ownership institutions in turn create conditions for changes in ownership relations. Precisely for this reason Marx held that to define bourgeois ownership is nothing other than to describe the whole of the social relations of bourgeois production — ownership was never an isolated legal category, but the legal expression of relations of production.

**(2) The powers of ownership**. By Marx's definition, ownership is the property right that fixes the final attribution of a thing and denotes the subject's exclusive possession and monopoly of the definite thing. It has three features: the owner may exercise ownership only within the limits the law permits; within the limits the law permits the owner may freely exercise each right; and others may not infringe the owner's exercise of rights over his property without permission. Ownership summarises and confers the four powers of possession, use, income and disposition that the owner actually enjoys.

**(3) The separation of the powers of ownership**. As production socialises, the powers separate further, broadly in two kinds: the separation of the legal ownership of capital from its economic ownership — interest and entrepreneurial income are respectively the forms of realisation of legal and of economic ownership, and when the functioning capitalist pays interest due he obtains exclusive possession, use, income and disposition over the borrowed capital; in the joint-stock economy this appears as the division of powers between shareholders and the corporate legal person, or as a principal-agent relation. And the separation of the function of capital from the functions of management and supervision — the joint-stock company separates the power to manage from the ownership of capital, and the capital owner need not manage personally.

Three implications follow for state-owned-enterprise reform. First, ownership relations and ownership (the form of realisation) are two levels — public ownership may have many forms of realisation, and the shareholding form is a form of organising capital rather than an attribute of social system, which supplies the theoretical basis for 'diversified forms of realising public ownership'. Second, the separation of the two rights is inevitable with the socialisation of production; the direction of separating ownership from the right to operate in state-owned-enterprise reform accords with the general law Marx revealed, but the separation simultaneously generates a principal-agent problem which needs a governance structure (Chapter 5) to constrain. Third, ownership institutions react back on ownership relations — property legislation (such as the property law and the state-asset law for enterprises) does not passively record but actively shapes economic relations, and property-right system reform therefore becomes the hinge of the institutional transition.

## Limitations and further work

The testing and correction of this chapter's theoretical frame in the practice of reform can be traced along four lines. First, **the idealising element of the four-tier authorised-operation model**: the commission and the competitive transfer of control rights the model envisages are academic designs, and the actual reform took a different path — the State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission (SASAC) was set up under the State Council in 2003, concentrating 'managing people, managing affairs and managing assets' in one body, which eased the 'nine dragons managing the water' absence of an owner, but also brought a role tension of 'investor-cum-regulator'; only the proposal of the Third Plenary Session of the Eighteenth Central Committee in 2013 to strengthen the supervision of state assets 'with management of capital as the mainstay', and the pilot and spread after 2018 of state capital investment and operation companies (the 'two kinds of companies'), are the realised versions of the second tier's 'holding company' conception. Second, **the institutionalisation of classified reform**: the 2015 *Guiding Opinions of the CPC Central Committee and the State Council on Deepening the Reform of State-Owned Enterprises* formally adopted the commercial/public-welfare classification — the textbook's classified idea entered national policy — but the operationalisation of the boundary between the 'classes' (stripping competitive links out of natural-monopoly industries, as in the reforms of the power grid, the railways and the oil-and-gas pipe networks) has kept advancing, and the 2020–2022 three-year action plan for state-owned-enterprise reform carried corporate restructuring, board building and mixed-ownership reform further in depth. Third, **the later reflection on 'opening up small state-owned enterprises'**: in the mid to late 1990s large-scale restructuring of small and medium state-owned enterprises went forward, with the release of efficiency and the controversies over worker resettlement and loss of state assets side by side, and after 2003 policy turned to regularisation (on-exchange trading, appraisal and audit); 'opening up' was not the end, and the fairness of the exit mechanism decides the legitimacy of the reform. Fourth, **the contemporary extension of property-right theory**: data property rights (the structural partition proposed by the 'Twenty Articles on Data'), securitisation of intellectual property, and the reform of rural collective property rights all show that the agenda of 'clarification of property rights' has spread from industrial enterprises to new factors and new spaces; and the transformation of state capital from 'managing enterprises' to 'managing capital' is essentially the re-application of this chapter's two-rights-separation logic at the level of state-asset supervision — the toolbox of property-right theory is far from exhausted.
