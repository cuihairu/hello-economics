# Chapter 10 · Government regulation under the socialist market economy

> **The central question of this chapter**: markets fail — but does the government necessarily do better? Where does the advantage of government intervention lie, and where does it end? When growth fights price stability, efficiency fights equity, and internal balance fights external balance, how should policy be weighed and combined?

## The arc of this chapter

The first nine chapters argued repeatedly for market efficiency; this chapter handles its symmetric side. The conclusion of the 'invisible hand' (Adam Smith) carries an implicit premise: a fully competitive market structure — the free choice of everyone driven by self-interest forms a natural order, and the market mechanism achieves of itself a balance in economic life. When the market is not complete, market failure is hard to avoid: the allocation the market realizes falls short of Pareto optimality, or the market mechanism cannot achieve certain desirable social and economic goals. In developing countries the causes of failure are multiple — externalities (the private cost of pollution below the social cost), monopoly (price above marginal cost, output restricted), incomplete markets (asymmetric information, missing factor markets), unequal distribution (the market distributes by contribution and ignores the fairness of the starting point), and imperfect institutions (institutional gaps peculiar to transition economies that leave the market mechanism itself incomplete). Since the market is not perfect, regulating its operation where it fails is necessary — this is the necessity argument for intervention. But necessity is not licence: one must also answer 'on what grounds can the government intervene' and 'how far'. The government can correct failure under the right conditions because it obtains a certain authorization through procedure, and that coercive power gives intervention its advantage: the power to tax (tax pollution, subsidize abatement, internalizing external costs); the power to prohibit (administrative or judicial prohibition of certain activities — blocking a monopolist from foreclosing the market, and equally limiting entry to preserve economies of scale); the power to punish (using the legal system to punish breach of contract and infringement of property rights, safeguarding market order); and the transaction-cost saving of centralized correction. The chief advantage of government intervention is coordination — among industrial sectors, among socio-economic relations, between short-term and long-term goals, and between aggregate and structural balance — and these are precisely the problems the market mechanism finds hardest to solve on its own. Conversely, the market mechanism regulates resources through interest relations and is generally more efficient at the micro level. Two sets of strengths and weaknesses stand opposed, so the conclusion is naturally 'combination', but the proportion of the combination is an operational question rather than a philosophical one — which is what the second half of this chapter unfolds.

The first set of operational questions is what the government does. There are nine areas for moderate intervention: providing public services (roads, public health, public schools and public security at home; defence and diplomacy abroad); maintaining market order (legislating to regulate conduct and restrain improper economic activity, with government in the referee's seat); directly engaging in certain economic activities (founding natural-monopoly enterprises the private sector is unable or unwilling to found but the national economy needs, such as basic industries); carrying out institutional innovation (reform is the government's self-revolution, and requires advancing marketization in good time with matching measures); influencing income distribution (narrowing the gap between rich and poor through relative factor-price policy, progressive income and property taxes, transfer payments and state distribution policy); exercising the fiscal regulatory function (adjusting accumulation and consumption, and the central-local ratio, through tax types and rates); influencing resource use (correcting externalities the price mechanism cannot handle); using fiscal, monetary, industrial and balance-of-payments policy to achieve macro objectives; and imposing administrative controls where necessary. Accompanying this is the separation of functions: government functions divide into three — administrative management, regulation, and ownership; when the three are fused, government readily uses its owner's position to intervene directly in operations through a vertically subordinate administrative structure, blurring property relations and separating neither government from enterprise, nor the regulatory objective from a poor environment; in the turn to a market economy system, the transformation of government functions is imperative (Chapter 4's property-right reform and state-asset system reform continue here). Intervention needs an institutional base and transmission mechanisms: diversified means (economic, legal, administrative). Where the micro foundations and market mechanism are comparatively mature, government feeds economic parameters expressing social preference into the market mechanism and influences market signals, and the micro agents respond with sensitivity under the profit motive — indirect regulation thereby replaces direct control, its preconditions including market subjects with sensitive marginal behaviour (enterprises operating independently and covering their own losses, with hardened budget constraints), signals such as prices and interest rates that reflect scarcity, and a complete market system.

The second set of questions is how goals are set. The goals of economic policy form a system with two layers, basic and specific. The basic (final) goals are normative concepts advanced on the basis of value judgements, in three groups: efficiency, growth and stability (efficiency is the primary economic goal; growth, which accompanies structural change, requires stability as its guarantee); economic welfare (efficiency and growth finally serve the people's material and cultural needs, distinguishable into the minimum subsistence requirement and the spiritual and cultural needs beyond it); and distributive justice (primary distribution combines distribution according to labour with distribution according to factors and pursues efficiency, redistribution aims at the guarantee of basic living and pursues equity, and the good choice combines the two principles, balancing equity and efficiency). The specific goals are derivatives of and conditions for the basic ones; China's specific goals include economic growth, price stability (the stability of the general price level rather than of relative prices, with the consumer price index, CPI, the most watched in practice), full employment (the economic state in which every worker willing to work finds a post at a wage they accept), the upgrading of industrial structure (industrial policy guiding structural evolution), and balance-of-payments equilibrium (centred on the balance of foreign-exchange supply and demand, whose material base is the balance of import and export). Goals can complement each other and can conflict; conflicts arise mainly in four areas — economic growth against price stability; full employment against price stability (the trade-off between unemployment and inflation, the Phillips curve, which Phillips found in British data in 1958); equity against efficiency (efficiency demands differentiated pay, equity demands narrowing the gap: emphasizing efficiency means tolerating a degree of inequality, and pursuing equalization sacrifices part of the efficiency — Okun called it 'the big trade-off'); and domestic balance (full employment and price stability) against external balance (balance-of-payments equilibrium). Beyond these lie further conflict zones: price stability against public needs, growth against environmental protection, and resource allocation against balanced regional and sectoral development. The existence of these trade-offs means the policymaker must make value judgements about the goals, weigh priorities, and set goal values and the order of implementation, making the goal system a coordinated organism.

The third set of questions is how instruments are chosen. There are five kinds: fiscal policy (regulating supply and demand through government revenue and spending, including tax, expenditure and subsidy policy, in expansionary and contractionary variants); monetary policy (the central bank regulating money supply and interest rates through the financial system, with the statutory deposit reserve ratio, the rediscount rate and open-market operations as its tools, supplemented by lending policy); administrative controls (intervention by command on the strength of state power — credit, import, foreign-exchange and wage controls and investment licences — compulsory, vertically subordinate and insistent on unity of interest, but neglecting the interests of micro agents and hurting efficiency, so to be confined to what is necessary); economic-legal means (using the legal force to guarantee objectives — universally binding, strictly compulsory, relatively stable); and institutional constraint (the state-asset system, the tax system, the financial system, the social security system, etc. — changing an institution directly affects transmission mechanisms and even goal choice, and is the means of long-horizon policy goals). The transmission mechanism matching the economic means is the market operating mechanism. Seven efficiency principles govern the choice: how strongly the target variable responds to the instrument (choose the strong one); the speed and certainty of the effect (choose the fast and certain one); whether it acts directly on the cause of the disequilibrium (choose the root-curing one); the resource cost, including direct and indirect (choose the thriftly one); selectivity and variability (choose the precisely targetable, readily adjustable one); indirect economic effects (choose those whose positive outweighs the negative); and socio-political effects (choose the one maximizing political benefit and minimizing cost). Finally, composition: the number of policy instrument variables must not be less than the number of policy goal variables — the necessary condition for theoretical attainability of a policy system, the Tinbergen rule (Tinbergen and Frisch shared the first Nobel Prize in Economics, in 1969); where marginal efficiency among instruments is uncertain, policy mixes are built from their comparative advantages. The standard of efficiency for government intervention itself is three-layered: static efficiency (maximum output from given resources and a product mix matching social wishes — the tangency of the production-possibility curve and the social indifference curve); distributive efficiency (a distribution of income that is fair in principle); and dynamic efficiency (avoiding inflation, balanced receipts and payments, an acceptable accumulation-consumption ratio, expansion along a path at the appropriate growth rate). Seven cautions follow: leave micro decisions to the market as far as possible; public goods are government's responsibility; remove monopoly's negative effects and restraints on competition; redistribution intervention is bounded by social cost below social benefit; stability should be an important goal; long-run dynamic efficiency needs moderate government intervention; and the ideal balance between government and the micro agents moves with time and needs tracking adjustment.

## Core concepts

### The 'invisible hand' and market failure

The 'invisible hand' is Adam Smith's economic-liberal policy idea: so long as competition is sufficient, the free choice of everyone driven by self-interest forms a natural order, and the market mechanism, as a system of freedom, has of itself the function of realizing balance in economic life — leading each person to social ends that were never their original intention. Market failure means the allocation realized by market competition falls short of Pareto optimality, or the market mechanism cannot achieve certain desirable social and economic goals; in developing countries the main causes fall into five classes: externality, monopoly, incomplete markets, unequal distribution, imperfect institutions.

Placing the 'invisible hand' beside 'market failure' shows that the latter completes the conditions of applicability of the former rather than refuting it: Smith's proposition is a conditional theorem (the conditions being perfect competition, complete information, no externalities, clear property rights), and the theory of market failure does precisely this — unfolding the conditions one by one; break a condition, and a class of failure appears. This pairing converts the ideological argument over 'whether government is needed' into an engineering problem: identify the type of failure, assess whether the particular strength of government intervention exactly fits it, and prevent the intervention from creating new distortions. There is a correspondence between the strengths of government (taxing, prohibiting, punishing, saving transaction costs) and the types of failure — externality answers to the power to tax, monopoly to the power to prohibit, opportunism to the power to punish — and 'matching the remedy to the illness' is the strongest defence of intervention's legitimacy and the best definition of its limits.

### Price stability and full employment

Price stability means the stability of the general price level across society as a whole — not the stability of relative prices among goods and factors; the change in the general price level is normally shown through a price index, and in practice governments watch the consumer price index (CPI) most closely. Full employment is the economic state in which every worker willing to work finds a post at a wage they accept (frictional and structural unemployment excepted); the state should foster the healthy development of the labour market and give policy guidance, so that labour flows rationally and the employment rate rises.

Why insist that 'price stability' mean the general level rather than relative prices? Because changes in relative prices are exactly the signal by which the market allocates resources: vegetable prices up and meat prices down is a shift in scarcity and needs no intervention; the only legitimate object of intervention is the general level (inflation and deflation), which distorts the real value of every contract and destroys money's accounting function. Likewise, 'full employment' must be qualified by 'at a wage they accept': zero unemployment is neither possible nor desirable (the instantaneous Walrasian clearing never existed), and the policy target is to eliminate the involuntary unemployment caused by insufficient demand. The precision of both definitions tells the policymaker: setting the target is itself part of policy design — get the target wrong (suppressing relative prices, say, or chasing zero unemployment) and even the best instruments will run backwards.

### Instruments of economic policy

The instruments of economic policy are the means the state adopts to achieve the objectives of economic policy, covering both policy tools and the methods of implementing them, and specifically including fiscal policy instruments (taxation, expenditure, subsidies), monetary instruments (the reserve ratio, the rediscount rate, open-market operations and lending policy), administrative controls (credit, import, foreign-exchange and wage controls, and investment licensing), economic-legal means, and institutional constraint — five kinds in all.

Why prepare a 'menu of instruments' rather than a single optimal tool? Because goals can conflict (a single fiscal expansion cannot both depress inflation and raise employment), because instruments have comparative advantages (in indirect effect, speed of operation and precision), and because the transmission mechanism depends on market conditions — the same tool has different force on markets at different stages of development. Policy design thus changes from 'find the optimal tool' to 'assemble a combination meeting the Tinbergen condition': having at least as many instrument variables as goal variables is only the necessary condition, the sufficient one coming from the complementarity of comparative advantages — fiscal is good at structural adjustment, monetary at aggregate management, legal at rule stability, controls only for emergencies, institutional constraint at the long term — the five are not competitors but a division of labour.

## Problems and analysis

### Where do the necessity and the advantage of government intervention come from?

The necessity comes from the market side: the conclusion of the 'invisible hand' presumes perfect competition, and where the market is incomplete failure is unavoidable — externalities make private decisions ignore social costs, monopoly makes price diverge from marginal cost, incomplete markets make transactions fail or information distorted, unequal distribution puts the fairness of market outcomes in doubt, and imperfect institutions leave the market mechanism itself incomplete (especially so in a transition economy). All five point to a deviation from Pareto optimality or a missed desirable social goal — hence regulation is necessary. The possibility comes from the government side: government obtains coercive authorization through procedure; the power to tax can internalize external effects (taxing pollution, subsidizing abatement), the power to prohibit can maintain or restrict entry to protect competition or realize economies of scale, the power to punish can use the legal system to punish breach and infringement, providing credible commitment for exchange, and the centralized correction of failure can itself save the transaction costs of dispersed coordination. Necessity plus possibility is still not sufficient — the effectiveness of intervention also depends on the government's own constraints and capacity: the main advantage of government lies in coordination (among sectors, among social relations, between long and short goals, between aggregate and structure), but government too can fail (insufficient information, goal drift, rent-seeking), which is why the nine areas of moderate intervention and the separation of the three functions (administration, regulation, ownership) are exactly the guardrails that bound 'necessary intervention': with the three fused, intervening directly in enterprises through the administrative hierarchy in the capacity of owner blurs property rights and sinks the regulatory objective, so functional separation is the institutional precondition of effective intervention.

### Why do the goals of economic policy conflict? Where does the conflict concentrate?

The root of conflict lies in the inner constraints of the economic system: resources are scarce in aggregate and at every point in time, and different goals typically draw on the same resources and instruments. There are four main conflict zones. First, growth against price stability: growth rides on investment, and raising the expected profit rate of investors needs low interest rates (more money) or falling real wages (rising prices); controlling prices requires tightening credit, which lowers expected profits and investment demand and sends the economy sliding — under full employment the two exclude each other. Second, full employment against price stability: full employment often needs deficit finance and more money, which stimulates aggregate demand and lifts prices; curbing inflation needs monetary contraction, which raises unemployment — the trade-off between the unemployment rate and the inflation rate is the Phillips curve. Third, equity against efficiency: efficiency demands differentiated pay for differentiated contribution, equity demands narrowing the gap; emphasizing efficiency means tolerating a degree of inequality, and equalizing incomes sacrifices efficiency by weakening incentives — at any one moment the two cannot both be had, and policy can only weigh them in combination. Fourth, domestic against external balance: achieving full employment pushes up wages and domestic income and raises imports, to the detriment of the balance of payments; pursuing balance of payments may then be to the detriment of full employment and price stability — this is the central problem of policy mix under openness (see the financial-opening part of Chapter 9). Beyond these lie further zones: price stability against public needs, growth against environmental protection, resource allocation against regional balance. The existence of trade-offs means the substance of policy is value judgement: fixing the values of the goals and their order of implementation, and coordinating among them — acknowledging conflict is not defeatism, but the way 'we want it all' is turned into an explicit weighing plus a compensating mechanism (for instance compensating the losers of an efficiency reform through redistribution).

## Synthetic essay

### The necessity of government economic regulation under the socialist market economy

At the first layer, the market mechanism is not perfect. The invisible hand presumes perfect competition, and the five causes of failure (externality, monopoly, incomplete markets, unequal distribution, imperfect institutions) bite harder in developing and transition economies — the last of which is a distinctly Chinese addition: the market mechanism's own incompleteness is itself a source of failure. Where markets fail, regulation is necessary. At the second layer, government has a comparative advantage in mending failure. The power to tax, prohibit and punish conferred by coercive authorization, plus the transaction-cost saving of centralized correction, are things private organizations and market self-healing do not have; the strength of intervention lies in coordination — industrial coordination, the coordination of socio-economic relations, of long and short goals, of aggregate and structure — exactly the part the market mechanism finds hardest to solve spontaneously. At the third layer, combination rather than replacement. Market regulation and government intervention each carry positive and negative effects and cannot be absolutized: the market is efficient at the micro level but blind to equity and the long run; government is good at coordination but can itself fail. The concrete form of combining them well is: the nine areas of moderate intervention set 'what to do', the separation of functions and the transmission mechanism settle 'how to do it', the goal system and the Tinbergen condition bound 'how much', and the three efficiency criteria check 'how well it is done' — the four layers together constitute the complete theoretical framework of government regulation under the socialist market economy.

### Instruments and their combination in government economic intervention

Five instruments, each with its own character. Fiscal policy regulates supply and demand through government revenue and spending — tax, expenditure and subsidy policy, expansionary or contractionary; strong at structural adjustment and redistribution, but bound by legislative procedure and the electoral cycle. Monetary policy is run by the central bank through the money supply and interest rates — the three traditional tools of reserve ratio, rediscount rate and open-market operations, supplemented by policy lending (priority lending to pillar industries, long-term preferential terms for vital public-interest industries); strong at aggregate management and highly flexible, but at a loss with structural problems. Administrative controls intervene directly on the strength of state power — compulsory, vertically subordinate, insistent on unity of interest; fastest in effect but neglecting micro agents and damaging efficiency, so confined to what is necessary. Economic-legal means guarantee objectives through the force of law — universally binding, strictly compulsory, relatively stable, the supplier of rules. Institutional constraint changes the transmission mechanism itself by changing state-asset management, the tax system, the financial system and the social security system — the means of long-horizon goals. Choice follows seven efficiency principles: response strength, speed of effect, direct action on the cause, resource cost (direct and indirect), selectivity and variability, the net sign of indirect economic effects, and socio-political effects. Combination follows the Tinbergen condition: having no fewer instrument variables than goal variables is the necessary condition for theoretical attainability, and where marginal efficiency among instruments is uncertain the mix is built from comparative advantages (fiscal for structure, monetary for totals, legal for rules, controls for emergencies, institutions for the long run). The condition list for transmission mechanisms (market subjects with hardened budget constraints, signals reflecting scarcity, a complete market system) reminds us: instrument effectiveness is endogenous to marketization — indirect regulation presupposes a market that is developed enough, which is exactly why 'transforming the mode of regulation' and 'building the market system' must advance together in a transition economy.

### The efficiency standards of government intervention and the cautions for policy choice

The efficiency standards are three-layered and broad. Static efficiency: maximum output given labour, capital and other resources, with a product mix that meets both technological possibility and the wishes of society as a whole (the tangency of the production-possibility and indifference curves). Distributive efficiency: a distribution of income that is fair in principle. Dynamic efficiency: avoiding inflation, achieving balance of receipts and payments, an accumulation-consumption ratio society can accept, expansion along a path at the appropriate growth rate; in the short run this shows as aggregate balance and the tapping of productive potential. By these standards, policy choice should observe seven things. First, a market mechanism with complete functions realizes static efficiency more easily; the production, operation and consumption decisions of micro agents are best left to themselves, and ill-judged government intervention damages static efficiency instead. Second, providing public goods is government's responsibility; doing so serves both efficiency and equity. Third, government should remove monopoly's negative effects and lift restraints on competitive market structure, avoiding the bias of market allocation. Fourth, spontaneous market adjustment may produce unfair distribution; where the social cost of narrowing the gap is below the social benefit, redistribution intervention is effective — the efficiency-equity trade-off becomes a calculable cost-benefit comparison. Fifth, laissez-faire comes with fluctuation and waste, and stability should be an important policy goal. Sixth, the market mechanism has difficulty reaching long-run dynamic efficiency on its own, or reaches it only at great cost, so moderate intervention helps. Seventh, the ideal balance between government and the micro agents moves with time and needs tracking adjustment — creating the right institutional environment for policy is itself policy. The spirit of the seven together: every link of government intervention takes the market as its frame of reference — what the market is good at is handed back to the market, where it is weak the government fills in, the performance of the intervention is accepted or rejected by the three efficiency tests, and the boundary of the intervention is tested and adjusted by cost and benefit.

## Limitations and further work

The framework of this chapter leaves seven coordinates. First, **the symmetric lesson of government failure**: the textbook is fuller on market failure than on government failure — public-choice theory (Buchanan) holds that the political process also drifts from the public interest: Niskanen's budget-maximizing tendency, the social cost of rent-seeking (Tullock, Krueger), the short electoral horizons of decision-makers; China's long transformation of government functions (approval reform, the negative list, streamline-delegate-regulate) is exactly the institutional answer to government failure, and the official phrasing of an 'effective market and a proactive government' (after 2013) rests on the premise that both failures must be governed. Second, **the evolution of the macro-control system**: the continuation and transformation of the five-year plans (directive plans → indicative outlines); the naming history of 'proactive fiscal policy and prudent monetary policy' after 1998; the 2015 supply-side structural reform (cut overcapacity, destocking, deleveraging, lowering costs, strengthening weak links), which widened control from the demand side to the supply side; the monetary toolbox extending from the three traditional tools to normalized open-market operations (reverse repos, MLF, PSL, structural monetary tools); fiscal tools adding special bonds and normalized transfer payments — the textbook's fivefold classification still holds, but the inner structure of each instrument is entirely new. Third, **the Chinese deformation of goal conflict**: the Phillips curve has long been weak in China (Okun's law failing, the inflation-unemployment relation attenuated, with CPI inflation at times below 1% in the low-inflation years, per the latest releases), and policy conflict shows up more as stabilizing growth against adjusting structure, securing employment against cutting capacity, dual energy controls against growth targets, and platform regulation against innovation incentives — the conflict zones move from the textbook's four to a multi-dimensional dynamic combination, and 'consistency of macro policy orientation' (raised at the 2023 Central Economic Work Conference) is the latest institutionalized response to managing goal conflict. Fourth, **from administrative control to rule-of-law regulation**: the antimonopoly law revision (2022) and platform-economy regulation, the financial-regulation reform (the National Financial Regulatory Administration, 2023), the cleanup of rules impeding fair competition under the unified national market (2022) — administrative control is progressively displaced by 'rules plus regulation', and the textbook's injunction to confine administrative control to what is necessary is cashed out in rule-of-law form. Fifth, **expectation management and policy communication**: the contemporary form of 'tracking adjustment' is expectation management — central-bank communication, policy-interpretation mechanisms and forward guidance joined the toolbox, and the object of control extended from the current variable to the formation of expectations. Sixth, **the debate on the multi-goal regime**: the People's Bank carries multiple goals — price stability, employment, growth, balance of payments (Zhou Xiaochuan's 2016 systematic account) — in contrast to the international mainstream of inflation targeting; whether multiple goals damage policy credibility, and how the Tinbergen condition is met in China, remain frontier questions in macroeconomics. Seventh, **the expansion of the efficiency standards**: distributive efficiency is being given new operational content (the three distributions under common prosperity, the metricization of equalization of basic public services), and dynamic efficiency is being coupled with the green transition (the growth path under carbon-neutrality constraints) — the extension of the three-layer standards is still growing, and the framework of this chapter stays open.
