# Socialist economics

This set of topics is not about memorizing a string of concepts, but about answering one overarching question:

**Under a socialist system, how should resources be allocated, firms run, distribution organized, and the government regulate, so as to hold the socialist direction while still driving modernization forward?**

Compressed into a chain of questions, the whole course roughly falls into four layers.

## II. How the answers to these questions were worked out step by step

Looking at the conclusions alone, this set of topics feels scattered. In fact, it follows a fairly clear chain of cognitive advance:

1. From utopian socialism to scientific socialism, resolving "does socialism have a theoretical basis".
2. From the traditional planned system to reform and opening up, resolving "why socialism had to change how it allocates resources".
3. To the establishment of the socialist market economy system, resolving "can markets enter a socialist system".
4. To SOE reform, distribution reform, and the transformation of government, resolving "how the system actually operates after marketization".
5. Finally to high-quality development, structural upgrading, opening up, and governance, resolving "how socialist modernization keeps advancing".

## III. Institutional change along the nodes of the Party's congresses

In socialist economics, many key conclusions were not abstracted out by the textbook authors themselves, but formed step by step along with the Party's line, policies, and major congresses.

The most crucial thread is:

1. The Third Plenary Session of the Eleventh Central Committee opened reform and opening up.
2. The Twelfth National Congress proposed "planned economy as the mainstay, market regulation as the supplement".
3. The Third Plenary Session of the Twelfth Central Committee proposed "a planned commodity economy on the basis of public ownership".
4. The talks in the South broke the old framework of "markets equal capitalism".
5. The Fourteenth National Congress formally proposed building a socialist market economy system.
6. The Third Plenary Session of the Fourteenth Central Committee unfolded that goal into an institutional framework.
7. The Fifteenth and Sixteenth National Congresses and the Third Plenary Session of the Eighteenth Central Committee continued rebuilding the relations among markets, government, firms, and distribution.
8. The Nineteenth and Twentieth National Congresses pushed the questions to high-quality development and a modernized economic system.

## IV. The birth and testing of the theory

What is distinctive about this set of topics is that its main propositions were tested by the largest-scale institutional experiment of the twentieth century.

- **The plan-versus-market debate:** Mises (1920) and Hayek (1945) held that without a price mechanism resources cannot be allocated sensibly; Lange (1936) answered that planning was feasible by "simulating the market through trial and error". Half a century later, the shortages of Soviet-style planning and the pains of the East European transitions were widely read as a vindication of Hayek's side (the information and incentive problems).
- **The household contract responsibility system (1978–1984):** collective labour was turned into "pay the state what it is owed, keep the rest for yourselves", and agricultural output surged within a few years — one of the cleanest country-level tests of incentive theory, and the released surplus labour unexpectedly launched the township and village enterprises.
- **Gradualism versus radicalism as a controlled experiment:** China's dual-track system, special-zone pilots, and the incremental-before-stock gradual path stood as a contemporary contrast to the "shock therapy" of the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe. The stark growth contrast of the 1990s pushed economics to rethink the cost of institutional change (the North paradox and demand-induced institutional change both entered the textbooks from here).
- **Merging of the dual-track prices:** planned and market prices for the same good existed side by side (from 1985), protecting the existing stock while letting marginal increments be priced by the market; corruption and arbitrage pressure eventually pushed the tracks together after 1992 — the full life cycle of a transitional institutional arrangement.

## VII. The question this set of topics keeps returning to

The core of what socialist economics discusses is not whether to have markets, but **how a socialist system makes use of markets, constrains them, steers them, and unifies growth, equity, opening up, and governance.**
