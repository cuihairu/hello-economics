# Introduction

> **The central question of this chapter**: why has a theory whose object is 'the socialist economy' been remade from the ground up over the last forty-odd years? Marxist economics and Western economics study the same economic world — where exactly does their disagreement lie, and can they be methodologically complementary?

## The arc of this chapter

Socialist economic theory is not an idea that evolved in the study alone; each of its major advances stepped on a node of institutional change. Since the reform and opening up, this theory has made major progress, and the fundamental reason lies in the change of the times, which can be compressed into three forces. The first is **marketization**: under a highly centralized planned system, resource allocation followed hierarchy rules — resources followed the administrative rank; the market-oriented reform since the Third Plenary Session of the Eleventh Central Committee gradually yielded the hierarchy rule to the property-right rule — resources now follow the asset. The second is **industrialization**: accompanying marketization, China pushed on a large scale the evolution from a traditional agricultural society to a modern industrial society, accelerating it by importing foreign capital, technology and management experience. The third is **internationalization**: from closed and semi-closed isolation to the basic state policy of opening to the outside world, and especially after joining the World Trade Organization in 2001, the pace of participating in economic globalization clearly quickened.

These three forces pushed the theoretical focus into staged shifts. In the early 1980s the discussion ran on the theme of decentralization and profit-sharing — reform was just beginning, and the problems concentrated on how to give enterprises and localities more autonomy; in the early 1990s the discussion ran on the theme of the plan-market relation — reform had gone deep and had to answer the fundamental question of whether socialism can use the market; since then, the discussion has run on the theme of property-right reform — once the market system was established, who owns, who decides and who bears the risk became the unavoidable core. The form of the theory accordingly showed four turns: from attending only to relations of production to attending equally to resource allocation; from a closed one-flower-alone bloom to an open hundred-flowers-contend; from pure theoretical deduction to a strong emphasis on connecting theory with practice; from a monotonous analytic method to a pluralism combining normative with empirical, qualitative with quantitative, and static with dynamic analysis.

The second set of questions is methodological. Marxist economics and Western economics give different explanations of the same economic phenomena; the disagreement is not in the material but in the starting point. On **premises**, Marxist economics starts from materialist dialectics and historical materialism, while Western economics starts from the scarcity of resources and the self-interested nature of man; on the **object of study**, the former examines social relations of production under a given mode of production rather than relations between things, while the latter studies how society allocates scarce resources; on the **logical starting point**, the former is the social man — behaviour embedded in specific social relations — and the latter is the economic man — the abstract individual maximizing utility under constraints; on the **analytic method**, the former builds holism, and the latter follows the individualist cost-benefit analysis. Once this set of differences is acknowledged, three tendencies appear in the methodological history of socialist economic theory: rejecting Western methods and building a system on traditional lines; wholesale adopting Western methods to rebuild the system; or splicing the two as blocks — you have your chapters, I have mine. Each of these tendencies is one-sided. The feasible path of innovation is to combine the reasonable aspects of the holist and individualist methods and reject their extreme forms — seeing both the constraints of structure and institutions, and the individual's choice and reaction under those constraints.

The third set is the research attitude the theory demands of itself: facing reality squarely. Its points can be reduced to six: hold to the attitude and method of facing reality squarely — when theory and practice conflict, let theory serve reality, not reality flatter theory; make connecting theory with practice the basic principle of economic innovation fitting the requirements of the times — rejecting both empty formalistic 'eight-legged essays' divorced from reality and an empiricism that wholly abandons theory; oppose dogmatism and book-worship — treating either Marxist or Western economics as doctrine beyond testing will drive Chinese economics into a dead end; achieve the modernization of economics — modernization inherently carries the dual meaning of internationalization and localization, absorbing the theories and advanced analytic methods of Western economics that reflect the general laws of a market economy while taking China's national conditions as the footing; keep an open posture — academic sources of ideas, academic teams and academic views should all be open; and break out of the old track of traditional political economy that prizes the normative over the empirical, the qualitative over the quantitative, deduction over induction, and conclusions over proof — emphasizing empirical analysis, econometrics and the testing of economic claims. These six are the methodological promise of the introduction, and also the standard by which the expository style of the chapters that follow is to be judged.

## Core concepts

### Marketization, industrialization, internationalization

Marketization is the process by which the way resources are allocated shifts from administrative hierarchy rules to market property-right rules; industrialization is the process by which an economy evolves from a traditional agricultural society to a modern industrial society; internationalization is the process by which a national economy joins the world economy through foreign trade, capital flows and the alignment of rules. The three stand side by side as the forces of the times behind the major progress of socialist economic theory since the reform and opening up.

Why explain the change of theory with these three forces? Because the agenda of an economic theory is set by the list of real problems: marketization raises the question of 'the relation of plan and market', industrialization raises 'the mode of growth and structural change', and internationalization raises 'opening and economic security' — almost every topic of socialist economic theory over forty years can find its source in reality among these three forces. Reducing theoretical change to the change of the times is also the first lesson of the 'face reality squarely' method: a theory's explanatory power must be tested by its ability to answer real questions.

### The hierarchy rule and the property-right rule

The hierarchy rule builds first a layered pyramid of subordination, then fixes each agent's position in that structure, and thereby fixes the power to allocate resources appropriate to that hierarchical position — the higher the position, the more resources one can command. The property-right rule holds that the power a person has to allocate resources is positively related to the quantity of assets they hold — the more assets, the more resources at one's disposal.

This pair of concepts strings together the institutional change of the later chapters: abstracted, the transition from the planned to the market system is the change of the right to allocate resources from 'distributed by administrative rank' to 'distributed by property right'. The institutional-change analysis of Chapter 3 (supply-driven, diffusion-mediating, demand-induced) and the state-owned-enterprise property-right reform of Chapter 4 are both concrete forms of these two rules rising and falling against each other.

### Holist and individualist analysis

Holist analysis treats society as an organic whole and explains individual behaviour and economic phenomena from the mode of production, class structure and social relations; individualist cost-benefit analysis explains economic phenomena from the optimal choice of the individual under constraints.

Their disagreement is not 'who is right and who is wrong', but 'what is taken as the starting point of explanation': holism takes individual behaviour as an object shaped by social structure, while individualism takes social outcomes as the sum of individual choices. Each has an explanatory blind spot — the first easily overlooks micro-mechanisms and takes institutional outcomes as a given premise needing no explanation; the second easily overlooks institutions and power structures and takes historical specificity as universal human nature. This is the reason socialist economic theory argues for a 'middle path': use holism to grasp institutions and structures, use individualism to depict incentives and choice, with each serving as a constraint on the other.

## Problems and analysis

### Where exactly do Marxist economics and Western economics diverge?

Putting the two systems side by side on the same table, the divergence concentrates at four levels. Premises: Marxist economics takes materialist dialectics and historical materialism as its premise and regards economic categories as the theoretical expression of historical social relations; Western economics takes the scarcity of resources and the self-interested nature of man as its premise and defines the economic problem as the allocation of scarce resources. Object: the former studies social relations of production under a given mode of production — who owns the means of production, who commands labour, how the surplus is distributed; the latter studies how society allocates scarce resources — how prices form, how equilibrium is reached. Logical starting point: the analytical unit of the former is the 'social man', whose motives and capacities are defined by social relations; the analytical unit of the latter is the 'economic man', whose self-interest and rationality are set as supra-historical assumptions. Method: the former is holist, moving from the whole to the individual; the latter is individualist, aggregating from the individual to the whole.

The divergences at the four levels are interlocking: the premise determines the object, the object the starting point, the starting point the method. Therefore the difference in conclusions between the two economics — for instance their judgements on exploitation, on market failure and on the role of the government — cannot simply be settled by 'putting the data on trial', because in many cases they are not even asking the same question. But divergence does not mean mutual exclusion: the insight of Marxist economics into institutions and distribution structures, and the formal instruments of Western economics for incentive and allocation mechanisms, are exactly complementary. The methodological innovation of socialist economic theory lies in refusing both extremes on the basis of acknowledging the difference, and exploring the road that combines holism and individualism.

### What exactly does 'facing reality squarely' require as a method?

The first requirement of facing reality squarely is attitudinal: when theory and practice conflict, let theory serve reality. If the existing theory is not enough to answer the questions implied in reality, one should reflect on the theory itself until the theory is revised in light of reality — rather than trimming reality to fit the theory. The second is principled: connecting theory with practice is the basic principle of economic innovation fitting the requirements of the times, rejecting both talk divorced from reality ('eight-legged-essay' economics) and the empiricism that abandons theory (a description without theory cannot be generalized or accumulated). The third is critical: connecting theory with practice must first oppose dogmatism and book-worship — treating any economics (whether Marxist or Western) as doctrine beyond testing will drive Chinese economics into a dead end.

The last three turn constructive. Fourth, facing reality squarely requires the modernization of economics, and modernization carries the dual meaning of internationalization and localization: absorb the theories and instruments of Western economics that reflect the general laws of how a market economy operates, while using them to answer China's specific questions and revising and developing the theory in the answering. Fifth, keep an open posture — open sources of academic ideas, an open academic team, open academic views; a single-source supply of theory cannot cope with a complex reality. Sixth, break out of the old track of traditional political economy that prizes the normative over the empirical, the qualitative over the quantitative, deduction over induction, and conclusions over proof: emphasize empirical methods, emphasize econometric methods, emphasize the testing of economic claims. Taken together, the six points can be summed up in one sentence — the vitality of economics lies in its power to explain and predict reality, and that power must be maintained by a continuous renewal of method.

## Synthetic essay

### The basic arc of socialist economic theory since the reform and opening up

**(1) The driving force of the progress: the triple force of the change of times**

The major progress of socialist economic theory in China over the last decades rests fundamentally on the change of the times. Marketization: the long-standing highly centralized planned system allocated resources by hierarchy rules, and the market-oriented reform since the Third Plenary Session of the Eleventh Central Committee gradually yielded the hierarchy rule to the property-right rule — this is the direct background for the theoretical focus expanding from 'relations of production' to 'resource allocation'. Industrialization: accompanying marketization, China pushed on a large scale the evolution from a traditional agricultural society to a modern industrial society, and large-scale import of foreign capital, technology and management experience accelerated the process — the domain of development economics (growth, structure, technology) thereby entered socialist economic theory. Internationalization: from self-seclusion to the establishment of the basic line of 'one centre, two basic points' and the fixing of opening to the outside world as a basic state policy, and especially the marked acceleration of participation in economic globalization after joining the WTO — institutional comparison and development strategy under openness became new theoretical topics.

**(2) The three stages of the mainstream development of the theory**

Along with marketization, industrialization and internationalization, the mainstream development of socialist economic theory showed a staged character. The first stage was the early-1980s discussion on the theme of decentralization and profit-sharing: reform began with the household contract responsibility system in the countryside and with expanding enterprise autonomy in the cities, and the theoretical focus was the boundary of administrative decentralization. The second stage was the early-1990s discussion on the theme of the relation of plan and market: whether reform can use the market and how market and plan combine; this discussion concluded with the establishment in 1992 of the goal of a socialist market economy system. The third stage was the discussion on the theme of property-right reform: once the framework of the market system was set, the property-right reform of state-owned enterprises, the modern enterprise system and the state asset management system became the main battleground of theory. The advance through the three stages is not a change of topic but a continuous deepening of one reform logic: decentralization solves incentives, pricing solves mechanisms, property rights solve subjects.

**(3) Four features of the theoretical achievements**

From attending only to relations of production to attending equally to resource allocation: the object of political economy is no longer confined to relations of production but simultaneously concerns how a society allocates scarce resources — what to produce, how much to produce, how to produce it. From a closed one-flower-alone bloom to an open hundred-flowers-contend: while treating Marxist economics with a developmental eye and keeping its capacity to keep pace with the times, introducing several schools of Western economics to improve the analytic paradigm. From pure theoretical deduction to a strong emphasis on connecting theory with practice: under the guidance of socialism with Chinese characteristics, facing squarely the major problems of economic reform and development. From a monotonous analytic method to a pluralism of methods: combining normative with empirical analysis, qualitative with quantitative analysis, and static with dynamic analysis. The four features point together to one judgement: this theory has shifted from 'a learning that argues for settled conclusions' to 'a learning that explains and guides transition'.

## Limitations and further work

The introduction offers framework-level judgements, and its boundaries should be stated. First, the three-stage division (decentralization — plan and market — property-right reform) summarizes the main line of reform theory in the twentieth century; after the twenty-first century the theoretical focus kept moving — from 'establishing the market system' to 'improving the market system' (after 2003), then to 'high-quality development and common prosperity' (after 2017), and the staged account needs to be extended backward. Second, the methodological 'middle path' is easy to say and hard to do: how holism and individualism combine inside one model is still without a standard answer in economics — transition economics, comparative institutional analysis and evolutionary economics are among the attempts, and the debate in the Chinese economics community over 'where Chinese economics is going' is still going, showing that methodological self-awareness remains an unfinished undertaking. Third, the methodological promise of 'facing reality squarely' has points where it falls short in practice: the diffusion of empirical and econometric methods is rising, but the forward-looking explanatory power of theory for major real problems (such as income distribution, real estate, and platform-economy regulation) is still limited by data availability and research independence. Fourth, the framework of comparison is itself evolving: the mathematical formalization of contemporary Marxist political economy (such as Marxist growth models inside a dynamic general-equilibrium frame) and the heterodox schools inside Western economics (post-Keynesianism, institutionalism) both exceed the old picture of 'two systems confronting each other', so the four-dimensional comparison of the introduction should be read as an analytic starting point rather than a closed conclusion.
