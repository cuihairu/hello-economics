# Chapter 6 · The distribution system under the socialist market economy

> **The central question of this chapter**: why must the distribution according to labour that Marx envisaged change its form of realisation in practice? Is the coexistence of multiple modes of distribution, with factors such as capital and technology taking part in distribution, inconsistent with the socialist goal of equality? What is the division of labour between income distribution at the micro level and governmental regulation at the macro level?

## The arc of this chapter

The theoretical origin of this chapter is still Marx's prediction of the principles of distribution for future society. In the *Critique of the Gotha Programme* Marx divided communist society into a lower stage and a higher stage: the higher stage practices distribution according to need; in the lower stage, because productive forces have not yet fully abounded, the old social division of labour still exists, and labour is still only a means of livelihood, personal consumer goods can be distributed only with labour as the measure — the worker supplies labour to society and society distributes personal consumer goods to the worker, with equal pay for equal work and more for more work. Marx had a deep and sober positioning of distribution according to labour: it measures different individuals by the same measure, taking the inequality of natural talent and working capacity as a 'natural privilege', and so the equality of its rights is, by its own principle, still 'a bourgeois right' — it abolishes the exploitation whereby, on the strength of possession of the means of production, the labour of others is appropriated without compensation, yet it retains the factual inequality arising from differences of talent and contribution. This is not a defect of distribution according to labour, but the price of its being the only legitimate principle of the lower stage.

The problem is that real socialism did not fall on the premises Marx had in mind: commodity-money relations did not die, and the level of productive forces is far short of that required for single public ownership and direct distribution by society. So the form of realisation of distribution according to labour suffered a triple deformation. First, distribution is not made according to the direct social labour time the worker supplies, but is realised indirectly through commodity-money relations — labour must first appear as value on the market before it can enter distribution; second, distribution cannot be executed by a single social centre across the whole society on a uniform standard, but must pass through the enterprise as an intermediate link — what the worker faces is not 'society' but his own enterprise, and the operating condition of the enterprise is superimposed on individual remuneration; and third, the vehicle of realisation is the wage and not the labour voucher — the direct accounting of 'society—individual' labour time becomes an indirect transmission of 'enterprise—market—individual'. These three deformations mean that distribution according to labour under market conditions is no longer the same order of thing as the distribution according to labour of the textbook as an abstract principle, and most of the analysis of this chapter is a treatment of that gap.

The diversity of the modes of distribution is decided by the distribution of the conditions of production. Marx's method is that 'any distribution of the means of consumption is nothing but the result of the distribution of the conditions of production themselves': to whom the objective conditions of production (the material means) belong decides by what standard income is distributed. The structure of the primary stage — public ownership as the mainstay, with multiple forms of ownership developing together — decides that the standard of labour and the standard of ownership coexist: the public economy practices distribution according to labour across the board, in the non-public economy the ownership of factors such as capital and land directly commands distribution, and in a mixed-ownership enterprise both standards are present at once; the subjective conditions of production (labour power) belong to the individual worker, and the form in which the worker combines his labour power with the means of production — entering a public enterprise, being employed by the private economy, or self-employment — directly decides the nature of his income. And all of this is accomplished through the market: the various conditions of production are allocated by the market, so the determination of factor prices is linked with the distribution of income; letting the owner of a factor take part in distribution at the factor price is to make the cost and the return of market allocation symmetrical — and this is the efficiency reason for 'distribution according to factors of production': capital must accumulate, technology must innovate, management requires talent, and if these factors do not receive an income return in the form of a property right they will not be supplied in full. The Fifteenth Congress established 'distribution according to labour as the mainstay, with multiple modes of distribution coexisting'; the Sixteenth Congress made explicit the combining of distribution according to labour with distribution according to factors of production; and successive congresses thereafter have kept expanding the list of factors taking part in distribution (knowledge, technology, management, and at last data).

The second half of the distribution question is fairness. Market distribution at the micro level naturally opens out gaps by contribution and scarcity; it manages incentive, not a safety net; so the process of income distribution was separated relatively independently into two layers: micro income distribution (led by the market mechanism, weighting efficiency) and macro income regulation (led by the government, weighting fairness) — taxation transfers part of high income into the hands of the state, budget expenditure and transfer payments and social security provide protection for low income, and the equalisation of public services pushes fairness from 'money' to 'opportunity'. The latter half of this chapter unfolds this regulatory chain from market to government, and traces fairness back to a deeper level: the root of the unfairness of Chinese income distribution lies mainly in the unfairness of the distribution of the conditions of production — agents controlling public conditions of production and public surplus, the non-uniformity of national treatment (the urban-rural identity barrier), market segmentation obstructing the equilibrated flow of factors — and so fairness cannot be entrusted entirely to redistribution; the micro foundation of equitable income distribution must be built, and the gap solved at its root rather than only at its end; this is also the theoretical landing point of the proposition of common prosperity.

## Core concepts

### Distribution according to labour

Distribution according to labour means that, after the necessary deductions have been made from social gross product (the compensation fund, the accumulation fund, the public consumption fund, and so on), personal consumer goods are distributed according to the effective quantity of labour the worker supplies to society, with more for those who work more, less for those who work less, and nothing for those who do not work. It is the basic principle of the distribution of personal consumer goods under socialist public ownership, and one of the most basic economic features of socialism. Its premises are that the means of production are owned by society, that people are equal in their possession of the means of production, that no one can, on the strength of possession of the means of production, command the labour of others, and that labour is therefore the sole ground for obtaining social product.

Why insist on both its legitimacy and its historical limitation? Because these two aspects decide the direction of policy: insisting on its legitimacy allows every system of exploitation to be denied — income must come from labour contribution, and obtaining without labour is ruled out on moral grounds; while insisting on its limitation (the 'natural privilege' that takes differences of talent as given, and moreover in practice the differences of enterprise operation must be superimposed) makes it understandable why distribution according to labour cannot level income, why it must be grafted onto the market mechanism, and why common prosperity must separately borrow redistribution rather than hope that primary distribution will make itself equal.

### Distribution according to factors of production

Distribution according to factors of production means that the owner of a factor takes part in income distribution in the form of the factor price, on the strength of his ownership of (or possession and use of) factors of production such as capital, land, knowledge, technology, management and data — interest, profit, rent, dividends on equity contributed in the form of technology, managerial remuneration, data income, and so on. What it practices is the standard of ownership and not the standard of labour, and it is the general principle of the market economy: whoever supplies a factor that is scarce and in demand receives his return at the factor price.

Why must socialism too speak of distribution according to factors? Because at the primary stage the conditions of production are themselves multi-owned (see the arc section), and the process by which the market allocates the conditions of production is the process of factor trading and factor pricing — only if the ownership of factors is economically realised is there anything to say about the incentive compatibility of factor supply. Its relation to distribution according to labour is not substitution but combination: within the same process of distribution, labour contribution and factor contribution take part by their respective standards, and forms such as mixed ownership and employee shareholding are precisely the meeting point of the two standards.

### Micro income distribution and macro income regulation

Micro income distribution is the process of primary distribution realised through the market mechanism: the enterprise obtains its income according to socially necessary labour time (whether individual value is realised depends on whether the single product conforms to socially necessary labour time, and market price and the sector's total income depend on whether total sector supply conforms to the second kind of socially necessary labour time), and is then distributed among the owners of the factors supplied. Macro income regulation is the process of redistribution built on micro distribution and yet independent of it: on the one hand it transfers part of the income of the high earners into the hands of the state through taxation and the like, and on the other hand it guarantees the development of the non-productive spheres through state budget expenditure, using transfer payments and the social security system to provide income protection for the low earners.

Why cut the process of distribution into these two layers? Because the function and the defect of the market mechanism are both exposed here: the market pays by contribution and its incentive is strong — mobilising the enthusiasm of the owners of every factor is precisely the driving force of growth; but the market cannot effectively regulate and balance the interests of all parties, and will not spontaneously maintain social fairness — the old, the weak, the sick and the disabled, the risk of unemployment, the urban-rural gap and external shocks are all outside the price signal. The standards of the two layers are therefore different: the micro layer distributes by efficiency and contribution, the macro layer considers the balance of interests on all sides of society and the needs of overall and long-run development. The significance of the division is to prevent two misplacements — twisting primary distribution by administrative means kills incentive, and leaving redistribution absent destroys social cohesion and the goal of sharing.

### Equalisation of basic public services

Equalisation of basic public services means that all citizens have equal opportunity to basic public services and roughly equal outcomes, while the freedom of choice of social members is respected. It covers compulsory education, public health and basic medical care, public culture, employment and social security, and basic housing security, among other fields.

Why push the question of fairness up to the level of public services? Because cash redistribution can only adjust differences of outcome, whereas basic public services (education, health care) directly decide people's 'subjective conditions of production' when they take part in micro distribution — human capital and capacity to work. In a society of unequal opportunity, income gaps transmit across generations; equalising public services is equivalent to promoting fairness at the starting point and in the process, and it fits 'the intrinsic requirement of developing the socialist market economy' better than high taxation — it both makes up for market failure and preserves the ethical floor of market competition: letting everyone have broadly the same qualification for entry.

### Social security

Social security is the system by which the state and society, through legislation, distribute and redistribute national income, providing a basic livelihood guarantee for social members, and especially for individuals or families with special difficulties in their livelihood; it generally consists of social insurance, social relief (social assistance), social preferential treatment and social welfare. Social security funds come mainly from social insurance contributions paid by employing units and employees and from the social security budgets of governments at every level.

Why is social security an integral component of the market economy and not a welfare decoration? Because marketised distribution inevitably creates exposures to risk: enterprise bankruptcy, unemployment, industrial injury, loss of working capacity in old age — these are precisely the corners that the market's logic of 'paying by contribution' does not reach; and at the same time a market economy without a safety net cannot obtain the political assent of workers, and reform itself cannot be sustained. This chapter also stresses two institutional principles: the social security system must stand apart from enterprises and public institutions (transforming 'enterprise security' into 'social security', otherwise state-owned enterprises cannot enter competition light of load); and the standard of security must be commensurate with the level of economic development and the capacity to bear it on all sides — this is the constraint of the basic national conditions of the primary stage, and a welfare level running ahead of the time is equally unsustainable.

## Problems and analysis

### Why must real distribution according to labour borrow commodity-money relations and the enterprise link?

In Marx's conception, individual labour exists directly as a component of total social labour, and labour time can directly serve as the measure of distribution. The primary stage does not have this premise: the social division of labour is still mediated by the market, and the social character of labour is confirmed only after the product is sold — so the labour a worker supplies is not of itself 'social labour', and it must first make the leap of faith through value. From this three real forms follow: distribution is mediated by commodity-money relations (labour remuneration is linked with the results of operation and with market prices), the enterprise is the intermediate link (equal labour forms unequal pay in enterprises of different performance), and the wage is the mechanism (rather than labour vouchers paid out by the hour). The reason the historical experiments with labour vouchers in socialist countries failed lies exactly here: they assumed that a centre could measure in real time the social effectiveness of each hour of every person's labour, and this is precisely what is informationally infeasible — prices and profits are crude, but they are the only distributed ledger by which society settles its accounts for 'effective labour'. To accept the three deformations is also to accept that distribution according to labour and the market mechanism are not opposites: the principle of equal labour for equal labour can be carried out only through the exchange of equivalents.

### Why must distribution according to labour be combined with distribution according to factors of production?

The first meaning of the combination is the combination of standards: in the public economy the standard of labour commands income distribution, in the non-public economy the standard of ownership commands income distribution, and in mixed ownership both standards are present at once — the distribution structure of a society is the reverse side of its ownership structure. The second meaning is the combination of forms of realisation: all distribution of the conditions of production goes through the market, the determination of factor prices and the distribution of income are joined as one, and both distribution according to labour and distribution according to factors are realised through the price mechanism — for the non-public economy, the process by which the market allocates factors is at once the process of trading ownership and the process of realising ownership, and distribution by the quantity and price of the factors supplied is an exchange of equivalents; for the public economy, equal labour for equal labour cannot be separated from the exchange of equivalents and must be realised through it. The economic effect of the combination is multiple: the worker can obtain income both by supplying labour and by supplying material factors of production and human capital — this mobilises the enthusiasm of factor owners, promotes both labour accumulation and capital accumulation, and guides factors toward the uses of highest efficiency. Conversely, if only the standard of labour is recognised and factor return is denied, that amounts to an uncompensated seizure of non-labour factors, and factor supply (investment, innovation, entrepreneurial talent) will wither or flee abroad — the task of productive forces at the primary stage does not permit such self-injury.

### Where lies the root of the unfairness of Chinese income distribution, and why must one start from the distribution of the conditions of production?

Fairness divides into fairness in primary distribution and fairness in redistribution: fairness in primary distribution is linked with, and decided by, the distribution of the conditions of production, and fairness in redistribution is linked with the balance of interests on all sides of society. Real income gaps can be traced back item by item. At the level of objective conditions of production: in the marketised separation there is a lack of institutional constraint, and various agents may come to control the public conditions of production and so appropriate the public surplus — this is the mechanism of the 'turning public into private' kind of gap. At the level of subjective conditions of production: labour power enters the market under different identities — household registration, establishment status and ownership identity produce unequal pay for equal work and non-uniform national treatment, and unfair allocation of rights inevitably produces unfair transaction and unfair distribution outcomes. At the level of sectoral structure: the distribution of the conditions of production among different sectors is obstructed by monopoly and market segmentation, resources cannot be allocated in equilibrium, and income cannot be distributed in equilibrium among sectors and among their members; the profit-seeking of marketisation may also draw conditions of production into non-productive spheres (speculative possession rather than productive use). The conclusion is methodological: whether the conditions of production can be distributed fairly in the double sense (in ownership structure and in sectoral distribution) is the decisive factor for the fair distribution of income; to entrust fairness entirely to redistributive regulation while ignoring the unfairness of the distribution of the conditions of production will not only treat the symptom and not the root, but will generate new unfairness and efficiency loss by over-reliance on transfer payments — welfare breeds the lazy and taxation drives off capital, and the marginal effect of end-stage regulation is limited.

## Synthetic essay

### Set out the road to achieving fairness of income distribution under the socialist market economy

First, establish the correct frame of appraisal. Both primary distribution and redistribution must handle the relation of fairness to efficiency: what matters in primary distribution for fairness is fairness of rights and fairness of rules — fair distribution of the conditions of production, fair opportunity of competition, factors receiving return by contribution; what matters in redistribution for fairness is fairness at the bottom line and moderation of regulation — holding up the basic livelihood of the distressed groups and restraining the improper sources of excessively high income. Real fairness does not entirely deny income differences arising from differences in objective conditions of production; what matters is to make the rights of producers and workers commensurate with the labour they supply, while at the same time giving people equally the opportunity to accumulate, and avoiding the over-concentration and monopoly of the conditions of production in some members.

Second, grasp the micro foundation, which is the root. Building the micro foundation of equitable income distribution requires four things: regularise institutions and strengthen constraint, preventing various agents in the marketised separation from controlling the public conditions of production and so the public surplus; unify institutional arrangements and practise uniform national treatment, abolishing discriminatory institutional provisions against farmers in the fields of household registration management, freedom of migration, market entry, compulsory education, labour employment, use of public goods, and democratic participation; eliminate market segmentation and promote the equilibrated distribution of the conditions of production among different sectors — break monopoly, implement anti-monopoly policy, remove excessively high barriers to entry between sectors, eliminate the identity and status systems linked with subjective conditions of production, widen the space of choice for social members, and perfect a unified, open, competitive and orderly market system; and strengthen the protection of the rights of vulnerable groups, increase investment in their human capital and improve their income situation.

Third, perfect the system of macro regulation. Regulate high income by taxation (the improvement of personal income tax and of the related tax system for property income), hold up low income by transfer payments and the social security system, and level opportunity by the equalisation of basic public services; the social security system stands apart from enterprises and public institutions, its standard commensurate with the level of economic development and the capacity to bear it on all sides, and a corresponding legal system and a socialised, scientific system of administration must be established to guarantee the regularisation and institutionalisation of the collection, payment and operation of security funds.

Fourth, include fairness in the long process of common prosperity. Common prosperity is not synchronous prosperity and not simultaneous prosperity, but the gradual process of allowing some people and some regions to become rich first, and of the rich leading the not-yet-rich; its institutional meaning is to link the efficiency of micro distribution and the sharing of macro regulation into a closed loop — making the cake big relies on the market distributing by contribution, dividing the cake well relies on redistribution and public services, and the two each take the other as their condition: fairness without efficiency is unsustainable, and efficiency without fairness loses its motive force.

## Limitations and further work

The framework of this chapter has been further unfolded on both the policy and the academic lines, and it should be read with these coordinates in hand. First, **the expansion of the list of factors**: the Sixteenth Congress wrote 'establishing that the factors of production such as labour, capital, technology and management take part in distribution according to contribution' into the programme; the Fourth Plenary Session of the Nineteenth Central Committee in 2019 went further to propose 'perfecting the mechanism by which factors of production such as labour, capital, land, knowledge, technology, management and data are evaluated by the market for their contribution and rewarded according to contribution'; and in 2020 the Centre explicitly listed data as a factor of production — distribution theory expands with the factor structure of the mode of production, and 'distribution according to labour as the mainstay, with multiple modes of distribution coexisting' was elevated at the Fourth Plenary Session of the Nineteenth Central Committee in 2019 into a component of the basic economic system of socialism, standing alongside ownership and the socialist market economy system. Second, **the evolution of redistribution into three-layer regulation**: in 2021 the Central Financial and Economic Affairs Commission proposed 'building the foundational institutional arrangements that coordinate primary distribution, redistribution and tertiary distribution', and social forces such as charity entered the field of distribution; meanwhile formulations such as enlarging the middle-income group, regulating excessively high income, and regulating capital income according to law have made this chapter's proposition that 'the distribution of the conditions of production decides the distribution of income' acute again at the level of equity, property and the stock of wealth — the textbook's frame of 'gaps arise from opportunity' is expanding toward the reality of 'gaps arise from stock wealth'. Third, **the scale of the real data**: the Gini coefficient of Chinese resident income has long stood in the rather high range of about 0.46–0.47 (on the published basis of the National Bureau of Statistics, rising and then levelling in recent years, the exact figure subject to the latest publication), and the urban-rural income ratio has already fallen to about 2.4 (subject to the latest publication) — the results and the pressures of factor marketisation reform and of the building of the redistribution system are both written into these figures. Fourth, **the unresolved questions of the form of realising distribution according to labour**: the remuneration of the heads of state-owned enterprises and the management of total employee wages, the wage system of administrative organs and public institutions, and the distortion of income formation by sectors under a soft budget constraint all show that the deformation of 'the enterprise as intermediate link' itself is still to be regularised — the exploration of workers sharing the surplus of the enterprise (employee shareholding, profit-sharing) is still in progress. Fifth, **the sustainability challenge of social security**: population ageing has raised the pressure on pension revenue and expenditure, national pooling of the basic pension insurance for employees was implemented in 2022 and a portion of state capital has been transferred since 2017 to strengthen the social security fund (10 per cent), the pension and medical insurance systems for urban and rural residents have been integrated, and the pilot of the personal pension system began in 2022 — the system has moved from 'is there any or not' into a new stage of 'is it sustainable'; and the principle that 'the standard of security must be commensurate with the level of economic development and the capacity to bear it' is precisely the academic prescience against the populistisation of welfare, the debates of Okun (the big trade-off) and Atkinson on the compatibility of equality and efficiency giving this principle an echo in the international literature.
