# Chapter 3: Gradual Transition to the Socialist Market Economic System

> **Core question of this chapter**: Both involve a transition from a planned economy to a market economy. Why did the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe adopt radical therapy while China chose a gradual transition? Who drives institutional change, and along what path does it unfold? What new problems does gradual reform itself produce?

## Overview of This Chapter

The question for this chapter is posed by a real comparative experiment. In the late 1980s and early 1990s, the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe went down the path of radical transition: multi-partyism politically, wholesale privatization economically, and shock therapy as policy — prices released overnight, state assets distributed by voucher, both fiscal and monetary policy tight. In the same period China continued gradually: leave the stock untouched, first grow the increment; a dual-track transition, pilot then spread. The subsequent divergence in growth (China averaged near double-digit growth a year in the 1990s, while the transition economies generally experienced a deep output decline before a slow recovery) pushed the economics profession to rethink the political economy of transition, and its source can be traced to a confrontation between two "consensuses." The Washington Consensus, proposed by the economist John Williamson in 1989, was a package of ten policy prescriptions written for Latin American countries trapped in high inflation and repeated debt crises — fiscal discipline, redirection of spending, tax reform, interest-rate liberalization, competitive exchange rates, trade liberalization, liberalization of foreign-investment restrictions, privatization of SOEs, deregulation, and protection of private property rights — whose core was the full opening of capital and markets on the condition of private property, bearing the liberal economic thought of Adam Smith, and called a "manifesto of neoliberal policy." The Beijing Consensus (summarized by the American scholar Vogel in 2004) was built on the Chinese experience that directly challenged the Washington Consensus, and contains three principles: the value of innovation — innovation is the engine of economic development and the means of sustained progress; the effort to build a macro-environment conducive to sustained, balanced, and stable development; and the theory of autonomous development — the path of development is defined by a country's own conditions, not copied from an external prescription.

The theoretical language of the transition question is "the shift from hierarchical rules to property-rights rules": the planned system first builds a pyramid of layered subordination, then locates each agent in that structure, and then defines the power over resource allocation appropriate to that position; under a market system, the allocation power an individual holds is positively correlated with the quantity of their assets. Transition is the handover of allocation power from the government to enterprises and individuals — and "how" that handover is done determines all the differences between the two roads. The differences between radical and gradual show up along four dimensions: in speed, radical reform can be swift and comprehensive, but its shock surface is large, causing a sharp drop in production and deterioration in living standards over a certain period (hence "shock therapy"), whereas gradual reform avoids major disruption and reduces resistance, but the two systems coexist for a long time, breeding corruption and confusion; in sequencing, radical reform advocates price reform first and the rapid full release of prices, while gradual reform advocates first cultivating market actors, clarifying property rights, and restructuring enterprise operating mechanisms, then gradually releasing prices; in stabilization measures, radical reform concentrates economic stability on price stability, taking the overcoming of inflation as its first objective, whereas gradual reform emphasizes transitioning in the course of development, using growth to absorb reform costs; in cost-benefit structure, the resistance costs and friction costs of gradual reform are a positive function of time, with the benefit curve rising slowly in a "small-wave" pattern, while the resistance costs of radical reform are an inverse function of time, with the benefit curve in a "big-wave" pattern, falling deeply first then leaping up. China did not start from a priori theory, but chose the gradual path according to its specific national conditions.

The deeper logic of gradual reform has to be read through the theory of institutional change. In the neo-institutional economics account (North), institutions are humanly devised rules that determine people's interactions, and their most important function is to establish a basic economic order for competition and cooperation by constraining the behavior of individuals seeking to maximize their own welfare or utility. The demand-driven model of institutional change assumes: an interest-maximizing agent, once it finds that the net benefit of creating a new institutional arrangement is positive, will generate a demand for change. But China's market-oriented reform is organized and led by a power center and advanced top-down through administrative orders and laws and regulations — this is summarized as the supply-driven mode of institutional change: the capacity and willingness of the power center to supply new institutional arrangements is the dominant factor determining institutional change. It has five features: the government actor is advantaged in both political power and allocation power, and is the dominant force deciding the direction, speed, sequence, and mode of change; the power center, relying on administrative orders, legal norms, and incentive stimuli, plans, organizes, and implements institutional innovation top-down within the pyramidal hierarchy; actual change occurs only when the power center's institutional benefit exceeds its cost, and friction of interests between the government actor and micro-level actors is unavoidable; the power center sets strict barriers to entry (such as reform pilot rights) to control risk, so lower levels can only innovate after being authorized; and a package of acceptable ideology is promoted to reduce resistance and lower transaction costs. The predicament of this mode is that it conflicts with its own goal: the market economy takes the clarification of property relations as its institutional foundation and requires the implementation of a decentralization principle, while supply-driven change follows precisely the hierarchical rules.

The mechanism that dissolves this conflict is the most creative part of research on China's reform. Within supply-driven change there is "North's paradox": the emergence of an efficient property-rights regime depends on the state's effective definition and protection of property rights, but the state is driven by dual objectives — maximizing total social output and maximizing the monopoly rents of the ruling group (the power center) — and under the dual constraints of transaction costs and competition, it will adopt discriminatory policies toward different interest groups, thereby tolerating the long-term existence of inefficient property structures. The breakthrough in resolving the paradox lies in the middle layer: with the implementation of the granting of power and concessions and the "separate-hearth" fiscal system (rolled out in the early 1980s), local governments gained independent behavioral objectives and patterns, becoming "first-mover coalitions" that actively pursue potential institutional net benefits; when local governments with independent interests became the intermediary linking the power center's willingness to supply institutions with the micro-level actors' demand for institutional innovation, it became possible to break through the entry barriers to institutional innovation that the power center had set, bringing the power center's monopoly-rent maximization into agreement with the protection of efficient property structures — this form, distinct from both supply-driven and demand-induced change, is called the intermediate-diffusion mode of institutional change (summarized by scholars such as Yang Ruilong). There are five routes by which local governments compete for entry rights into institutional innovation: obtaining pilot rights directly from the power center's program; conveying local institutional-innovation demands through formal or informal channels and getting them transformed into a formal supply program; competing for pilot rights after a reform program has been formed; implementing a program through "workarounds" so that the actual institutional arrangement deviates from the power center's intent, thereby obtaining entry rights in disguised form; and engaging in spontaneous institutional innovation in areas neither explicitly authorized nor explicitly forbidden (act first and report later, imitate in secret, forge a new path). The legitimacy of spontaneous innovation then follows a gradual curve: do it first and say nothing — say something only after doing it — obtain a leader's endorsement or inscription, moving from "underground" to "on the surface" — board the train first, buy the ticket later. When institutional change shifts from command-and-control to negotiated coordination, the intermediate diffusion by local governments produces a dual effect: the monopoly rents brought by preferential entry rights induce rent-seeking activity by local governments; and the intermediate diffusion of institutional innovation is in fact a process of establishing exclusive property rights, achieving the internalization of external profits by clarifying the income expectations of micro-level actors. The ultimate direction is the demand-induced mode of institutional change — an individual or group, under given constraints, spontaneously organizes and implements institutional innovation to establish the institutional arrangements and rights definitions expected to maximize its own interest.

## Core Concepts

### The Washington Consensus and the Beijing Consensus

The Washington Consensus is the ten reform propositions that John Williamson put forward in 1989 for the Latin American debt crisis and high-inflation predicament (fiscal discipline and macroeconomic stability, redirection of public spending, tax reform, interest-rate liberalization, competitive exchange rates, trade liberalization, liberalization of foreign-investment restrictions, privatization of state-owned enterprises, deregulation, and protection of private property rights), whose core is the full opening of capital and markets on the condition of private property, and which is regarded as the policy manifesto of neoliberalism. The Beijing Consensus is the contrasting framework that Vogel summarized in 2004 on the basis of China's experience: innovation-driven, a balanced and stable macro-environment, and autonomous development.

Why is this pair of concepts the watershed of transition economics? Because they represent two philosophies about "how institutions are generated": the Washington Consensus believes a universal policy checklist can be rapidly transplanted — institutions can be designed and imported; the Beijing Consensus (and the practice of gradual reform behind it) believes institutions are endogenous and context-dependent — effective institutional arrangements must be compatible with a country's stage of development, organizational resources, and political constraints. The ten prescriptions are not themselves entirely wrong (macroeconomic stability and property protection matter in every country); what is wrong is the "package, rapid, externally imposed" manner of rollout — the failure of shock therapy lies not in the content of the list but in the manner of transplantation.

### Hierarchical Rules and Property-Rights Rules

Hierarchical rules mean first building a pyramidal structure of layered subordination, then locating each agent in that structure, and then defining the allocation power appropriate to that position — the higher the position, the greater the allocation power. Property-rights rules mean that the amount of allocation power an individual holds is positively correlated with the quantity of their assets — the more assets, the greater the allocation power.

This pair of concepts (introduced in the introduction) carries the role of the theoretical axis of transition analysis here: the essence of the marketized transition is the shift from hierarchical rules to property-rights rules, with the core being that allocation power shifts from the government to enterprises and individuals. Understanding this, one can understand why every measure in the reform — granting of power and concessions, dual-track pricing, fiscal contracting, shareholding restructuring — can be read as a round of "reallocation of allocation power," and why the greatest friction of gradual reform always occurs at the interface between the hierarchical structure (the administrative system) and the property-rights structure (the market system).

### North's Paradox

North's paradox holds that an efficient property-rights regime capable of promoting sustained rapid economic growth depends on the state's effective definition and protection of property rights, but driven by the dual objectives of maximizing total social output and maximizing the monopoly rents of the ruling group, the state, in the course of defining and protecting property rights, is under the dual constraints of transaction costs and competition and will adopt discriminatory policies toward different interest groups, and may thereby tolerate the long-term existence of inefficient property structures.

Why is this paradox the key to understanding the state-property rights relationship? Because it reveals the two-sidedness of the state in economic growth: without a state, the cost of defining and protecting property rights is too high to trade (the economies of scale of the "potent for violence"); with a state, rulers may again maintain inefficient ownership structures for their own rents — the state's existence is both the key to economic growth and the source of man-made economic decline. Applied to the transition context: the planned system itself can be read as the extreme form in which monopoly-rent maximization overrides output maximization, and whether reform can succeed depends on whether a mechanism can be found that makes the power center gain more from "protecting efficient property rights" than it loses — and intermediate-diffusion institutional change is precisely such a mechanism.

### Supply-Driven, Intermediate-Diffusion, and Demand-Induced Institutional Change

Supply-driven institutional change refers to the coercive institutional change pushed by the power center: under a given constitutional order and norms of behavioral ethics, the capacity and willingness of the power center to supply new institutional arrangements is the dominant factor determining institutional change, and this capacity and willingness depend mainly on the power structure and balance of forces among the various vested-interest groups in society. Intermediate-diffusion institutional change refers to the form of change in which local governments with independent interests become the intermediary linking the power center's willingness to supply institutions with the micro-level actors' demand for institutional innovation, breaking through the entry barriers to institutional innovation and bringing the power center's monopoly-rent maximization into agreement with the protection of efficient property structures. Demand-induced institutional change refers to institutional innovation spontaneously organized and implemented by an individual or a group, under given constraints, to establish the institutional arrangements and rights definitions expected to maximize its own interest.

Why divide the modes of change into three types? Because the three types correspond to three structures of change dynamics and risk: supply-driven is centralized in decision-making and fast in advancement, but information asymmetry makes the program prone to "distortion," and the change is constrained by the power center's objective function; demand-induced is close to real needs and rich in incentives, but free-riding and externalities make the supply of spontaneous innovation insufficient, and it may conflict with the existing order; intermediate-diffusion, with local governments as intermediaries, combines the legitimacy of top-down with the interest-driven force of bottom-up, and is the most distinctive institutional innovation of China's gradual reform. The three types roughly connect in time (supply-driven in the early reform period -> intermediate-diffusion in the mid-to-late 1980s -> a rising share of demand-induced after the mid-1990s), forming a powerful summary of the evolution of the transition's dynamic structure over more than 40 years.

## Questions and Analysis

### What is the difference between radical and gradual reform?

Radical reform refers to the rapid, comprehensive marketization of a socialist country; gradual reform is marketization carried out on the foundation of industrialization and the socialist constitutional order, emphasizing the use of existing organizational resources and implementing incremental reform while essentially leaving the vested-interest structure untouched. The differences between the two appear in four respects.

Dimension of speed: the radical strategy makes reform swift and comprehensive, but the social shock surface is large, leading over a certain period to a sharp drop in production and deterioration in residents' living conditions — hence the name "shock therapy"; the gradual strategy avoids major social disruption and reduces reform resistance, but the two systems coexist for a long time, easily breeding corruption and confusion and not favorable to the healthy development of the market. Dimension of sequencing: the radical approach advocates price reform first and the rapid full release of prices — set up market rules first, and let the actors grow on their own; the gradual approach advocates adjusting and straightening out prices in gradual stages — first cultivate market actors, clarify property relations, and restructure the operating mechanisms of state-run enterprises, then fully release prices. The former bets that "once prices are right, efficiency comes on its own," the latter that "if the actors are wrong, right prices are useless" — the contrast of the results of these two bets becomes one of the most classic topics of transition research. Dimension of stabilization measures: radical reform concentrates economic stability on price stability, taking the overcoming of inflation as the first goal of stabilizing the macroeconomy; the gradual strategy instead emphasizes advancing the transition within stability and development, absorbing costs through growth. Dimension of cost and benefit: the resistance costs and friction costs of gradual reform are a positive function of time — the longer it is dragged out, the greater the dual-track friction; the benefit curve takes a "small-wave" shape, rising smoothly. The resistance costs of radical reform are an inverse function of time — paid in full at once; the benefit curve takes a "big-wave" shape, falling deeply first then recovering. The J-curve effect (output falling first then rising) is the typical trajectory of radical transition, while China, using the increment as a buffer, almost flattened out the falling segment of the J-curve.

### What are the features of China's gradual reform?

The first feature is using existing organizational resources to advance market-oriented reform. Our country did not "tear down and start over" like the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe — first thoroughly dismantling the hierarchical structure and then establishing property rights — but instead retained the original pyramidal hierarchical structure intact, and organized market-oriented reform through the leaders within that original structure, gradually reducing the role of hierarchical rules in resource allocation as the function of market mechanisms expanded. Hence our reform first appeared as granting-of-power-and-concessions reform, gradually transitioning to price reform and property-rights reform. The advantage of retaining organizational resources is that state capacity and public-good supply are not interrupted in the course of reform; the cost is that the administrative system's own interests settle out in the course of reform, making the transformation of government functions a long-term hard-battle task.

The second feature is incremental reform: without taking the lead in touching the vested-interest structure, market-oriented reform is advanced at the margin — at the margin where hierarchical rules have little effect, choosing modes of interest adjustment that carry the meaning of Pareto improvement, gradually transitioning to the market economic system. The political-science advantage of incremental reform is that it widens the beneficiary surface of reform along with the increment, reducing the resistance of the "dispossessed" to a minimum; its economic advantage is that the old and new systems can divide labor during the transition period (the stock ensures stability, the increment seeks efficiency). The cost is the arbitrage and rent-seeking during the dual-track coexistence period, and the political-economy difficulty of the timing of track convergence.

The third feature is pilot first, then spread: first gain reform experience locally, then promote it nationally. In top-down reform, lower levels transmit reform-demand information upward level by level, and the central government, according to its own constraints and objective function, screens the information and designs and promotes reform programs; because information is asymmetric and "distorted" or "deformed" as it is transmitted through the hierarchical structure, the central government — which is both the organizer of reform and the ultimate bearer of risk — chose pilot-then-spread in order to make the market-oriented reform process irreversible while making the uncertain risk of reform's consequences bearable. The economic substance of the pilot mechanism is a "put option": failure stops at the scope of the pilot, while success yields the option value of promotion — special economic zones, development zones, and comprehensive coordinated reform pilot zones are all the vehicles of this mechanism.

### What are the main problems in our country's gradual reform? What is the direction of further reform?

Judging the government-led marketization process from the standpoint of normative analysis, one finds that gradual reform has produced a series of negative effects. First, the blurring of the code of conduct of political entrepreneurs leads to increasingly serious corruption — dual-track price spreads, approval rights, and pilot monopoly rights all create rents, while the construction of constraint mechanisms lags behind the pace of granting power. Second, the blind pursuit of GDP by local officials leads to neglect of resource, environmental, and social problems, reducing local sustainable development capacity — fiscal contracting and performance assessment lock local attention onto measurable growth numbers. Third, intensifying competition between localities leads to local blockades, producing the so-called "feudal-lord economy" phenomenon — local protection, market segmentation, and repetitive construction weaken the efficiency of the unified national market. Fourth, "pilot first, then spread" lets the enterprises or localities that obtain pilot rights continuously gain monopoly rents and develop preferentially, while other actors' development capacity is relatively insufficient, becoming one of the causes of widening income gaps among different classes and regions — pilot rents are an endogenous distributive distortion of gradual reform.

The root of these negative effects is still the conflict between hierarchical rules and property-rights rules under gradual reform, or in other words the contradiction between politics and the economy under the coexistence of old and new systems: the hierarchical structure has not exited; in new forms (approval, subsidies, pilot allocation) it continues to participate in resource allocation. The fundamental way out, to dissolve the conflict and thereby reduce the negative effects, lies in further emancipating the mind and deepening reform, especially deepening property-rights reform and political reform (transformation of government functions, constraining power, unifying rules), creating better conditions for a further marketized process.

## Comprehensive Discussion

### The Modes and Pathways of Our Country's Transition to the Socialist Market Economic System

The marketized transition question refers to the process of shifting from the traditional planned system to the market system; from the standpoint of the mode of resource allocation, it is in fact a shift from hierarchical rules to property-rights rules, with the core being that allocation power shifts from the government to enterprises and individuals. The choice of transition mode occurs between two routes.

According to the package program built on the Washington Consensus, the content of radical reform appears in three aspects: politically implementing democratization (multi-partyism); economically carrying out wholesale privatization of state-owned enterprises; and adopting shock therapy as economic policy. The Soviet Union and Eastern Europe largely followed this path, and its consequences — a deep drop in output, inflation, and oligarchization — became the empirical footnote on the cost of the radical path.

Our country did not start from a priori theory, but chose a top-down gradual reform path according to its specific national conditions, which has three features. Using existing organizational resources to advance market-oriented reform: retaining the pyramidal hierarchical structure intact and having reform organized by the leaders within it, gradually reducing the role of hierarchical rules as the function of market mechanisms expands — reform begins with granting of power and concessions and gradually transitions to price reform and property-rights reform. Incremental reform: not taking the lead in touching the vested-interest structure, and advancing institutional change at the margin by the method of Pareto improvement. Pilot first, then spread: experiment locally and promote nationally, both ensuring that the direction of reform is irreversible and making the uncertain risk bearable.

The dynamics of the path is composed of the linking of three modes of institutional change. In the early period it was supply-driven: the power center planned, organized, and implemented institutional innovation, with the five features described in the concepts section; among its constraining factors the most important are the constitutional order (the basic structure that defines property rights and control, and delineates space for institutional innovation) and the cost of institutional supply. The contradiction is that the market economy, as the target of institutional choice, takes the clarification of property relations as its institutional foundation and requires decentralization, while supply-driven change follows precisely the hierarchical rules — marketized reform will inevitably buffet the hierarchical rules. From the mid-1980s onward, intermediate-diffusion took over the baton: the granting of power and concessions and the "separate-hearth" fiscal system made local governments "first-mover coalitions," and fiscal contracting not only changed local government behavior but formed the competition between localities that is distinctive to China; local government officials had the entrepreneur characteristics in Schumpeter's sense — under conditions of institutional disequilibrium they keenly discerned potential institutional benefits and, at political risk, seized them first, obtaining political promotion through faster economic development. Local governments competed for entry rights into institutional innovation through five routes (obtaining pilot rights, conveying demands to make them into a formal program, competing for pilot rights, implementing through "workarounds," and spontaneous innovation in areas neither authorized nor forbidden), while spontaneous innovation followed the legitimation curve of "do first and say nothing — say after doing — leader's endorsement — board first, buy the ticket later." That the power center could tolerate and even ratify these innovations after the fact depends on three factors: the negotiating capacity of local governments based on economic strength, the degree of dispersion of authority, and whether the power center gains more from the altered institutional structure than it costs to maintain control over entry rights. As reform deepened, the mode of change converted toward demand-induced: micro-level actors spontaneously organized and implemented institutional innovation, consistent with the intrinsic requirements of the market economy. The normative evaluation of this conversion can be seen in the negative-effect analysis above — gradual reform has no terminal station; the problems it itself produces constitute the agenda of the next round of reform.

## Limitations and Follow-Ups

This chapter's framework leaves four coordinates worth following up. First, **the competition and fusion of explanations**: there is more than one explanation of gradual success — proponents of the "big bang" program (Sachs and others) attribute China's success to structural starting conditions (a high agricultural share, easily released reform dividends), while gradualists (Lin Yifu and others) emphasize the match between development strategy and comparative advantage; North and the theory of induced institutional change (the induced/coercive dichotomy of Ratan and Lin Yifu) were activated by this contrast and entered the mainstream textbooks; this chapter's three-type model is China's contribution to that literature, but the three-stage division needs re-extension for the new pattern after 2010 of "combining top-level design with grassroots exploration." Second, **the continuation and updating of the negative-effect list**: the four negative effects listed here — corruption, GDP-ism, feudal-lord economy, pilot rents — later evolved into more concrete governance agendas, each respectively: comprehensive strict Party governance and reform of the national supervision system (the establishment of the National Supervisory Commission in 2018), incorporating environmental protection and livelihood indicators into performance assessment ("do not judge heroes by GDP alone"), the building of a unified national market (a 2022 opinion expressly clearing rules that impede a unified market), and the normalization of pilot rights (the institutionalization of the negative list in free trade zones). Third, **the double-edgedness of the "Beijing Consensus" concept**: as a challenge to the Washington Consensus it is explanatory, but both officials and scholars remind that it should not be used to endorse an "alternative orthodoxy" — China's policy practice contains many elements that overlap with the Consensus list (macroeconomic stability, openness, property protection), with the differences in sequencing, speed, and autonomy; both essentializing the Chinese experience as "anti-market" and deifying it as a "new paradigm" are misreadings. Fourth, **the incompleteness of transition comparison**: Russia and the Eastern European economies resumed growth after 2000, and some countries completed EU-style institutional transformation, so evaluation of the long-run effects of the radical path is still ongoing — the final verdict on "gradual versus radical" requires a longer historical window, and transition economics is therefore still a live discipline.
