# Chapter 3 · Gradual transition to the socialist market economy system

> **The core question of this chapter**: the same transition, from a planned to a market economy — why did the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe adopt radical therapy while China chose a gradual transition? Who drives institutional change, and along what path does it unfold? And what new problems does gradual reform itself produce?

## The thread of the chapter

The question of this chapter is posed by a real comparative experiment. In the late 1980s and early 1990s the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe took the radical path: multi-party democracy in politics, across-the-board privatization in the economy, shock therapy in policy — prices liberalized overnight, state assets handed out by voucher, a twin tightening of fiscal and monetary policy. Over the same period China kept going gradually: leave the stock untouched, grow the increments first, a dual-track transition, pilots followed by rollout. The growth divergence that followed (China grew at close to double-digit rates a year through the 1990s, while the transition economies generally suffered a deep fall in output before a slow recovery) led economics to rethink the political economy of transition, and the source of that rethink can be traced to a confrontation between two "consensuses". The Washington Consensus was put forward by the economist John Williamson in 1989, and wrote a ten-item prescription for the troubles of the Latin American countries — runaway inflation and recurrent debt crises: fiscal discipline, redirection of expenditure, tax reform, liberalization of interest rates, competitive exchange rates, trade liberalization, liberalization of foreign-investment restrictions, privatization of state enterprises, deregulation, and the protection of private property rights. Its core is the full opening up of capital and markets on the basis of private property, in the tradition of Adam Smith's free economics; it has been called a "neoliberal policy manifesto". The Beijing Consensus (summed up by the American scholar Kroeber in 2004) is built directly against the Washington Consensus on the basis of the Chinese experience, and contains three principles: the value of innovation — innovation as the engine of economic development and the means of continuous progress; the effort to build a broad environment favourable to continuous, balanced and stable development; and autonomous development — the road of development is defined by the country's own conditions rather than copied from an outside prescription.

The theoretical language of the transition problem is "the shift from hierarchical rules to property rights rules": the planned system first builds a pyramid-shaped structure of layered subordination, then fixes each actor's place in that structure, and thereby defines the power to allocate resources as fitting that place; under a market system the power an individual holds over the allocation of resources is positively correlated with the quantity of assets that individual owns. Transition means handing the power to allocate resources from the state to enterprises and to individuals — and "how it is handed over" accounts for the whole of the divergence between the two roads. The differences between the radical and the gradual path show up in four dimensions. Speed: the radical path can be rapid and comprehensive, but the shock is wide, and for a period it brings a sharp collapse in production and a worsening of people's lives (hence the name shock therapy); the gradual path avoids violent jolts and reduces resistance, but the period in which the two systems coexist is long and is prone to breed corruption and disorder. Steps: the radical path argues for price reform first, prices liberalized quickly and comprehensively; the gradual path argues for first cultivating market actors, clarifying property rights and remaking the operating mechanism of enterprises, and only then releasing prices step by step. Stabilizing measures: the radical path concentrates economic stability on the stability of prices, with defeating inflation as the first goal; the gradual path stresses more strongly the idea of transitioning within development, digesting the cost of reform with growth. Cost-benefit structure: for gradual reform the resistance cost and the friction cost are an increasing function of time and the benefit curve climbs slowly in a "small-wave" pattern, while for radical reform the resistance cost is a decreasing function of time and the benefit curve runs in a "large-wave" pattern — a deep fall first and then a jump. China did not start from an a priori theory, but chose the gradual road according to its concrete national conditions.

The deeper logic of gradual reform has to be read through the theory of institutional change. According to the new institutional economics (North), institutions are the artificially devised rules that determine how people relate to one another, and their most important function is that, by constraining the behaviour of individuals who seek to maximize their own welfare or utility, they establish the basic economic order of competition and cooperation. The assumption about the demand for institutional change runs: an actor pursuing maximum benefit, once it finds that the net benefit of creating a new institutional arrangement is positive, will generate a demand for change. But China's market-oriented reform was organized and led by the centre of power and was pushed from top to bottom by administrative order and by law and regulation — which has been summarized as the supply-led mode of institutional change: the capacity and the willingness of the centre of power to provide a new institutional arrangement are the dominant factor determining institutional change. It has five characteristics: the government actor is dominant both in political power and in the power to allocate, and is the leading force deciding the direction, speed, sequence and mode of change; the centre of power plans, organizes and carries out institutional innovation from top to bottom, by administrative order, legal norm and interest incentives, inside the pyramid-shaped hierarchical structure; actual change occurs only when the institutional benefit of the centre of power is greater than its cost, and the friction of interests between the government actor and the micro-level actors is unavoidable; the centre of power sets strict entry barriers in order to control risk (such as the right to pilot a reform), so a subordinate level can innovate only once it has been authorized; and an acceptable ideology is propagated to reduce resistance and to lower transaction costs. The predicament of this mode is that it conflicts with its own goal: the market economy takes the clarification of property relations as its institutional foundation and requires the principle of decentralization, while the change driven by supply follows exactly the hierarchical rules.

The mechanism that resolves the conflict is the most creative part of research on the Chinese reform. Within supply-led change there is a "North paradox": the appearance of an efficient property-rights system depends on the state's effective definition and protection of property rights, but the state is driven by a double goal — the maximization of total social output and the maximization of the monopoly rents of the ruling group (the centre of power) — and under the double constraint of transaction costs and competition it will adopt discriminatory policies towards different interest groups, and so may tolerate an inefficient structure of property rights for a long time. The breakthrough in dissolving the paradox lies in the middle layer: as devolving power and ceding profit were implemented together with the "separate stoves" fiscal system (introduced in the early 1980s), local governments came to have independent goals and modes of action, and became the "first action group" actively seeking out the latent net benefit of new institutions; when such local governments, whose interests have become independent, become the intermediary link communicating the centre of power's willingness to supply institutions with the micro-level actors' demand for institutional innovation, they may break through the entry barriers to institutional innovation set by the centre of power, so that the maximization of the centre's monopoly rents and the protection of an efficient structure of property rights fall into line — this form, distinct from both the supply-led and the demand-induced, is called the middle-diffusion mode of institutional change (summarized by Yang Ruilong and other scholars). Local governments compete for the right of entry into institutional innovation by five routes: obtaining the right to pilot directly from a scheme issued by the centre of power; transmitting local demand for institutional innovation through formal or informal channels and getting it turned into a formal supply scheme; competing for the pilot right once a reform scheme has been formed; implementing a scheme by "variance", so that the actual institutional arrangement departs from the centre of power's intention and the right of entry is obtained in effect; and carrying out spontaneous institutional innovation in fields where neither authorization nor prohibition is explicit (acting first and reporting afterwards, imitating in secret, or striking out on a different path). The legitimacy of spontaneous innovation follows a gradual curve: do it without saying it — say it only after it has been done — a leader's instruction or inscription, moving from "underground" to "above ground" — get on the bus first, buy the ticket later. When institutional change shifts from command and control to bargaining and coordination, the middle diffusion by local governments has a double effect: the monopoly rents that come with priority entry provoke rent-seeking by local governments, and the middle diffusion of institutional innovation is in fact a process of establishing exclusive property rights, internalizing external profits by clarifying the benefit expectations of the micro-level actors. The final direction is the demand-induced mode of institutional change — individuals or groups, under given constraints, spontaneously organizing and implementing institutional innovation in order to establish the institutional arrangements and definitions of rights expected to maximize their own interests.

## Core concepts

### The Washington Consensus and the Beijing Consensus

The Washington Consensus is the set of ten reform propositions that John Williamson put forward in 1989 in response to the Latin American debt crisis and the plight of high inflation (fiscal discipline and macroeconomic stability, redirection of public expenditure, tax reform, liberalization of interest rates, competitive exchange rates, trade liberalization, liberalization of foreign-investment restrictions, privatization of state-owned enterprises, deregulation, and the protection of private property rights); its core is the full opening up of capital and markets under private property, and it is regarded as the policy manifesto of neoliberalism. The Beijing Consensus is the contrasting framework that Kroeber summarized in 2004 on the basis of the Chinese experience: innovation-driven, an environment of balanced stability, and autonomous development.

Why is this pair of concepts the watershed of transition economics? Because they stand for two philosophies about "how institutions are produced": the Washington Consensus believes that a universal list of policies can be transplanted quickly — institutions can be designed and imported; the Beijing Consensus (and the gradual reform practice behind it) believes that institutions are endogenous and context-dependent — an effective institutional arrangement must be compatible with the country's stage of development, its organizational resources and its political constraints. The ten prescriptions are not all wrong in themselves (macroeconomic stability and the protection of property rights matter in any country); what is wrong is the manner of "all at once, at high speed, imposed from outside" — the failure of shock therapy lies not in the content of the list but in the way of transplanting it.

### Hierarchical rules and property rights rules

Hierarchical rules mean first building a pyramid-shaped structure of layered subordination, then fixing each actor's place in that structure, and thereby defining the power to allocate resources as fitting that place — the higher the place, the greater the power to allocate. Property rights rules mean that the size of the power an individual holds over the allocation of resources is positively correlated with the quantity of that individual's assets — the more assets, the greater the power to allocate.

This pair of concepts (introduced in the introduction) carries here the role of the theoretical axis of transition analysis: the essence of the market-oriented transition is the shift from hierarchical rules to property rights rules, and its core is the transfer of the power to allocate resources from the state to enterprises and individuals. Once this is understood, you can see why every measure in the reform — devolving power and ceding profit, the dual price track, fiscal contracting, conversion to joint-stock form — can be read as one round of "reallocating the power to allocate", and why the greatest friction of the gradual reform has always occurred at the boundary between the hierarchical structure (the administrative system) and the property-rights structure (the market system).

### The North paradox

The North paradox is this: an efficient property-rights system capable of promoting sustained rapid growth depends on the state effectively defining and protecting property rights, but the state, driven by the double goal of maximizing total social output and maximizing the monopoly rents of the ruling group, is under the double constraint of transaction costs and competition in defining and protecting property rights, and will adopt discriminatory policies towards different interest groups, and so may tolerate an inefficient structure of property rights for a long time.

Why is this paradox the key to understanding the relationship between the state and property rights? Because it reveals the two-sidedness of the state in economic growth: without a state the cost of defining and protecting property rights is too high to transact (economies of scale in "the potential for violence"); with a state, the rulers may maintain an inefficient structure of ownership for the sake of their own rents — the existence of the state is at once the key to economic growth and a source of artificially induced economic decline. Applied to the transition: the planned system itself can be read as the extreme form in which the maximization of monopoly rents overrides the maximization of output, and whether the reform succeeds depends on whether a mechanism can be found in which the centre of power gains more from "protecting efficient property rights" than it loses — the middle-diffusion mode of institutional change is exactly such a mechanism.

### Supply-led, middle-diffusion, and demand-induced institutional change

Supply-led institutional change is the coercive institutional change advanced by the centre of power: within a given constitutional order and norms of conduct, the capacity and the willingness of the centre of power to provide a new institutional arrangement are the dominant factor determining institutional change, and this capacity and willingness depend mainly on the structure of power and the balance of strength among the various vested interest groups in society. Middle-diffusion institutional change is the form of change in which local governments whose interests have become independent become the intermediary link communicating the centre of power's willingness to supply institutions with the micro-level actors' demand for institutional innovation, break through the entry barriers to institutional innovation, and bring the centre's maximization of monopoly rents into line with the protection of an efficient structure of property rights. Demand-induced institutional change is the institutional innovation that individuals or a group spontaneously organize and implement, under given constraints, in order to establish the institutional arrangements and definitions of rights expected to maximize their own interests.

Why divide the modes of change into three types? Because the three types correspond to three structures of driving force and three structures of risk: the supply-led type concentrates decision-making and moves fast, but asymmetric information makes the scheme easy to be "distorted", and the change is bound by the centre of power's objective function; the demand-induced type is close to real demand and richly incentivized, but free-riding and externalities leave spontaneous innovation under-supplied, and it may conflict with the order in place; the middle-diffusion type uses local governments as the intermediary, and combines the legitimacy of the top-down with the interest drive of the bottom-up — the most distinctive mechanism innovation of China's gradual reform. The three types roughly connect in time (supply-led in the early period of the reform → middle diffusion in the mid- to late 1980s → a rising share of demand-induced after the mid-1990s), and together they form a strong summary of how the driving structure of more than forty years of transition has evolved.

## Questions and analysis

### What is the difference between radical reform and gradual reform?

Radical reform is the rapid, comprehensive marketization carried out in a socialist country; gradual reform is marketization carried out on the foundation of industrialization and the socialist constitutional order, stressing the use of existing organizational resources and carrying out incremental reform without basically disturbing the pattern of vested interests. The differences between the two appear in four respects.

The dimension of speed: the radical strategy makes reform rapid and comprehensive, but the social shock is wide, and for a period it brings a sharp fall in production and a worsening of people's living conditions, hence the name "shock therapy"; the gradual strategy avoids major social jolts and reduces resistance to reform, but the period in which the two systems coexist is long, which easily breeds corruption and disorder and is not good for the healthy development of markets. The dimension of steps: the radical path argues for price reform first, prices liberalized quickly and comprehensively — set the rules of the market first and wait for the actors to grow themselves; the gradual path argues for adjusting and straightening out prices in stages — first cultivate market actors, make property relations explicit, remake the operating mechanism of state-run enterprises, and only then liberalize prices comprehensively. The former bets that "get the price right and efficiency will come of its own"; the latter bets that "if the actors are wrong, getting the price right is no use" — the contrast of the outcomes of these two bets has become the classic problem of transition research. The dimension of stabilizing measures: the radical path concentrates economic stability on price stability, and the first goal of stabilizing the macroeconomy is to defeat inflation; the gradual strategy stresses more strongly advancing the transition within stability and development, and digesting costs with growth. The dimension of cost and benefit: for gradual reform the resistance cost and the friction cost are an increasing function of time — the longer it drags on, the greater the dual-track friction; the benefit curve rises steadily in a "small-wave" pattern. For radical reform the resistance cost is a decreasing function of time — paid off in one instalment; the benefit curve runs in a "large-wave" pattern, falling deeply first and then recovering. The J-curve effect (output falling first and then rising) is the typical trajectory of a radical transition, while China, using the increments as a buffer, almost flattened out the downward part of the J curve.

### What are the characteristics of China's gradual reform?

The first characteristic is the use of existing organizational resources to advance market-oriented reform. China did not "start again from scratch" as the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe did — first completely dissolving the hierarchical structure and then establishing property rights — but preserved the original pyramid-shaped hierarchical structure intact, and used the leaders inside that structure to organize market-oriented reform, gradually reducing the role of hierarchical rules in the allocation of resources as the effect of market mechanisms gradually widened. As a result the reform at the outset appeared as reform by devolving power and ceding profit, and gradually moved over to price reform and reform of the property-rights system. The advantage of preserving organizational resources is that national capacity and the supply of public goods are not interrupted during the reform; the price is that the administrative system's own interests settle into the reform, and the transformation of government functions becomes a long-term hard task.

The second characteristic is incremental reform: without taking the lead in disturbing the pattern of vested interests, the reform is advanced at the margin in a market direction — at the margin where hierarchical rules matter less, a way of adjusting interests with the significance of a Pareto improvement is chosen, and the transition to a socialist market economy system proceeds gradually. The political advantage of incremental reform is that it widens the circle of beneficiaries as the increment widens, keeping the resistance of the "dispossessed" to a minimum; its economic advantage is that the old and the new systems can divide the labour during the transition period (the stock is kept stable, the increment seeks efficiency). The price is the arbitrage and rent-seeking during the period of dual tracks, and the political-economic difficulty of the timing for merging the tracks.

The third characteristic is pilot first, then expand: first gain reform experience locally, then roll it out across the board. In a top-down reform, subordinate levels transmit reform demand upwards step by step, and the central government, following its own constraints and objective function, screens the information, designs a reform scheme and rolls it out; because information is asymmetric and gets "distorted" or "warped" as it is transmitted through the hierarchical structure, the central government — which is at once the organizer of the reform and the ultimate bearer of its risk — chooses to pilot first and then expand, in order to make the market-oriented reform process irreversible and at the same time to keep the risk of the reform's uncertain outcomes within its means. The economic substance of the pilot mechanism is a "put option": failure stops at the boundary of the pilot, and success yields the option value of expansion — special economic zones, development zones and comprehensive integrated reform pilot zones are all the carriers of this mechanism.

### What are the main problems of China's gradual reform, and what is the direction of further reform?

Judging the government-led process of marketization from the standpoint of normative analysis, one finds that gradual reform has produced a series of negative effects. First, the blurring of the code of conduct for political entrepreneurs has led to steadily worsening corruption — the gap between the two price tracks, approval authority and the monopoly right to pilot all create rents, while the building of constraining mechanisms has lagged behind the pace of devolution. Second, the blind pursuit of GDP by local government officials has led to the neglect of resources, the environment and social problems, and has reduced local sustainable development capacity — fiscal contracting and the assessment of official performance have locked local attention onto measurable growth figures. Third, the intensifying competition between localities has produced local blockade, giving rise to the so-called "princely economy" — local protection, market fragmentation and duplicated construction have weakened the efficiency of the unified national market. Fourth, "pilot first, then expand" has let the enterprises or localities that obtained the pilot right keep earning monopoly rents and develop preferentially, while other actors have had relatively weaker capacity to develop, which has become one of the causes of the widening income gap between different classes and between different regions — pilot rents are an endogenous distributional distortion of gradual reform.

The root of these negative effects is still the conflict between hierarchical rules and property rights rules under conditions of gradual reform, or, said differently, the contradiction between politics and economics under conditions in which the old and the new systems coexist: the hierarchical structure has not withdrawn, and it continues to take part in the allocation of resources in new forms (approval, subsidies, the distribution of pilot rights). The fundamental way out of resolving the conflict and thereby reducing the negative effects lies in further opening up the mind and deepening the reform, and especially in deepening reform of the property-rights system and of the political system (transforming government functions, constraining power, unifying rules), in order to create better conditions for the further process of marketization.

## Comprehensive discussion

### The way and the path of China's transition to a socialist market economy system

The question of the market-oriented transition is the process of moving from the traditional planned system to the market system; from the standpoint of the way resources are allocated it is in fact the shift from hierarchical rules to property rights rules, the core being the transfer of the power to allocate resources from the state to enterprises and individuals. The choice of the mode of transition takes place between two routes.

According to the package of measures built on the Washington Consensus, radical reform is embodied in three respects: democratization (a multi-party system) in politics; across-the-board privatization of state-owned enterprises in the economy; and shock therapy in economic policy. The Soviet Union and Eastern Europe broadly followed this path, and its consequences — a deep fall in output, inflation, oligarchization — became the empirical footnote on the cost of the radical route.

China did not start from an a priori theory, but chose the top-down path of gradual reform according to its concrete national conditions, and this path has three characteristics. Using existing organizational resources to advance market-oriented reform: the pyramid-shaped hierarchical structure was preserved intact and the leaders inside it organized the reform, with the role of hierarchical rules gradually reduced as the effect of market mechanisms widened — the reform started with devolving power and ceding profit, and passed in sequence to price reform and reform of the property-rights system. Incremental reform: not taking the lead in disturbing the pattern of vested interests, and advancing institutional change at the margin in the manner of a Pareto improvement. Pilot first, then expand: trial locally and rollout across the board, which at once keeps the direction of reform irreversible and keeps the risk of uncertainty within its means.

The dynamics of the path are made up of the connection of the three modes of institutional change. In the early period the supply-led mode: the centre of power plans, organizes and carries out institutional innovation, with the five characteristics described in the section on concepts; among its constraining factors the most important are the constitutional order (the basic structure defining the property rights and the control, delimiting the space for institutional innovation) and the cost of institutional supply. The contradiction is that the market economy, which is the goal of the choice of institutions, takes the clarification of property relations as its institutional foundation and requires decentralization, while the change driven by supply follows exactly the hierarchical rules — market-oriented reform must collide with the hierarchical rules. From the mid-1980s the middle-diffusion mode took over the baton: devolving power and ceding profit together with the "separate stoves" fiscal system turned local governments into the "first action group", and fiscal contracting not only changed local government behaviour but also created the competition between localities that is particular to China; local government officials have the entrepreneurial characteristics in the Schumpeterian sense — under conditions of institutional disequilibrium they keenly spot the latent institutional benefit and take the political risk of seizing it first, winning political promotion through faster economic development. Local governments compete for the right of entry into institutional innovation by five routes (obtaining the pilot right, transmitting demand so that it becomes a formal scheme, competing for the pilot right, implementing by "variance", and spontaneous innovation in fields neither authorized nor prohibited), and spontaneous innovation follows the curve of legitimation "do it without saying it — say it after it is done — the leader's instruction — get on the bus first, buy the ticket later". The reason the centre of power is able to tolerate and even ratify after the fact these innovations depends on three factors: the bargaining power of local governments based on economic strength, the degree of diffusion of authority, and whether the benefit the centre of power gains from the altered institutional structure is greater than the cost of maintaining control over the right of entry. As the reform deepened, the mode of change converted towards the demand-induced: micro-level actors spontaneously organized and implemented institutional innovation, in line with the inner requirements of the market economy. The normative evaluation of the conversion is the analysis of negative effects given above — gradual reform has no terminal station, and the problems it produces itself make up the agenda of the next round of reform.

## Limits and what follows

The framework of this chapter leaves four coordinates worth following up. First, **the competition and the fusion of explanations**: there is more than one explanation of the gradual success — the advocates of the "big bang" plan (Sachs and others) attribute China's success to its structural starting point (a high share of agriculture, reform dividends easy to release), while the gradualists (Lin Yifu and others) stress the fit between the development strategy and comparative advantage; after the confrontation here, North and the theory of demand-induced institutional change (Laitin's and Lin Yifu's dichotomy of induced and coercive) were activated and entered the mainstream textbooks, and the three-type model of this chapter is this literature's Chinese contribution, but the three-stage division needs to be extended again for the new pattern after 2010 of "combining top-level design with grassroots exploration". Second, **the continuation and the updating of the list of negative effects**: the four negative effects listed here — corruption, GDP-ism, the princely economy, pilot rents — subsequently evolved into more concrete agendas of governance: comprehensive and strict discipline of the Party and the reform of the national supervisory system (the establishment of the supervisory commissions in 2018), the inclusion of environmental protection and public welfare indicators in the assessment of official performance ("not judging by GDP alone"), the building of a unified national market (a 2022 opinion explicitly requiring the clearing away of regulations that obstruct a unified market), and the regularization of the pilot right (the institutionalization of the negative list of the pilot free trade zones). Third, **the two-sidedness of the concept of the "Beijing Consensus"**: it is explanatory as a challenge to the Washington Consensus, but both officials and scholars have at the same time cautioned against endorsing it as an "alternative orthodoxy" — China's policy practice contains many elements that overlap with the consensus list (macroeconomic stability, openness, the protection of property rights), and the difference lies in sequence, speed and autonomy; to essentialize the Chinese experience as "anti-market" or to deify it as a "new paradigm" is a misreading. Fourth, **the incompleteness of the transition comparison**: the Russian and Eastern European economies resumed growth after 2000 and some countries have completed their EU-oriented institutional reform, and the long-term assessment of the radical path is still in progress — the final verdict of "gradual against radical" needs a longer historical window, and transition economics therefore remains a living discipline.
