# Chapter 8 · Economic structural adjustment under the socialist market economy

> **The central question of this chapter**: why does the economy of a developing country naturally split into 'two worlds'? How was China's urban-rural dual structure manufactured by institutions, and how should it be dismantled? What force drives the upgrading of industrial structure — the market, technology, or the state?

## The arc of this chapter

Development economics formed in the 1950s a judgment of deep influence: the national economy of a developing country is not 'one' economy but two economies existing side by side. Lewis's 1954 dual-economy model gives the classic portrait — the traditional agricultural sector holds a mass of surplus labour with marginal productivity near or at zero, wages anchored at subsistence; the modern industrial sector can absorb rural labour without limit at a wage slightly above subsistence, converting surplus labour into accumulation and growth; once the surplus labour is absorbed, wages begin to rise and the economy reaches the 'turning point'. The dual economic structure (a modern sector of mainly urban industry coexisting with a traditional sector of mainly rural agriculture, an oversized traditional sector, a marked urban-rural gap) thereby became a shared feature of the national economies of developing countries. Three indicators measure this duality: comparative labour productivity (the ratio of a sector's share of output value to its share of employment — agriculture generally below 1, non-agriculture generally above 1); the dual comparison coefficient (the ratio of agricultural to non-agricultural comparative labour productivity, in theory between 0 and 1 and moving inversely to the strength of duality — the smaller the coefficient, the greater the duality); and the dual contrast index (the absolute value of the gap between the non-agricultural share of output value and the share of labour, moving with the strength of duality). Together the three answer one question: how much labour is held down in the low-productivity sectors.

China's dual structure has its own institutional causes, and this is the part of the chapter most worth reading closely. In developing countries generally the duality is a 'stage illness' of development; China's duality is largely a 'manufactured article' of policy and system. First, the catch-up development strategy produced sectoral differences in productivity: city-based heavy industrialization transferred agricultural funds forcibly to industrial accumulation through the price 'scissors gap' between industrial and agricultural products — the Chinese version of Soviet-style primitive accumulation. Second, the traditional system created the urban-rural split: the household registration system together with its supporting institutions — grain supply, non-staple food and fuel supply, housing, employment, social security — ruled out the free movement of labour between city and countryside, stripped farmers of the right to settle and work in the cities, and pinned them to the land, so surplus labour was stranded in excess. Third, the tilt toward non-agricultural industries widened the gap: state investment concentrated in capital-intensive industries, and the heavy-industry route raised per-capita capital in industry rapidly, so the same capital absorbed less and less employment — capital deepening at the cost of excluding labour. The symptoms were four 'gaps': low agricultural labour productivity, widening urban-rural and regional disparities, a widening urban-rural income gap, and a steep urban-rural difference in consumption levels. The core task of structural transformation is thus plain: to move the excess surplus labour out — through two channels, transfer in place (into township industry, 'leaving the land without leaving the village') and transfer to towns and cities ('leaving both the land and the village'); large-scale transfer began with the reform and opening up, and from the mid-1990s the transfer to towns clearly accelerated.

The second layer of structural problems lies between industries. An industry is a set of enterprises producing similar or identical products (in the narrow sense, industries formed by the general division of labour; in the broad sense, those formed by special division of labour on top of the general); industrial structure is the economic links and quantitative proportions formed among the industries of the national economy in the process of reproduction — the three industries that develop in sequence (agriculture, industry, services) and the labour-, capital-, technology- and knowledge-intensive industries classified by resource intensity are its two basic dimensions. Industrial structure evolves by laws of its own: as per-capita income rises, the centre of gravity of labour and output moves in order from the primary industry to the secondary and tertiary; structure is a dynamic category, its adjustment driven by four forces — social demand (the market orientation), technological progress (the direct motive force), institutional arrangements (the systemic guarantee), and resource supply (the material base). The textbook's diagnosis of China's industrial structure, at its time of writing, listed five problems: a low level of structure; a low level of industrial development (slow agricultural upgrading, a heavy-chemical industry with too large a share, assembly-processing dominant, basic industry and processing industry out of step, high technology underdeveloped, the tertiary sector lagging); false upgrading (the share of processing industry rising while basic industries are underdeveloped and returns are low — a 'fake upgrade' of structure); low industrial concentration; and regional convergence of industrial structure (the textbook put the similarity rate between eastern and central structures at about 93.5% and between central and western at about 97.9% — measurements of that time; for current figures rely on the latest research). The principle of adjustment combines market regulation with government guidance; the direction: consolidate the agricultural base, strengthen infrastructure, revive equipment manufacturing, develop high-technology industries, raise the share of services, develop a circular economy, optimize industrial organization and regional layout, and pursue a mutually beneficial opening strategy.

The third layer is the motive force of technology. Technological progress is not only the more important source of growth (compared with adding to capital and labour) but the prime mover of structural evolution — opening new technologies into new industries, transforming traditional industries, driving the renewal of structure. The technology path of latecomer countries holds a 'latecomer advantage': with the external stock of technology highly developed, some stages of development can be skipped and new technologies adopted directly; but the exercise of the advantage is constrained by three things — the technology 'blueprint' comes at a price and strains financial capacity, part of the blueprint has already been retired by its country of origin, and the most advanced technology does not necessarily suit the social needs of the backward country (from which comes the question of 'appropriate technology': the choice among capital-, technology- and labour-intensive types should be assessed for social impact from nine aspects — production, remuneration, indirect income, savings, employment and distribution, skills, institutions, participation, and birth planning). The relation between technological innovation and market structure (the degree of competition decides the necessity of innovation, firm size affects the market prospect of innovation, monopoly power decides its durability — monopoly-prospect-driven and competition-prospect-driven innovation) and the diffusion of innovation (five conditions for the actual use of a new technology) form the micro mechanism of the technology-structure interaction. The policy landing point is the building of the mechanism of technological progress: making enterprises the subjects of innovation, building technology-leading enterprises, perfecting the technology market, intensive management, the policy environment, equipment renewal and technical upgrading, and high-technology development zones.

## Core concepts

### The dual economic structure and the dual comparison coefficient

The dual economic structure is the economic structure in which a modern sector of mainly urban industry coexists with a traditional sector of mainly rural agriculture, the traditional share being too large, the modern sector underdeveloped, and the urban-rural gap marked; it is a shared feature of the national economies of developing countries. The dual comparison coefficient is the ratio of agricultural to non-agricultural comparative labour productivity, in theory between 0 and 1 and moving inversely to the strength of the dual structure.

Why invent a 'coefficient' specifically to measure duality? Because 'there is a gap between city and countryside' is a qualitative judgement, while policy needs a comparable scale: the comparison coefficient compresses the productivity gap between the two sectors into one number, so that degrees of duality across countries and across time can be ranked and tracked — the low level and slow rise of China's coefficient before and after reform is itself the dashboard of structural transformation. Measurement precedes governance: without this scale, 'urban-rural integration' can set no target and accept no delivery.

### Comparative labour productivity and the dual contrast index

Comparative labour productivity is the ratio of a sector's share of output value to that sector's share of employed labour, showing how much output value each 1% of labour in that sector creates — agriculture generally below 1, non-agriculture generally above 1. The dual contrast index is the absolute value of the gap between the non-agricultural (or industrial) share of output value and the share of labour, moving with the strength of duality.

Why use the two indicators as a pair? Because they capture two sides of duality: comparative labour productivity measures 'how far productivity differs for the same labour in different sectors' (the efficiency gap), while the contrast index measures 'how far the output share and the employment share are out of line' (structural mismatch) — a large contrast index means much labour is held down in low-output sectors, and both the potential and the pressure of transfer are quantified at once. The policy meaning is direct: the convergence of the contrast index is exactly the process of transferring surplus labour and converging productivity.

### Industrial structure and false upgrading

Industrial structure is the set of economic links and quantitative proportions formed among the industries of the national economy in reproduction. False upgrading is the phenomenon of the share of processing industry rising while basic industries are underdeveloped and returns are low — an 'upgrading' in name only.

Why name 'fake upgrading' at all? Because the policy incentives of structural upgrading invite number games and misaligned development: expanding the processing and assembly links is easy, breaking through in basic materials and equipment is hard; judged only by the ranking of industrial shares, an economy can 'prematurely' enter high-processing stages while its base is weak, with the result that the upstream and downstream break apart, import dependence climbs, and returns diverge from the structural 'height'. The concept of false upgrading reminds us: the substance of structural evolution is the evolution of productivity and of the depth of the division of labour, and shares are only the appearance — an 'upgrade' divorced from base and efficiency cannot last.

### Latecomer advantage and appropriate technology

Latecomer advantage is the advantage of backwardness available to late-developing countries whose external environment is relatively favourable (above all, a highly developed technology base), allowing them to skip certain stages and adopt new technologies directly; its constraints include the price of the technology 'blueprint', the obsolescence of part of the blueprint, and the fit between advanced technology and the country's own social needs. Appropriate technology is the type of technology that fits a country's factor endowment, stage of development and social needs (the choice among capital-, technology- and labour-intensive types), selected by assessing social impact from the aspects of production, remuneration, indirect income, savings, employment and distribution, skills, institutions, participation, and birth planning.

Why is the latecomer advantage not cashed in automatically? Because it is in essence a set of options, not a gift: skipping the intermediate stages presupposes absorptive capacity (human capital, infrastructure, the institutional environment), otherwise the most advanced technology takes root poorly on arrival — that is the substance of the three blueprint constraints. Appropriate technology pushes the question one layer deeper: for a backward economy, 'the most advanced' is not necessarily 'the most suitable' — labour-intensive technology may create more jobs and improve distribution, and intermediate technology may be better for learning and diffusion. The essence of technology choice is a social comparison, not a technical one — the methodological self-awareness that distinguishes development economics from growth theory at large.

## Problems and analysis

### What are the features and causes of China's dual economic structure?

Four features: agriculture lags far behind industry and the other sectors, with low agricultural labour productivity; the urban-rural and regional gaps widen; the urban-rural income gap widens; the urban-rural difference in consumption is steep. The four features point to one diagnosis — China's duality is the product not only of the stage of development but of the system. Three causes. The catch-up strategy produced sectoral differences in productivity: city-based industrialization converted the agricultural surplus forcibly into industrial accumulation through the 'scissors gap' in industrial-agricultural prices; agriculture was drained and stalled at once. The traditional economic system created the urban-rural split: the combination of household registration, grain supply, non-staple food and fuel supply, housing, employment and social security ruled out free movement of labour and stripped farmers of the right to settle and work in cities — a mass of surplus labour was institutionally stranded in the countryside, deepening the duality. Non-agricultural development policy widened the gap: state investment concentrated in capital-, technology- and knowledge-intensive industries while investment in agricultural research and in biochemical and machinery development was seriously short — the investment gap between sectors became a productivity gap. The three causes together show: the key variable in dismantling the dual structure is institutions (household registration and the equalization of public services, the integration of factor markets), not just agricultural technology.

### What factors shape the adjustment of industrial structure?

Social demand is the market orientation and the starting point of adjustment: personal consumption demand (the demands of subsistence, development and enjoyment) fixes the structure of final demand, changes in fixed-investment demand create new industries and alter the existing structure, and export demand transmits through international comparative advantage into the domestic industrial make-up. Technological progress is the direct motive force: opening new production technologies into new industries, driving the technical transformation of traditional industries, driving the renewal of structure. Institutional arrangements are the systemic guarantee: they shape the mode of resource allocation, the direction of adjustment and its state — the same set of demand and technology conditions converges to different structures under different institutions (heavy-industry priority under the planned system and demand-led adjustment under the market system are the standing examples). Resource supply is the material base: the endowments of natural, human and financial resources directly determine and constrain the space for adjustment. The value of the four-factor analysis is to guard against single-cause attribution: a structural problem may be weak demand guidance (a market defect), but may equally be a technology bottleneck, an institutional distortion or a resource constraint — matching the remedy requires locating the dominant factor first.

## Synthetic essay

### Policies for transferring rural surplus labour in the transformation of China's dual structure

Root-cause analysis is the premise of policy. The basis of the excess surplus labour is the excess rural population with a share too large against a relatively scarce land resource, plus an unreasonable rural industrial structure and lagging non-farm economy that leave too little employment space inside the countryside; the most important root is the dual economic structure formed under the planned system — institutional factors such as household registration excluded urban-rural mobility, and the capital-intensive heavy-industry route made the same capital absorb less and less employment. Transfer policy is therefore a two-pronged combination of institutions and employment. First, accelerate rural urbanization: urbanization is not merely the movement of population across regions but the transfer of labour from the traditional to the modern sector, setting off a leap in labour productivity — the urbanization rate rose from about 18% at the start of reform to about 66% today (National Bureau of Statistics basis; for the current figure rely on the latest release), the most direct progress bar of China's structural transformation. Second, support township enterprises and realize transfer in place: in-place transfer was the main channel in the early reform years and relieved the cities' carrying pressure, at the cost of dispersion and diseconomies of agglomeration — later trends proved the two channels complements rather than substitutes. Third, improve rural education: the key to converting population pressure into human-resource advantage is education — the sustainability of transfer depends on whether the skill structure of the labour force can match the post structure of the modern sector. Fourth, progressively abolish the dual household registration system: unify 'agricultural' and 'non-agricultural' population into a single resident registration, let market competition rather than administrative status allocate labour, and give everyone equal standing for entry into the cities — the State Council opinion of 2014 abolished the dual registration of agricultural and non-agricultural hukou and unified it into resident registration; the textbook's policy proposal entered institutional reality, while the equalization of the public services attached to the hukou (education, health care, housing security) is still under way.

### The main problems of China's industrial structure and the policies for them

Five problems. The level of industrial structure is low, hurting the optimal allocation of resources, overall returns and international competitiveness. The level of industrial development is low: in the primary sector, agriculture still takes a high share but upgrades slowly, with backward technology and low intensification; in the secondary sector, heavy and chemical industry takes too large a share, manufacturing is dominated by assembly and processing, basic and processing industry grow out of step (energy and materials lag, processing capacity is excessive, construction is duplicated), traditional industries dominate while high technology is underdeveloped; the tertiary sector is backward overall. The structure shows false upgrading — a spurious rise of the processing share on a weak base. Industrial concentration is low — over-dispersed competition wastes resources and depresses profit margins, weakening overall competitiveness. Regional structures converge — the similarity rates between eastern and central, and between central and western structures, run as high as about 93.5% and 97.9% (textbook estimates; for current figures rely on the latest research), with duplicated construction and local protection feeding each other. Four policies: exploit comparative and competitive advantage — upgrading cannot depart from the factor endowment, and technical accumulation must convert static comparative advantage into dynamic competitive advantage; pursue leapfrog development and the information industries, letting industrial upgrading pull structural adjustment — seize the window when technological trajectories change; guide the flow of foreign capital actively and rationally — aligning it with domestic upgrading rather than letting it crowd in parallel; and keep optimizing the allocation of state assets — concentrating state capital in basic, pillar and strategic industries, in line with the strategic adjustment of the state sector (Chapter 4). The unified logic of these policies: the protagonists of structural adjustment are the market and the enterprise, while government's role is to remove the obstacles to factor mobility (hukou, local protection, monopoly), supply public goods (infrastructure, basic research) and correct externalities (the environment) — structures 'adjusted' directly by government are precisely the source of false upgrading and convergence.

### The relation between technological innovation and the evolution of economic structure

Technical change does more than promote growth; it drives structural evolution. The micro mechanism lies in the relation of innovation to market structure: the degree of competition, firm size and monopoly power are the three great constraints on innovation — competition creates the necessity of innovation (the innovator gains extra profit in competition with rivals), firm size affects the market prospect innovation opens (the larger the size, the larger the market), and monopoly power decides its durability (the higher the degree of monopoly, the harder to imitate). Two kinds of innovation motive result: monopoly-prospect-driven (innovating in expectation of monopoly profit) and competition-prospect-driven (innovating for fear of losing profit to imitation) — the coexistence of the two pressures shows that the market structure most favourable to innovation is neither perfect competition nor perfect monopoly but 'workable competition': enough market pressure, and enough expectation of return to innovation. The completing link of structural evolution is diffusion: the last stage of innovation is its spread, and the diffusion through the population of potential adopters affects a country's overall strength and per-capita income; for developing countries international diffusion (import, imitate, digest, re-innovate) matters more than domestic diffusion — this is exactly the channel through which the latecomer advantage is cashed. Five conditions govern the actual use of a new technology: the innovator sees the possibility of innovation, is confident the new result outperforms the old, is willing to bear the risk, has purchasing power (savings or credit) to finance it, and can obtain the needed materials at the right time and place — five conditions that reduce 'technology' to an 'economic process': without financing, without a supply chain, without risk-bearing capacity, no advanced technology can turn into structural change. The policy meaning is to build an ecology of progress rather than mount isolated assaults: make enterprises the subjects of innovation, let technology-leading firms and the development zones provide the carriers, let the technology market clear the diffusion channels, and let government lower the fixed costs of innovation through the policy environment and support for equipment renewal.

## Limitations and further work

Six coordinates mark the later evolution of this chapter's framework. First, **the debate over the Lewis turning point**: around 2004 the coastal 'migrant worker shortage' and the continuing rise of migrant wages set off the debate on whether China had reached the turning point — Cai Fang and others hold it was crossed sometime in 2004–2010, the demographic dividend turning negative; other research holds that agricultural surplus labour has not finished transferring and the turning point was overstated. The substance of the dispute is the definition of 'surplus' (marginal productivity, age structure, or institutional stranding), and it decides the direction of response: if the turn has come, rely on productivity and human capital; if surplus remains, on continued transfer and hukou reform. Second, **the deepening of hukou reform**: after the abolition of dual registration in 2014, the focus moved to the attached entitlements — the residence permit (2016), points-based settlement in megacities, and the equalization agenda of basic public services following the person; the migrant total runs at about 300 million (NBS monitoring basis; for the current figure rely on the latest release), and the cost-sharing of citizenization (central and local, enterprise and individual) remains a fiscal problem. Third, **the updated state of industrial structure**: the tertiary sector's share of value added has passed the secondary and gone over half (about 54% at present; for the current figure rely on the latest release), but the sliding share of manufacturing has triggered a policy swing — 'keeping the manufacturing share basically stable' entered the plans, and Made in China 2025 plus the new industrialization (intelligent, green, convergent) redefined the textbook-era 'new road of industrialization'; 'from the real to the virtual' is false upgrading in its contemporary form. Fourth, **the governance turn of regional convergence**: main functional zones (2010), Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei coordination, the Yangtze belt, the unified national market (2022) — the agenda moved 'anti-convergence' from administrative dissuasion to a unified factor market and functional division; the root of convergence is the institutional incentive of local government competition, and governance must aim at the incentive. Fifth, **the upgrade of technology policy**: the innovation-driven development strategy (2012), the new whole-nation system, the specialized-sophisticated-novel-innovative push, and the expansion of high-tech zones into self-innovation zones — technology policy turned from 'import and digest' to 'self-reliance coexisting with international competition'; the latecomer advantage still holds on the digital and green trajectories, but geopolitical technology controls have re-tightened the access constraint on the 'blueprint'. Sixth, **the contemporary echo of appropriate technology**: automation and AI displacing jobs raise again the question of the 'social assessment of technology' — the textbook's nine-aspect checklist (employment, remuneration, distribution, skills) is not dated in the era of algorithmic management; it is a ready framework for assessing digital technology.
