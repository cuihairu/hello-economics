# Chapter 7 · Socialist economic growth and economic development

> **The central question of this chapter**: where does growth differ from development? Why can an economy "grow fast" yet "grow badly"? In turning the growth mode from extensive to intensive, why does the key lie not in technology itself but in institutions? And how is development made sustainable?

## The outline of this chapter

This chapter starts from a pair of concepts that must be kept apart. Economic growth refers to the long-run increase in the real output (real income) of an economy, usually expressed as the growth rate of GNP, GDP or their per capita values — it answers "by how much has the cake grown"; economic development includes, besides the output content of growth, the changes in the technology and institutional arrangements on which the production and distribution of products depend, and the evolution of the economic structure they bring about — the changes in the production structure, the employment structure, the income distribution structure, the consumption structure and the demographic structure, the improvement of the people's material welfare and the raising of environmental quality — it answers "how did it grow, and what did society become after growing". The distinction is necessary because national accounting can see only the former: GDP (the monetary expression of the total of final products and services produced by the country's own and foreign residents within its territory) and GNP (the sum of all final products and services produced by a country's resident population) measure output at constant prices, yet stay silent on the state of distribution, the welfare composition of the products and services, and the environmental cost. The environmentally adjusted domestic product (EDP, that is, green GDP) proposed by the United Nations statistical bodies in 1993 — deducting from GDP the value of resource depletion and the cost of pollution treatment — was the first round of accounting response to this silence.

Around "how to turn a backward agricultural country into an industrial one", development economics once staged a classic methodological confrontation. The balanced growth strategy (the "big push", proposed by Nurkse and Rosenstein-Rodan) was built on the diagnosis of the "vicious circle of poverty": low income leads to low saving, low saving leads to low investment, and low investment locks in low income — a push by a single sector on its own finds no buyer, and the only way out is an investment plan for growth across the board, letting the industrial sectors develop simultaneously, serve as each other's demand, and make investment in every sector profitable. The unbalanced growth strategy (Hirschman) held instead that what developing countries lack most is decision-making capacity and resources, that investment should be concentrated in key sectors, and that the process of development should be a structural evolution in which different sectors grow one after another, exerting pressure and thrust on one another — balance created by imbalance itself. The period of planned economy in China in fact carried out a state-led, tilted, unbalanced advance, but it differed from both classic strategies: it allocated resources by directive planning rather than price signals, and a catch-up logic with high-speed growth as the main goal overwhelmed the internal rhythm of sectoral linkages. Thus took shape the five features of the traditional development model — catch-up type (priority to speed targets), administratively implemented type (direct mobilization of government power), tilted-economy type (heavy industry first), extensive type (reliance on the expansion of factor quantities), and closed type (self-reliance first). Growth mode and system are here cause and effect of each other: the system built on the micro-foundation of traditional ownership rejects the market mechanism and allocates resources by directive planning, while the planning system happens to be just what lets the government carry out the extensive growth mode effectively — to change the growth mode, the system must be changed first.

This is the theoretical pivot of the chapter: the transformation of the growth mode depends on the switch of the institutional model. Traditional growth theory (such as the Solow model) abstracts institutions away as exogenous variables and examines only how factor inputs and productivity determine growth; the new institutional economics (North) in turn treats institutions as the endogenous variable determining growth — institutions are a set of made rules, compliance procedures and norms of behavioral ethics and morality, meant to constrain the individual behavior of agents pursuing the maximization of welfare or utility, and they fall into three types: the constitutional order (the "rules for making rules" that define the basic structure of state property rights and control and establish principles for collective choice), institutional arrangements (the concrete operational rules under the constitutional order that constrain particular patterns of behavior and define the terms of exchange, including statute law, common law and voluntary contracts), and norms of behavioral ethics and morality (derived from ideology, endowing the other two with legitimacy). The system can push or block growth because it changes both income distribution and the efficiency of resource use: it sets formal and informal rules of behavior (constraints) for every agent, can arouse the motive for transaction and technological innovation by shaping new incentive mechanisms, can overcome opportunism and free riding with the help of ethical norms so that faster growth is bought at a lower cost, and it also affects the availability of information and resources. The turn from extensive to intensive (from reliance on the expansion of factor quantities to reliance on raising the efficiency of factor use) is marked by the contribution rate of technological progress, and technological innovation depends precisely on property-right incentives — when the property rights system is inefficient, the individual's ability to profit is both lacking and incomplete, and technological progress must lag. This framework was then applied at two levels: one, to reflect on the traditional development model and turn toward sustainable development; the other, to prepare the ground theoretically for the Scientific Outlook on Development. The outlook of sustainable development (whose landmark document is the World Commission on Environment and Development's *Our Common Future* of 1987) stresses the coordination of economy, society, resources and environmental protection, with sustainability, fairness and commonality as its basic principles — the material consumption of development must not exceed the carrying capacity of resources and the environment, the development of the present generation must not damage the development space of future generations, and all countries bear common but differentiated responsibilities in development affairs.

Finally it lands on China's own strategic expression. Deng Xiaoping's theory of economic development gave the stepped "three-step" development strategy (the first step, adequate food and clothing; the second, moderate prosperity; the third, reaching the level of moderately developed countries by the middle of the 21st century), with agriculture, energy and transport, and education and science as the strategic priorities; the Party Centre then put forward the strategy of rejuvenating the country through science and education, placing education in the position of priority development. Around 2003, in response to the imbalances in growth (urban and rural, regional, economic and social, humanity and nature, domestic and external), the Party put forward the Scientific Outlook on Development, which is people-centred, comprehensive, coordinated and sustainable — people-centredness is its essence and core, comprehensive, coordinated and sustainable development its basic content, and overall consideration (the five balances) its fundamental requirement. At this point the chapter has in effect completed a full logical chain: the outlook on development (what kind of development is wanted) determines the strategy (how many steps, and where the priorities lie), the strategy determines the system (what institutions can carry it out), and the system in turn locks in the growth mode (extensive or intensive) — so changing the growth mode is a systematic project running from the outlook on development all the way down to the institutions.

## Core concepts

### Economic growth

Economic growth refers to the long-run increase in the real output (or real income) of an economy, that is, the increase of full-employment output measured at constant price levels, usually gauged by the increase of GNP or of per capita GNP: aggregate growth describes the expansion of a country's overall productive capacity, and per capita growth indicates the improvement of material living standards.

Why separate it from development? Because growth is a necessary but not a sufficient condition of development — growth can leave the structure unchanged, the distribution unimproved and welfare unraised (when the benefits of growth concentrate on the few, when the output structure is out of joint with demand, and when the environmental cost exceeds the gain in output, the aggregate numbers and living standards run in opposite directions). Only once the two are separated can one speak of evaluating the "mode" of growth, and the whole analysis of this chapter rests on this distinction.

### Economic development

Economic development refers to the evolutionary process in which a country's or region's economic growth goes together with the continuous optimizing and upgrading of its economic structure and social structure; besides the growth of output, it includes the changes in the production structure, the employment structure, the income distribution structure, the consumption structure and the demographic structure, the improvement of the people's material welfare and the raising of environmental quality — a concept covering quantity as well as quality.

Why replace growth with a broader concept? Because the purpose of development is the improvement of the human condition, and the human condition is determined jointly by structure and environment. This concept is also the intellectual source of the later composite measures of development (the Human Development Index and the like): they all try to bring "what lies outside growth" into the measuring system — fairness of distribution, education and health, environmental quality — to correct the systematic undercounting or misreporting of development by the single GDP indicator.

### Green GDP

Green GDP (the environmentally adjusted domestic product, EDP) was proposed by the United Nations statistical bodies in 1993: on the basis of GDP, one deducts the value of the resources consumed in creating GDP, and then the cost of treating the pollution caused by the production of GDP.

Why invent a "discounted GDP"? Because standard accounting treats nature as a free input: deforestation is counted as output, soil erosion costs nothing, and mineral extraction books only the proceeds of extraction, not the depletion of the stock. Green GDP, by keeping accounts of the depletion of resources and the environment, turns "the quality of growth" into comparable numbers. Its practical difficulty is equally worth remembering — the methods of monetizing resource depletion and environmental degradation remain disputed to this day — which explains why it exists more as an accounting framework than as a formal assessment indicator.

### The economic growth mode and its transformation

The economic growth mode refers to the type of avenues and means by which economic growth is achieved; the transformation of the growth mode means turning from growth achieved mainly by the quantitative expansion of the factors of production to growth achieved mainly by raising the efficiency with which the input factors are used, that is, from the extensive type to the intensive type. The transformation is to a considerable degree evaluated and measured by the contribution rate of technological progress to economic growth, and technological innovation depends in turn to a considerable degree on institutional innovation.

Why is the key to the transformation institutions rather than technology? Because technological progress is itself a response to incentives: only when property rights are clearly defined and innovators can appropriate the returns to innovation are the motives for researching and adopting new technologies sufficient; when property rights are murky, enterprise budget constraints are soft, and factor prices are distorted (cheap resources, a free environment, soft financing), being extensive is precisely the rational choice of every party involved — without changing these institutional conditions, "intensive" stays a slogan.

### Sustainable development

Sustainable development refers to development that meets the needs of the present generation without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs; it stresses the coordinated development of economy, society, resources and environmental protection. Its core is development, but it requires that economic and social development be achieved on the premise of strictly controlling population, raising the quality of the population, protecting the environment and using resources sustainably. Its theoretical content contains three basic principles: the principle of sustainability (the total material consumption of human economic and social activity must not exceed the carrying capacity of natural resources and the ecological environment), the principle of fairness (fairness among contemporaries weighted equally with fairness across generations), and the principle of commonality (all countries bearing common responsibilities and obligations in development affairs, and cooperating on an equal footing).

Why could this definition become a global consensus? Because it accommodates two positions at once: developing countries insist that "the core is development" — poverty is itself unsustainable (over-exploitation for the sake of survival), and the right to development comes first; developed countries stress "do no harm to future generations" — growth must submit to ecological constraints. The three principles are the synthesis of the two positions: sustainability draws the ecological boundary of development, fairness confirms its legitimacy, and commonality leaves the allocation of responsibility as an enduring topic of international negotiation.

## Problems and analysis

### Why can per capita GDP or GNP not fully reflect a country's level of economic development?

Per capita output is an average, while development is multidimensional, and the mismatch appears in five places. First, per capita indicators say nothing about distribution: per capita GDP may grow fast, but the beneficiaries may be only a small part of the population, with no substantive improvement in the employment and income of the majority — the average hides the structure. Second, population growth may swallow growth: if the rate of population growth approaches or even exceeds the rate of output growth, little remains of the improvement in per capita terms, and population becomes an important variable obstructing development. Third, international comparability is flawed: overvalued official exchange rates, incomplete statistical materials, and the exclusion of a large mass of non-market products and services (household labour, subsistence production) all distort cross-country GDP figures. Fourth, the indicators do not reflect the composition of output or its welfare attributes: with the same per capita GDP, a country spending on education and health delivers a national welfare utterly different from one spending on armaments and luxury goods, and the negative effects of environmental pollution, urban congestion and population expansion are also outside the accounting. Fifth, the quality of life is multidimensional: indicators such as educational attainment and literacy, life expectancy, nutritional level and per capita energy consumption cannot be inferred from per capita GDP — two countries with a similar GDP may differ in life expectancy by more than a decade. Taken together, the policy meaning of these five points is: setting growth targets that judge success by GDP is itself a question of the outlook on development — bias in the measuring system converts automatically into bias in the development strategy, and this is exactly the target of the Scientific Outlook on Development discussed below.

### Why must the transformation of the economic growth mode depend on institutional innovation?

The first reason is the chain of transmission: the transformation is measured by the contribution rate of technological progress, and technological innovation depends on institutional innovation — when the property rights system is inefficient, the increase of the stock of knowledge and technology cannot be converted into the individual's ability to profit, with the result that technological progress lags, the returns to the resource stock diminish, and growth stays locked on the extensive track.

The second reason is the four channels through which the system affects choices. The system sets formal and informal rules of behavior for every agent, which amounts to fixing the constraint set for the pursuit of maximal benefit; when the constraints change, the choices change with them — institutional innovation creates new conditions for growth precisely by changing the rules of exchange. Institutional innovation shapes new incentive and motive mechanisms, arousing the agent's motive to take part in exchange and technological innovation; where a society lacks vitality or stagnates, one can generally trace it back to an established system that smothered the motive for innovation, or to innovative activity earning less than the minimum reward. When the ethical and moral norms of behavior help overcome opportunism and free riding, faster growth can be bought at lower cost — trust lowers transaction costs. The system also affects the availability of information and resources: the established institutions determine how information is distributed, transmitted and trusted, and hence determine the cost of information; the information costs pushed up by market distortion constrain growth directly.

The third reason lies in the particular pathology of planning: under the traditional system, being extensive was not only the result but also the means — appraisal by quantitative indicators encouraged investment expansion rather than efficiency gains, soft budget constraints made firms insensitive to costs, and distorted factor prices made "economizing" unprofitable. The move from extensive to intensive is therefore not something called into being by exhortation; it is the result of new constraint conditions (property rights, prices, competition, hard budgets) making intensive methods the self-interested choice of the parties — institutional innovation is the necessary and sufficient channel for the transformation of the growth mode.

## Comprehensive discussion

### On the mechanism by which institutional innovation drives economic growth

When institutions are treated as the most important variable determining growth, economic growth appears mainly as the result of institutional change. Institutions act on growth through three links.

**The property rights link.** The strength of the stimulus to economic growth is directly related to the structure of property rights. Efficient property rights should be competitive and exclusive — exclusivity reduces future uncertainty and narrows the space for opportunistic behavior. Unclear property rights limit growth through the increase of transaction costs: a transaction is in essence an exchange of property rights, and when the rights are ill-defined, agents cannot improve their position through exchange, and the motive to produce or exchange is weak — either the transaction never happens, or its scale shrinks; the direct consequence is higher transaction costs and lower economic efficiency, which in turn limit growth.

**The state link.** Defining and protecting property rights itself costs something, and the state possesses economies of scale in the "potential for violence", so that defining and protecting rights by the state can lower transaction costs. The state determines the structure of property rights, and it is therefore ultimately answerable for the efficiency of the property rights structure that produces growth, decline or stagnation. The state achieves two goals by providing society the two services of "protection" and "justice": first, to define the basic rules of competition and cooperation that form the property rights structure, so as to maximize the ruler's rent; second, to lower transaction costs so as to maximize the output of society, thereby increasing state tax revenue. The crux is that a lasting conflict lies between the two goals — the property rights structure that maximizes the ruler's rent is often inconsistent with an efficient system that lowers transaction costs and promotes growth. This conflict (the reappearance, in the context of growth, of the North paradox of Chapter 3) is an important root for understanding the intrinsic instability of a country's growth process: the tug between the ruler's short-run rent and society's long-run output explains why the establishment of efficient institutions has never depended on unidirectional natural evolution.

**The ideology link.** Ideology is made up of interrelated world views. A successful ideology must solve the problem of "riding free without paying" — it gives the various groups vitality and enables people to act contrary to simple, hedonistic and individualistic cost-benefit calculations, thereby saving transaction costs. Property rights, the state and ideology together form the complete framework of North-style growth analysis: property rights determine the incentive structure, the state determines the supply of property rights, and ideology determines the legitimacy basis of the order — the effect of institutional innovation on growth finally lands on the improvement of these three links.

### On the Scientific Outlook on Development

Background: the Scientific Outlook on Development was put forward by the Party on the basis of analysing the trends of world economic and political development, summing up the practical experience of development at home and abroad, and keeping in view the practice of building a moderately prosperous society in all respects. In the early 21st century, after China's per capita GDP passed one thousand dollars, the country entered a period of sharpening contradictions — the urban-rural and regional gaps widened, economic and social development ran on one long and one short leg, resource and environmental constraints tightened, and external dependence climbed — the cost of the traditional growth mode came into full view, and the outlook on development had to be upgraded.

Its content has three parts. Putting people first and promoting well-rounded human development is the essence and core: the human being is the seat of value, and human survival and development are the highest goal of value — development is not for GDP but for people, and taking human development as the yardstick for examining social development is a basic tenet of Marxism; putting people first is also a qualitative leap on the basis of sublating the outlooks of "people as the basis" and "things as the basis". Comprehensive, coordinated and sustainable development is the basic content: the three form an organic whole, embodying the unity of economic development, social development and well-rounded human development, the unity of economy and society with population, resources and environment, and the unity of material, political and spiritual civilization; sustainability requires measuring development by lasting long-run interests, and any so-called development that looks only to the present and discounts the future is not scientific or rational development. Overall consideration is the fundamental requirement: balancing urban and rural development (gradually changing the urban-rural dual structure), balancing regional development (benign interaction of east, centre and west, reversing the trend of widening regional gaps), balancing economic and social development (improving social security, attending to vulnerable groups, advancing social fairness), balancing the harmonious development of humanity and nature (taking the new road of industrialization high in technological content, good in economic returns, low in resource consumption, low in environmental pollution, and giving full play to the advantage in human resources), and balancing domestic development with opening to the outside world (using the two markets and the two kinds of resources, and taking part in international cooperation and competition over a wider range, in broader fields and at a higher level).

As a major innovation in the outlook on development, its significance lies in: providing the outlook with a value idea and pointing to its value goal (attending not only to people's survival, but more to their development on the basis of survival); expanding the scope of the outlook and perfecting its theoretical basis; and pointing to the road of realization — depending both on the autonomous and conscious social practice of the agents and on the guarantees, regulation and intervention of the state. The Scientific Outlook on Development thereby advanced "development is the hard truth" into "development must be scientific development", providing a unified coordinate of value for the transformation of the growth mode and the strategy of sustainable development that follow.

## Limitations and beyond

In the decade and more of policy and theoretical evolution that followed, this chapter's framework has five coordinates worth adding. First, **the further upgrading of the idea of development**: in 2015 the Fifth Plenary Session of the 18th Central Committee proposed the new development concepts of innovation, coordination, green, openness and sharing; in 2017 the 19th Congress made the major judgment that China's economy had moved from the stage of high-speed growth to the stage of high-quality development, condensing the demand of "both good and fast" into the proposition of "high quality"; afterwards supply-side structural reform, the new development pattern (with the domestic circulation as the main body and domestic and international circulations reinforcing each other) and new quality productive forces (proposed in 2023) came on stage in turn — the chain of transmission from outlook to strategy to system keeps running, and the textbook dichotomy (extensive/intensive) has been partly replaced by more operational indicators such as total factor productivity and input-output efficiency. Second, **the exploration of the measuring system**: the green GDP accounting pilots (started in 2004) ran aground for a time over valuation disputes, but the direction was carried on — the pilots of gross ecosystem product (GEP), the shift from dual control of energy consumption to dual control of carbon emissions under the "dual carbon" goals (peak carbon and carbon neutrality, proposed in 2020), and the dropping of GDP-only criteria in cadre appraisal in favour of environmental and livelihood indicators are all institutionalized responses to this chapter's judgment that "per capita GDP cannot reflect the level of development". Third, **the internationalization of sustainable development**: the UN Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) of 2000 and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) of 2015 turned the three principles of this chapter into a monitorable agenda of 17 items; China's ecological civilization was written into the Party constitution and the state constitution, and the proposition "lucid waters and lush mountains are invaluable assets" re-united sustainability with the right to development. Fourth, **the real-world magnitude of growth accounting**: estimates of the contribution rate of total factor productivity to growth since reform and opening differ from source to source, generally around one third (with considerable differences across institutions; the latest published figures prevail), still short of what intensive growth requires — the character of growth driven by investment and factor inputs has not fundamentally changed, and this is exactly the reality the new development concepts address. Fifth, **the theoretical debate remains open**: the controversy between the institutionalist view of growth (North, Acemoglu — "institutions are the fundamental cause") and the geographic/cultural/technological explanations (Sachs and others) continues; the applicability of the big-push strategy to contemporary Africa and South Asia, and the question of the sequence of "unbalanced–balanced", recur repeatedly in regional policy (the tension between the Western Development, the Rise of Central China and the Revitalization of the Northeast on one side and the unified national market on the other) — the theoretical tools of this chapter are still active on the front line of policy debate.
