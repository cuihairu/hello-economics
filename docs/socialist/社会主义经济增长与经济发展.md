# Chapter 7 · Socialist economic growth and economic development

> **The central question of this chapter**: where does the difference between growth and development lie? Why can an economy 'grow fast' yet 'grow badly'? Why, in turning the growth pattern from extensive to intensive, is the key not technology itself but institutions? And how can development be made sustainable?

## The arc of this chapter

The starting point of this chapter is a pair of concepts that must be kept apart. Economic growth refers to the long-run increase in the real output (real income) of an economy, usually expressed by the growth rate of GNP, GDP or their per capita values — it answers 'how much bigger has the cake become'. Economic development, besides containing the output content of growth, also includes the transformation of the technical and institutional arrangements on which production and distribution rest, and the evolution of economic structure that follows — changes in the structure of production, employment, income distribution, consumption and population, improvement in the people's material welfare and in environmental quality — it answers 'how did it grow, and what did society become after growing'. The distinction is necessary because national accounting itself sees only the former: GDP (the money value of the total final goods and services produced by domestic and foreign residents within a country's territory) and GNP (the total final goods and services produced by a country's resident population) measure output at constant prices, yet remain silent on distribution, on the welfare composition of goods and services, and on environmental costs. The proposal by the United Nations statistical bodies in 1993 of the environmentally adjusted domestic product (EDP, the 'green GDP') — GDP minus the value of resource depletion and the cost of pollution treatment — was precisely the first accounting response to that silence.

Around 'how to turn a backward agricultural country into an industrial one', development economics staged a classic methodological confrontation. The balanced growth strategy (the 'big push', advanced by Nurkse and Rosenstein-Rodan) rests on the judgment of the 'vicious circle of poverty': low income leads to low saving, low saving to low investment, low investment locks in low income — no single sector advancing alone can find a buyer, and the only way out is a comprehensive investment program of simultaneous growth, in which the industrial sectors develop together, supply demand to one another, and investment in every sector is profitable. The unbalanced growth strategy (Hirschman) held instead that what developing countries lack most is decision-making capacity and resources: investment should be concentrated in key sectors, and the process of development made a structural evolution in which sectors mature in succession, imposing pressures and pushes on one another — balance created by imbalance itself. China's planned-economy period in fact executed a state-led, tilted form of unbalanced advance, but it differed from both classic strategies: it allocated resources by directive plan rather than price signals, and the catch-up logic that took high-speed growth as the main goal overwhelmed the intrinsic rhythm of inter-sector linkage. Thus were formed the five features of the traditional development pattern — catch-up type (speed targets first), administratively implemented type (government power directly mobilized), tilted type (heavy industry first), extensive type (relying on quantitative expansion of inputs), closed type (self-reliance above all). Growth pattern and system are here cause and effect of each other: a system built on the traditional micro-foundation of ownership excludes the market mechanism, resources are allocated by directive plan, and the planned system in turn is exactly what lets the government effectively carry through the extensive growth pattern — to change the growth pattern, the system must be changed first.

Here is the theoretical pivot of this chapter: the transformation of the growth pattern depends on the transformation of the institutional pattern. Traditional growth theory (the Solow model, say) abstracts institutions away as exogenous and examines only how factor inputs and productivity determine growth; new institutional economics (North) turns this around and makes institutions the endogenous variable that determines growth — institutions are a set of rules, compliance procedures and behavioral ethical norms devised to constrain the behavior of individuals pursuing the maximization of their welfare or utility, and fall into three types: constitutional order (the 'rules for making rules' that define the basic structure of state property rights and control and set principles for collective choice), institutional arrangements (the concrete operating rules that constrain specific modes of behavior and define the conditions of exchange under the constitutional order, including statute, common law and voluntary contracts), and behavioral ethical norms (derived from ideology, lending legitimacy to the other two). That institutions can propel or obstruct growth lies in the fact that they change both income distribution and the efficiency of resource use: they set formal and informal rules of conduct for every actor (the constraint conditions); by shaping new incentive mechanisms they can arouse the motives for exchange and technological innovation; by enlisting ethical norms they can overcome opportunism and free-riding and purchase faster growth at lower cost; and they also affect the availability of information and resources. The shift from extensive to intensive (from relying on the quantitative expansion of inputs to relying on the rising efficiency of input use) is marked by the contribution rate of technical progress, and technological innovation depends precisely on property-rights incentives — when the property-rights system is inefficient, the individual's capacity to profit is both lacking and imperfect, and technical progress is bound to be slow. This framework is then applied at two levels: rethinking the traditional development pattern and turning to sustainable development, and preparing the theory of the scientific outlook on development. The view of sustainable development (with the World Commission on Environment and Development's 1987 *Our Common Future* as the landmark document) stresses the coordination of economy, society, resources and environmental protection, with sustainability, fairness and commonality as its basic principles — the material consumption of development must not exceed the carrying capacity of resources and environment, the development of the present generation must not harm the development space of future generations, and all countries bear common but differentiated responsibilities in the affairs of development.

Finally, this comes down to China's own strategic expression. Deng Xiaoping's theory of economic development gave the three-step stair-case development strategy (the first step food and clothing, the second step moderate prosperity, the third step reaching the level of the moderately developed countries by the mid-twenty-first century), with agriculture, energy and transport, education and science as the strategic priorities; the Party center later advanced the strategy of rejuvenating the country through science and education, putting education in a position of priority. Around 2003, in response to the imbalances in growth (urban-rural, regional, economic versus social, humanity versus nature, domestic versus external), the Party put forward the scientific outlook on development — people-oriented, comprehensive, coordinated and sustainable: being people-oriented is its essence and core, comprehensive coordination and sustainability its basic content, overall consideration (the five overalls) its fundamental requirement. With this, the chapter has in fact completed a full logical chain: the view of development (what kind of development is wanted) determines the strategy (how many steps, where the priorities are), the strategy determines the system (what institutions can carry it through), and the system in turn locks in the growth pattern (extensive or intensive) — changing the growth pattern is therefore a systematic project running from the view of development to institutions.

## Core concepts

### Economic growth

Economic growth refers to the long-run increase in the real output (or real income) of an economy — the increase in full-employment output measured at constant prices — usually gauged by the increase in GNP or per capita GNP: aggregate growth describes the expansion of a country's overall productive capacity, per capita growth the improvement of the material standard of living.

Why separate it from development? Because growth is a necessary but not a sufficient condition of development — growth can leave the structure unchanged, the distribution unimproved, welfare unraised (when the benefits of growth are concentrated in a few hands, when the output structure is decoupled from demand, when the environmental cost exceeds the output gain, the aggregate figure and the standard of living move in opposite directions). Only by keeping the two apart can one evaluate the 'pattern' of growth; the whole analysis of this chapter rests on this distinction.

### Economic development

Economic development refers to the evolving process in which a country or region experiences economic growth together with the continual optimization and upgrading of the economic and social structure; besides output growth it includes changes in the structure of production, employment, income distribution, consumption and population, improvement in the people's material welfare and in environmental quality — a concept that includes quality as well as quantity.

Why replace growth with a wider concept? Because the purpose of development is the improvement of the human condition, and the human condition is jointly determined by structure and environment. This concept is also the intellectual source of the later composite development measures (the Human Development Index among them): all of them try to put 'what lies beyond growth' into the measuring system — distributive fairness, education and health, environmental quality — to correct the systematic underestimation or misreporting of development by the single GDP indicator.

### Green GDP

Green GDP (the environmentally adjusted domestic product, EDP) was proposed by the United Nations statistical bodies in 1993: starting from GDP, subtract the value of the resources consumed in creating GDP, and then subtract the cost of treating the pollution generated in producing GDP.

Why invent a 'discounted GDP'? Because standard accounting treats nature as a free input: logging counts as output, soil erosion costs nothing, mineral extraction counts the gains of extraction but not the depletion of the stock. By putting resource and environmental losses on the books, green GDP turns 'the quality of growth' into a comparable number. Its practical difficulties are equally worth remembering — the methods of monetizing resource depletion and environmental degradation remain disputed to this day — which explains why it exists more as an accounting framework than as a formal performance indicator.

### The pattern of economic growth and its transformation

The pattern of economic growth refers to the type of path and means by which growth is realized; the transformation of the pattern means shifting from relying mainly on the quantitative expansion of the factors of production to realizing growth mainly by raising the efficiency with which the factors put into production are used — that is, the shift from the extensive to the intensive. The mark of the shift is to a considerable extent evaluated and measured by the contribution rate of technical progress to growth, and technological innovation in turn depends to a considerable extent on institutional innovation.

Why is the key to the transformation institutions rather than technology? Because technical progress is itself a response to incentives: only when property rights are clearly defined and innovators can appropriate the returns of innovation is the motive to research and adopt new technology sufficient; when property rights are blurred, enterprise budgets are soft, and factor prices are distorted (resources underpriced, environment free, capital soft-constrained), the extensive pattern is precisely the rational choice of every party concerned — without changing these institutional conditions, 'intensive' remains a slogan.

### Sustainable development

Sustainable development refers to development that meets the needs of the present generation without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs, stressing the coordinated development of economy, society, resources and environmental protection; its core is development, but it requires that economic and social development be achieved under the premises of strictly controlling population, raising the quality of the population, protecting the environment, and the perpetual use of resources. Its theoretical content contains three basic principles: sustainability (the total material throughput of human economic and social activity must not exceed the carrying capacity of natural resources and the ecological environment), fairness (fairness within the present generation and fairness between generations given equal weight), and commonality (all countries bear common responsibilities and obligations in the affairs of development and cooperate as equals).

Why could this definition become a global consensus? Because it accommodates both positions at once: the developing countries insist that 'the core is development' — poverty is itself unsustainable (over-exploitation for survival) and the right to development comes first; the developed countries stress 'do no harm to future generations' — growth must submit to ecological constraints. The three principles are a synthesis of the two positions: sustainability draws the ecological boundary for development, fairness confirms the legitimacy of development, and commonality leaves the allocation of responsibility as the enduring topic of international negotiation.

## Questions and analysis

### Why can per capita GDP or GNP not fully reflect a country's level of economic development?

Per capita output is an average, and development is multidimensional; the misalignment appears in five places. First, per capita indicators do not reflect distribution: per capita GDP may grow fast while the beneficiaries are only a small minority and the employment and income of the majority see no substantive improvement — the average conceals the structure. Second, population growth may swallow growth: if the population growth rate approaches or exceeds the output growth rate, little improvement in per capita terms remains, and population becomes an important variable obstructing development. Third, international comparability is flawed: overvalued official exchange rates, incomplete statistics, and large volumes of non-market goods and services (household labor, production for self-consumption) left uncounted all distort cross-country GDP figures. Fourth, the indicators do not reflect the composition and welfare attributes of output: the same per capita GDP corresponds to utterly different national welfare when education and health take the share than when armaments and luxuries do; the negative effects of environmental pollution, urban congestion and population expansion are likewise outside the accounts. Fifth, quality of life is multidimensional: education and literacy, life expectancy, nutrition, per capita energy consumption — none of these can be derived from per capita GDP — countries with similar GDP may differ in life expectancy by more than a decade. The policy import of the five points taken together: setting growth targets that judge performance by GDP is itself a question of the view of development — bias in the measuring system translates automatically into bias in the development strategy, and this is exactly the target the scientific outlook on development later takes aim at.

### Why must the transformation of the economic growth pattern depend on institutional innovation?

The first reason is the chain of transmission: the transformation is measured by the contribution rate of technical progress, and technological innovation depends on institutional innovation — when the property-rights system is inefficient, an increase in the stock of knowledge and technology cannot be converted into the individual's capacity to profit; the result is slow technical progress, diminishing returns to the stock of resources, and growth locked onto the extensive track.

The second reason lies in the four channels through which the system affects choice behavior. The system sets formal and informal rules of conduct for every actor, which is to say it fixes the constraint conditions under which the pursuit of maximum benefit proceeds; when the constraints change, choice behavior changes with them — institutional innovation creates new conditions for growth precisely by changing the rules of exchange. Institutional innovation shapes new incentive and motive mechanisms, arousing the actors' motives to participate in exchange and in technological innovation; when a society lacks vitality or stagnates, one can almost always trace it back to an established system that has suffocated the motive to innovate, or to innovation that cannot earn even a minimum return. When the ethical norms of conduct help overcome opportunism and free-riding, they can purchase faster growth at lower cost — trust lowers transaction costs. The system also affects the availability of information and resources: the established institution determines the distribution, transmission and credibility of information, and hence the cost of information; information costs pushed up by market distortion directly constrain growth.

The third reason lies in the specific pathology of planning: under the traditional system the extensive pattern was not only a result but an instrument — quantitative-target assessment encouraged investment expansion rather than efficiency improvement, the soft budget constraint made enterprises insensitive to cost, and factor-price distortion made 'economizing' unprofitable. The shift from extensive to intensive is therefore not achieved by exhortation: it is the new constraint conditions (property rights, prices, competition, hard budgets) that make intensification the outcome of the self-interested choices of the parties concerned — institutional innovation is the necessary and sufficient channel of the transformation of the growth pattern.

## Synthetic essays

### Expound the mechanism by which institutional innovation propels economic growth

When institutions are taken as the most important variable determining growth, economic growth appears mainly as the outcome of institutional change. Institutions act on growth through three links.

**The property-rights link.** The magnitude of the impetus to growth is directly related to the structure of property rights. Efficient property rights should be competitive and exclusive — exclusivity reduces future uncertainty and compresses the space for opportunistic behavior. Unclear property rights restrict growth through rising transaction costs: exchange is in essence an exchange of property rights; when rights are ill defined, actors cannot better their position through exchange and the motive to produce or to trade is weak — either the transaction never happens at all, or its scale shrinks; the direct consequence is rising transaction costs and falling economic efficiency, which in turn restricts growth.

**The state link.** Defining and protecting property rights itself costs resources, and the state enjoys economies of scale in 'violence potential'; letting the state define and protect property rights can lower transaction costs. The state decides the structure of property rights and must therefore bear final responsibility for the efficiency of the property-rights structure that causes growth, decline or stagnation. The state provides society with two services, 'protection' and 'justice', to realize two goals: one is to define the basic rules of competition and cooperation that form the structure of property rights so as to maximize the ruler's rent; the other is to lower transaction costs so as to maximize social output and thereby increase the state's tax revenue. The crux is that a durable conflict exists between the two goals — the property-rights structure that maximizes the ruler's rent is often inconsistent with the efficient system that lowers transaction costs and promotes growth. This conflict (the reappearance, in the context of growth, of North's paradox of Chapter 3) is an important root of the intrinsic instability of a country's growth process: the tug-of-war between the ruler's short-term rent and society's long-term output explains why the establishment of efficient institutions never comes from one-directional natural evolution.

**The ideology link.** Ideology is composed of interrelated world views. A successful ideology must solve the problem of 'free-riding without paying' — it gives vitality to the various groups and enables people to act against simple, hedonistic and individualistic cost-benefit calculation, thereby economizing on transaction costs. Property rights, the state and ideology together form the complete framework of Northian growth analysis: property rights determine the structure of incentives, the state determines the supply of property rights, ideology determines the foundation of the legitimacy of order — the effect of institutional innovation on growth ultimately falls on the improvement of these three links.

### On the scientific outlook on development

Background: the scientific outlook on development was advanced by the Party in analyzing the trends of the world economy and politics, summing up domestic and foreign experience of development practice, and with an eye on the practice of building a moderately prosperous society in all respects. At the start of the twenty-first century, after China's per capita GDP passed one thousand US dollars, the country entered a period of acute contradictions — widening urban-rural and regional gaps, economic development running ahead of social development like a longer leg before a shorter one, tightening resource and environmental constraints, rising external dependence — the costs of the traditional growth pattern took concentrated visible shape, and the view of development had to be upgraded.

The content has three layers. Being people-oriented and promoting the all-round development of the person is the essence and core: taking the person as the standard of value, making human survival and development the highest value goal — development is for the sake of people, not of GDP, and measuring social development by the development of the human being is a basic Marxist viewpoint; being people-oriented is also a qualitative leap made on the basis of sublating both the 'people-as-root' and the 'things-as-root' views of development. Comprehensive, coordinated and sustainable development is the basic content: the three form an organic whole, expressing the unity of economic development, social development and the all-round development of the person, the unity of the economy and society with population, resources and environment, and the unity of material, political and spiritual civilization; sustainability requires measuring development by sustained long-run interests, and any so-called development that attends only to the present and never to the future is neither scientific nor rational. Overall consideration is the fundamental requirement: coordinating urban and rural development (gradually changing the urban-rural dual structure), coordinating development among regions (positive interaction of the eastern, central and western regions, reversing the widening of regional gaps), coordinating economic and social development (improving social security, attending to vulnerable groups, enhancing social fairness), coordinating the harmonious development of humanity and nature (taking the new road of industrialization with high scientific and technological content, good economic returns, low resource consumption, little environmental pollution and full play given to the advantage of human resources), and coordinating domestic development with opening to the outside world (using the two markets and two kinds of resources, participating in international cooperation and competition over a wider range, in broader fields and at higher levels).

As a major innovation in the view of development, its significance is: it supplies the value concept for the view of development and points to its value goal (attending not only to human survival but, on that basis, to human development); it widens the scope of the view of development and perfects its theoretical foundation; and it points out the path of realization — depending both on the self-conscious social practice of the subjects and on the guarantee, regulation and intervention of the state. The scientific outlook of development thereby advanced 'development is the absolute principle' into 'development must be scientific development', providing the unified value coordinate for the later transformation of the growth pattern and the strategy of sustainable development.

## Limits and what follows

In the decade and more of policy and theoretical evolution since, five coordinates deserve note. First, **the further upgrading of the development concept**: in 2015 the Fifth Plenary Session of the Eighteenth Central Committee put forward the new development concepts of innovation, coordination, green development, opening up and sharing; in 2017 the Nineteenth Congress made the major judgment that China's economy has shifted from the stage of high-speed growth to the stage of high-quality development, and the demand for 'both fast and good' condensed into the thesis of 'high quality'; since then supply-side structural reform, the new development pattern (domestic circulation as the mainstay with domestic and international circulations reinforcing each other) and new quality productive forces (put forward in 2023) have come on stage in turn — the transmission chain of view of development — strategy — system continues, and the textbook dichotomy (extensive/intensive) has been partly replaced by more operational indicators such as total factor productivity and input-output efficiency. Second, **the exploration of measuring systems**: the green GDP accounting pilot (launched in 2004) once ran aground over valuation disputes, but the direction was inherited — the gross ecosystem product (GEP) pilots, the shift from dual control of energy consumption to dual control of carbon emissions under the 'dual carbon' goals (carbon peaking and carbon neutrality, put forward in 2020), and the removal of GDP-only assessment of cadres with environmental and livelihood indicators brought in, are all institutional responses to this chapter's judgment that 'per capita GDP cannot reflect the level of development'. Third, **the internationalization of sustainable development**: the UN's Millennium Development Goals of 2000 and Sustainable Development Goals of 2015 turned the three principles of this chapter into 17 monitorable items of agenda; China's ecological civilization has been written into the Party constitution and the state constitution, and the proposition that 'lucid waters and lush mountains are invaluable assets' reunites sustainability with the right to development. Fourth, **the real magnitudes of growth accounting**: estimates of total factor productivity's contribution to growth since reform and opening up vary by institution, generally around one third (differences across estimates are large; the latest published figures prevail), still short of the requirement of intensive growth — the features of investment- and input-driven growth have not fundamentally changed, which is precisely the reality the new development concepts take aim at. Fifth, **unresolved theoretical disputes**: the debate between the institutionalist view of growth (North; Acemoglu's 'institutions as fundamental cause') and geographic, cultural or technological explanations (Sachs among others) continues; the applicability of the big-push strategy to contemporary Africa and South Asia, and the sequencing question of 'unbalanced to balanced', recur again and again in regional policy (the tension between the western development strategy, the rise of central China, the revitalization of the Northeast and the unified national market) — the theoretical instruments of this chapter are still active on the front line of policy debate.
