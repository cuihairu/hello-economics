# Chapter 2 · The theory of the socialist market economy

> **The central question of this chapter**: can the market mechanism enter a socialist system? Are plan and market two opposed institutional attributes, or two instruments that can be combined at will? In 'socialist market economy', what does each of the two words constrain?

## The arc of this chapter

Whether socialism needs the market was one of the great debates of twentieth-century economics, and the theory of this chapter was born on the extension of that debate. Its academic source was the socialist calculation debate that began in 1920: Mises asserted that without a market for the means of production and real prices, rational economic calculation is impossible; Lange replied in 1936 with 'trial-and-error market simulation' — a central planning board can simulate the Walrasian auctioneer and adjust prices by trial and error; Hayek struck back in 1945, pointing out that the problem is not whether static equilibrium can be computed but that dispersed, tacit knowledge simply cannot be centralized — information and incentives are the crux. The debate was unresolved on paper but settled in history: decades of operation of the Soviet-type planned system demonstrated the information and incentive problems Hayek had pointed out, over and over, in the form of shortage, waste and inefficiency. The directive planned system that China initially established by the late 1950s, characterized by a high degree of state centralization, had the same pathologies as the Soviet and East European ones: in the decision structure, centralized control; in the incentive structure, neglect of material interests and egalitarian distribution; in the information structure, vertical transmission of plan directives with enterprise behaviour barely constrained by market signals; in the ownership structure, the pursuit of 'the bigger and more public, the better'. Its operating features were cut from the same cloth: enterprises coped with momentary supply-demand mismatches by forced substitution (input-mix changes induced by shortage) and by letting output composition follow whatever inputs were available; top-down quantity drives meant that enterprise demand for scarce resources was never satisfied and hoarding of inputs was the rule; since enterprises bore no responsibility for investment decisions, an 'investment hunger' and cyclical fluctuation of investment followed. Severe bureaucratism, weak incentives, a rigid economic structure, chronic shortage, low worker morale, low economic efficiency — these ills were plain to all and constituted the direct case for reform.

But 'the plan does not work' does not automatically mean 'the market works'; the confirmation of the latter proposition went through four stages in the history of Chinese understanding. 1978–1983: the plan as primary and market regulation as supplementary — the market obtained a legitimate marginal position. 1984–1987: the Third Plenary Session of the Twelfth Central Committee advanced the theory of the planned commodity economy — conceding that the commodity economy is a stage socialist economics cannot skip past, and that the planned economy and the commodity economy are unified. 1987–1992: the Thirteenth Congress advanced the theory of the socialist commodity economy — the roles of plan and market both cover the whole of society, and the operating model is 'the state regulates the market, the market guides enterprises'. After 1992: Deng Xiaoping's southern tour talks made explicit that plan and market are both economic instruments and not the essential difference between socialism and capitalism; the Fourteenth Congress formally established the reform goal of building a socialist market economy system; the Third Plenary Session of the Fourteenth Central Committee unfolded its institutional framework; the Fifteenth and Sixteenth Congresses confirmed it in turn, up to the Third Plenary Session of the Eighteenth Central Committee in 2013, which assigned the market a decisive role in resource allocation. The substance of this cognitive curve is the step-by-step promotion of the market from 'tolerated auxiliary instrument' to 'basic and eventually decisive mechanism of resource allocation', with every step of the promotion conditioned on 'the basic socialist system does not change' — exactly the division of labour between the two qualifiers in 'socialist market economy': the market economy answers the mode of allocation, socialism answers the institutional base tone of ownership and distribution. The concepts of this chapter supply five props — market, market economy, market mechanism, incremental reform and gradual reform — and the synthetic essay unfolds the features of the system and the tasks of building it.

## Core concepts

### Market and market economy

The market is the place, channel and nexus of exchange, the necessary product of the social division of labour and the development of the commodity economy; through information feedback the market directly influences what people produce, how much they produce, when goods come to market and how sales go, linking production, supply and sales and realizing the economic interests of each party. The market economy is an economy in which resources are allocated by the market mechanism.

Why distinguish the two words? Because the traditional view equated 'market economy' with the capitalist system, and precisely this conceptual cleanup is the point of this section: the market economy is the product of socialized production and the development of the commodity economy; it is a mechanism for allocating resources, not a marker distinguishing social systems — socialism, too, can have a market economy. The market economy has four basic features. Resource allocation follows the property-right rule, that is, the power to allocate resources is proportional to the assets the agent holds, and the establishment of exclusive property rights is the necessary condition for the market mechanism to coordinate micro decisions effectively. Decentralized decision-making: households and enterprises, as independent economic entities, decide independently what to produce, how much and how, by comparing cost and benefit, and bear the corresponding consequences. Free and equal competition: as a contract economy, the status and opportunities of transacting agents are equal before the law, and all participate freely in competition under the norms of equal opportunity and fair dealing. Prices coordinate micro decisions: as signals revealing the scarcity of resources, prices are determined by supply and demand and automatically coordinate dispersed decisions — here price is taken broadly, covering product prices and the factor prices such as interest and wage rates.

### Market mechanism

The market mechanism is an organic system of economic operation and regulation formed in market trading relations, resting on the trinity of price, supply-demand and competition, and including the supply-demand mechanism, the price mechanism, the competition mechanism and the risk mechanism; these mechanisms constrain and act on one another, unified within the whole of the market mechanism. Economics calls it the 'invisible hand' — the free choice of everyone driven by self-interest forms a natural order that leads people to achieve social ends that were never their original intention.

Why can the market mechanism coordinate hundreds of millions of dispersed decisions? The answer lies in the distributed processing of information: no central agency needs to know every consumer's preferences and every producer's costs; price movements have compressed 'what is scarce' into a single number, and each party needs only to respond to the local price signal. The failure of the planned system lies exactly there — it requires dispersed knowledge to be centralized into one centre, and the centralizing process both loses information and distorts incentives. This also explains why 'having the planning agency simulate the market' (the Lange scheme) could hardly work in practice: the procedure of price adjustment can be simulated, but dispersed knowledge and hard budget constraints cannot.

### Incremental reform

Incremental reform means advancing market-oriented reform at the margin without first disturbing the existing pattern of vested interests — choosing, at margins where hierarchy rules act weakly, adjustments with Pareto-improving properties to transform the system, and gradually transiting toward the market economy system.

Why could reform start from the increment? Because behind the stock of assets stand the existing structure of power and pattern of interests, and a frontal assault meets enormous resistance and risk; the increment (new enterprises, new products, new prices, new employment under the new system) grows outside the old system, its beneficiaries growing in number as the increment grows, so the vested interest behind reform turns from a minority into a majority. The dual-track price system, the special-zone experiments, and the rise of township enterprises and the private economy are all concrete forms of incremental reform. Its cost is equally clear: the coexistence of two tracks opens room for arbitrage and corruption, and Chapter 3 devotes itself to the negative effects of gradual reform.

### Gradual reform

Gradual reform is marketization carried out on the basis of industrialization and the socialist constitutional order, emphasizing the use of existing organizational resources to advance reform, and implementing incremental reform without basically disturbing the existing pattern of interests. On this basis China chose a top-down path of gradual reform, transiting step by step from the highly centralized planned economy to a market economy.

Why could China only choose gradualism? Because reform proceeded under the constraint of the socialist constitutional order — the goal of reform was to build a socialist market economy system, not to overturn the basic system, which ruled out the 'raze and rebuild' shock therapy. The three operational features of gradualism (using existing organizational resources, incremental reform, experimenting before generalizing) and the reasons for its success are unfolded in Chapter 3.

## Problems and analysis

### Why did the traditional planned system necessarily bring shortage and inefficiency?

The pathologies of the system lie in four structures. In the decision structure, centralized control: what to produce, how much and how were all set by the plan. In the incentive structure, neglect of material incentives and egalitarian distribution. In the information structure, vertical transmission of plan directives, with enterprise behaviour barely constrained by market signals. In the ownership structure, the pursuit of 'the bigger and more public, the better' and a 'transition in poverty'. These four structures lock together and lock the economy into a low-efficiency equilibrium: without real prices, scarcity cannot show itself; without material incentives, effort cannot be mobilized; without horizontal information, supply and demand cannot be matched.

At the enterprise level the micro behaviour makes the problem concrete. Facing momentary supply-demand mismatches, the enterprise can only: adapt output to the currently scarcest resource; practise forced substitution — changes of the input mix induced by shortage; and let output composition follow the inputs it can obtain — 'make whatever the materials at hand allow', not 'find materials for whatever product is needed'. Driven by high-growth targets, a strong quantity drive runs from top to bottom: to fulfil output-value quotas, enterprise demand for scarce resources is never satisfied, and the enterprise tries to hoard as many inputs as possible, just in case. Because the enterprise bears no responsibility for investment decisions, investment demand greatly exceeds the investable funds, producing 'investment tension' — the tension of investment plans, of investment-goods supply and the sense of shortage becomes a major driver of the enterprise's expansion drive and investment hunger, further producing cyclical fluctuation of investment. Forced substitution, queuing, waiting, quotas and ration coupons thus became the everyday scenery of the shortage economy.

The economist's version of this diagnosis comes from Kornai's *Economics of Shortage* and its analysis of the 'soft budget constraint': knowing that losses will be covered by someone, the enterprise has no reason to economize on resources — shortage is not an accidental misalignment but the necessary product of a soft-budget-constraint system. This diagnosis is also the logical starting point of reform: to eliminate shortage, the budget constraint must be hardened; and hardening it presupposes that the enterprise becomes a subject responsible for its own profits and losses — from which the property-right reform agenda of Chapter 4 follows naturally.

### Why exactly these four features of the market economy?

The four features form an interdependent whole. The property-right rule is the premise: only when exclusive property rights are established does exchange mean anything and do prices carry real opportunity costs — this answers 'who has the right to allocate resources'. Decentralized decision-making is the structure: allocative power is dispersed to millions of households and enterprises, each maximizing its own interest under given conditions and bearing the consequences — this answers 'who decides'. Free and equal competition is the rule: status and opportunity are equal before the law, freedom of consumption choice, of entry, of employment and of transaction is guaranteed, and only then can competition select the better — this answers 'by what rules the game is played'. Price coordination is the mechanism: broad prices (product prices, interest rates, wage rates) serve as scarcity signals determined by supply and demand and automatically coordinate dispersed decisions — this answers 'how the parts are linked'.

The joint opposite of the four is exactly the four features of the planned system: the property-right rule versus administrative subordination (the hierarchy rule), decentralized decision versus central concentration, equal competition versus vertical rationing, price coordination versus directive transmission. Hence 'building a socialist market economy system' was never merely 'freeing prices' but the simultaneous rebuilding of these four structures — the market system, market subjects (enterprise property-right reform) and the mode of macro control must go together, which is exactly the frame of the 'how to build' part of this chapter.

### Why did gradual reform succeed in China?

First, combining the basic socialist economic institutions with the market economy: vigorously developing the non-public economy under the condition of public ownership as the mainstay, and adopting multiple modes of distribution under the condition of distribution according to labour as the mainstay — advancing marketization steadily while adapting the socialist system to the current level of productive forces. This answers 'what to reform and what not to': what changes is the mode of allocation and the micro institutions; what stands untouched is the framework of basic institutions, so that reform always had a clear political boundary and a stable expectation.

Second, judging the results of reform by the criterion of the 'three favourables': whether it favours the development of socialist productive forces, the comprehensive national strength of the socialist state, and the living standards of the people. This answers 'how to judge': putting the standard on results rather than form, every disputed measure (special zones, wage labour, the stock market) could be adjudicated by observable performance, avoiding ideological idling.

Third, handling the relation among reform, development and stability: actively advancing the transition from the planned to the market economy system amid stability and development. Reform entails adjustment costs; unemployment, prices and regional gaps are all fuses that could go off; unifying the intensity of reform, the speed of development and the degree society can bear is the core management technique that distinguishes the gradual path from shock therapy.

Fourth, as economic reform deepened, advancing political-structure reform in good time — including the transformation of government functions and the development of democracy and the rule of law. Economic marketization requires the government to exit micro management and turn to the supply of rules and macro control; the corresponding adjustment of the administrative system and government agencies is the condition for marketization not to be reversed by administrative inertia.

## Synthetic essay

### The main stages of China's understanding of the plan-market relation

The first stage (1978–1983): the plan as primary, market regulation as supplementary. After the Third Plenary Session of the Eleventh Central Committee, more and more of the profession held that decentralization and profit-sharing should proceed and that planning should be built on the law of value; the Twelfth Congress formally established 'the planned economy as primary, market regulation as supplementary' as the guiding thought of structural reform. The market's legal identity went from none to some, but was still confined to the 'supplementary' position.

The second stage (1984–1987): the theory of the planned commodity economy. The Third Plenary Session of the Twelfth Central Committee adopted the *Decision of the CPC Central Committee on Economic Structural Reform*, proposing that the commodity economy is a stage socialist economics cannot skip, that the planned economy and the commodity economy are unified, and that plan and market combine as blocks. The term 'commodity economy' acquired socialist citizenship — a big step forward; but the qualifier 'planned' shows that the market was not yet established as the general mode of resource allocation.

The third stage (1987–1992): the theory of the socialist commodity economy and the design of the operating model. The Thirteenth Congress proposed that the socialist commodity economy should be a system in which plan and market are internally unified, that the roles of plan and market both cover the whole of society, with planned regulation and market regulation organically combined; the target of reform was the operating model of 'the state regulates the market, the market guides enterprises'. The discussion advanced from 'whether the market is allowed to exist' to 'how state, market and enterprises form a new operating structure'.

The fourth stage (after 1992): the theory of the socialist market economy. In his southern tour talks Deng Xiaoping made explicit that more plan or more market is not the essential difference between socialism and capitalism, and that plan and market are both economic instruments; the Fourteenth Congress formally established the goal of building a socialist market economy system; the Third Plenary Session of the Fourteenth Central Committee adopted the *Decision of the CPC Central Committee on Several Issues Concerning the Establishment of a Socialist Market Economy System*, systematically expounding the basic framework and strategic plan; the Fifteenth Congress confirmed that 'to build the economy of socialism with Chinese characteristics is to develop the market economy under socialist conditions and continuously liberate and develop the productive forces'; the Sixteenth Congress reaffirmed the direction of socialist market economy reform and the market's basic role in resource allocation under state macro control; the Third Plenary Session of the Eighteenth Central Committee went further, assigning the market a decisive role in resource allocation together with a better role for the government.

The progressive logic of the four stages deserves note: the market moved from 'permitted supplement' (primary and supplementary) to 'component within the system' (the planned commodity economy), to 'mechanism covering the whole of society' (internal unity), and then to 'basic—decisive role' (the market economy system) — each promotion answered contradictions exposed in the practice of the previous stage, and each was accompanied by fierce debate. Behind the cognitive curve is a shift of standard: the anchor of judgement moved from 'the ideological attribute of plan and market' to the performance standard of the 'three favourables'.

### The basic features of the socialist market economy system

**(1) The ownership structure: one of the meeting points of market economy and socialism**. Public ownership as the mainstay with multiple forms of ownership developing together constitutes the ownership base of the socialist market economy. Holding to this structure requires two unwaverings: unswervingly consolidating and developing the public economy — the state sector controls the lifelines of the national economy and is key to displaying the superiority of the socialist system and strengthening economic power, while the collective economy has an important role in eliminating polarization and achieving common prosperity; and unswervingly encouraging, supporting and guiding the development of the non-public economy — the individual, private and foreign-funded economies contribute importantly to accumulating funds, expanding employment, adding tax revenue, meeting diversified needs and accelerating the development of productive forces. The public and non-public economies are unified in the process of socialist modernization; they promote and interpenetrate each other rather than being split off and opposed.

**(2) The distribution system: the mode of distribution of the market economy**. Distribution according to labour as the mainstay with multiple modes coexisting is the mode of distribution of the socialist market economy. At the primary stage of socialism the conditions for the pure realization of the distribution-according-to-labour principle Marx envisaged do not exist: an ownership structure with public ownership as the mainstay and multiple ownership forms developing together decides that distribution according to labour must be the mainstay with multiple modes coexisting; the principle of distribution according to labour can only be realized through market trading relations; and even public assets must, under market economy conditions, be used with compensation — enterprises occupying public assets must operate independently and be responsible for their own profits and losses, and the price of using them (interest, rent, etc.) should reflect resource scarcity, thereby more or less affecting the process and form in which distribution according to labour is realized. The distribution question is unfolded in Chapter 6.

Together the two features show the double qualification the word 'socialist' imposes on the market economy: in ownership, the property base of market operation has public ownership as its mainstay; in distribution, the market-determined pattern takes distribution according to labour as mainstay and common prosperity as goal. The market economy answers efficiency; socialism constrains distribution and direction — the special institutional problems their combination raises (how public-ownership enterprises become true market subjects, how efficiency and equity are balanced) are taken up in Chapters 4, 5 and 6.

### How should the socialist market economy system be built?

First, deepen state-owned-enterprise reform and remould the subjects of market competition. SOE reform went through three stages: decentralization and profit-sharing; experiments with the share system and the building of a modern enterprise system; and the strategic adjustment of the state sector. The reform centred on strategic adjustment includes: implementing the strategy of classified reform; reforming the state asset management system; converting large competitive SOEs into shareholding companies by international practice and building a standardized modern enterprise system; achieving innovation in corporate governance; improving the enterprise technology-innovation mechanism; and using reform to promote management. This agenda is unfolded in Chapters 4 and 5.

Second, improve the modern market system. The market system is the organic whole formed by the sum of all types of markets and market elements, covering product and factor markets; a unified, open, competitive and orderly market system is the necessary precondition for letting the market play the basic role in resource allocation. The core of improving the market environment is perfecting the market mechanism; beyond clear property rights for market subjects and hardened budget constraints, its conditions include: fostering an environment of equal competition; opening the market further and building a sound, unified, open, competitive and orderly modern market system; and deepening circulation reform and developing modern distribution. At the same time, market order must be rectified and standardized and the credit system improved — the market economy is a moral economy built on the legal system, and needs a triple set of constraint mechanisms: self-restraint subject to personal belief and moral norms; mutual supervision between the two sides of a transaction; and third-party constraint relying on law, public opinion and government.

Third, strengthen and improve macro control. Macro control is both an intrinsic requirement of the socialist market economy and an important condition for its effective operation. Through reform the government needs to transform its functions: straighten out the government-enterprise relation and avoid administrative intervention — leave to the market what the market can do, and let government act only where the market does it poorly or cannot do it; set clear policy goals, optimize policy instruments and raise policy efficiency; build through institutional reform a lean, efficient and clean government service system; raise the transparency of policy and its making and introduce public oversight; administer according to law and create a fair legal environment; and apply national treatment and non-discrimination so that all market participants, including foreign capital, obtain equal opportunity to compete. This agenda is unfolded in Chapter 10.

Fourth, deepen distribution reform and improve the social security system. The core is to build a distribution system with distribution according to labour as the mainstay and multiple modes coexisting, expressing the principle of balancing efficiency and fairness: opposing egalitarianism while preventing excessive income gaps. National income distribution divides into primary distribution and redistribution: the primary distributes by the standard of efficiency and may widen the gaps; through redistribution oriented to the overall and long-run interests of society, the government keeps income gaps within a reasonable range and safeguards social fairness.

## Limitations and further work

The theoretical judgements and policy agenda of this chapter have kept evolving over three decades; four coordinates should be added. First, **the continuing promotion of the market's role**: the text keeps the textbook wording 'basic role', while the Third Plenary Session of the Eighteenth Central Committee in 2013 updated it to 'decisive role' together with 'a better role for the government' — from 'basic' to 'decisive' is not rhetoric but a repositioning aimed at the real contradiction that 'government still intervenes too much in the micro economy'; since 2020 the formulation has condensed further into the better combination of an 'effective market' and a 'proactive government'. Second, **the contemporary echo of the calculation debate**: Hayek's information-and-incentive critique did not expire with the end of the planned system; it turned into a question put to every mechanism of centralized coordination (planning inside large firms, algorithmic coordination in platform economies, the informational basis of industrial policy); whether big data and algorithms have changed the premise that 'dispersed knowledge cannot be centralized' is the new round of the calculation debate — the current mainstream judgement: computing power relieves the computation problem, but the objective function and the incentive problem remain. Third, **the expansion of the list of market-economy features**: the textbook four (property-right rule, decentralized decisions, free competition, price coordination) carry a goods-market perspective; in 2020 the Centre listed data as the fifth major factor of production alongside land, labour, capital and technology, and the 2022 *Opinions on Accelerating the Building of a Unified National Market* pushed rule unification against local protection and market segmentation — the emphasis of market-system building has shifted from 'freeing prices' to 'setting rules, breaking segmentation, strengthening factors'. Fourth, **the boundary of gradual logic**: the experience of nurturing the market outside the old system through incremental reform was successful, but the arbitrage and corruption costs of the dual track and the duplicated construction and local protection bred by inter-locality competition show that the gradual path itself generates problems requiring re-reform — which is exactly the entry point of the institutional-change analysis of Chapter 3.
