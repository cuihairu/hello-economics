# Chapter 9 · The external economic relations of socialism

> **The central question of this chapter**: why is opening up to the outside world an intrinsic necessity rather than an expedient? How did China lever world industrial transfer with its labour-cost advantage and complete an outward-oriented industrialisation? Beneath the achievements of export orientation, what costs have accumulated, and how are openness and economic security to be re-balanced?

## The arc of this chapter

The theoretical ground for opening up to the outside world lies not in international politics, but in the internal logic of the economy of the division of labour. The division of labour brings an increase in productive efficiency and is the source of the long-run growth of the economy; but the process of division of labour endogenously generates contradictions that obstruct the further development of division of labour — the finer the division, the more it depends on exchange to complete cooperation. Plan and market are the two mechanisms that solve the problem of exchange, and the market is open by nature: both economic theory and practice show that the market has a 'threshold effect' in scale and liquidity — only when it reaches a certain scale and liquidity does the market exist and realise its advantage in efficiency; a market too small in scale and too low in liquidity cannot withstand an accidental shock, and the division of labour therefore cannot be sustained. The nature of the market and its interaction with the division of labour decide that the market has an intrinsic impulse towards openness — for a country's system of division of labour to draw the full benefit, it must sooner or later cross national frontiers. Economic opening means that an economic system comes into contact with the outside world through the flow of products, services, technologies, factors and so on; its effect on growth works at two levels: directly, breaking through the two constraints of inputs and of markets that a single country's production process faces, the deepening of the division of labour and the expansion of the market in turn pushing technological innovation and the rise of efficiency; and indirectly, changing the mechanism of factor accumulation and guiding domestic structural adjustment through the spillover effects of factor flows, learning by doing and institutional change. The dynamic benefits of foreign trade (especially of exports of manufactured goods) may be summarised in six points: strengthening import capacity and narrowing the technological gap; guiding resources toward the sectors of comparative advantage and raising the degree of specialisation; overcoming the constraint of domestic market size and obtaining economies of scale; pulling up upstream industries and promoting the formation of a domestic unified market; attracting foreign investment to relieve the savings gap and disseminating management experience; and stimulating continuous improvement in the traded-goods sector through the pressure of international competition. But trade promotes growth only conditionally: the state of external markets is not controllable, and more critically it depends on whether trade can bring about or accompany institutional change and structural adjustment that benefit long-run growth — openness itself is no free ticket to growth.

China's practice of opening up unfolded against the macro background of globalisation. Economic globalisation (in the broad sense) is the process by which economic activity expands from the domestic to the global range, together with the changes it brings in the economic, social, political and life spheres; the flows of commodities, of capital, of technology and of people are the four basic channels of economic interaction between states, and the interaction is linked by the transfer of industries — industrial transfer is the extension to the international range of domestic industrial adjustment and upgrading, its root domestic and external factors playing a reinforcing role. China took this linkage on at exactly the right moment: at the start of the reform and opening up the economy faced three constraints — a savings constraint (domestic savings insufficient to support the expansion of investment), a foreign-exchange constraint (foreign exchange insufficient to support necessary imports) and an absorption-capacity constraint (a lack of technology, of entrepreneurs and of managerial talent) — and the first two gaps are the famous 'two gaps' of development economics (proposed by Chenery and Strout in 1966). Policy accordingly moved from import substitution to export orientation: after the savings gap had gradually yielded place to the foreign-exchange gap in the late 1980s, large-scale export became the strategic axis for driving growth and maintaining macro balance. Once the goal of the socialist market economy system was established in 1992, the marketisation reform of external economic and trade relations opened up in full — cutting tariffs and cutting quotas and licences, abolishing the import-substitution catalogue, gradually devolving trading rights, a single exchange-rate system with bank settlement and sale of foreign exchange, and convertibility of the renminbi in current-account transactions; after joining the WTO in 2001 openness moved from quantitative to qualitative change, entering the process of multilateral economic and trade rule-making. The spatial pattern opened out step by step in the same way: in 1979–1980 the four special economic zones of Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Shantou and Xiamen set out; in 1984 fourteen coastal port cities were opened; in 1985 the coastal economic open areas of the Yangtze River delta, the Pearl River delta and the southeastern Fujian coast were created; in 1988–1990 the range was extended to the Liaodong and Shandong peninsulas and the Hainan special economic zone and the Pudong new area of Shanghai were established; and from 1992 five riverine cities, thirteen inland border cities and eighteen inland provincial (capital) cities were opened — so that a pattern of all-round, multi-level and broad-field openness took shape.

The core of the opening strategy was outward-oriented industrialisation: using the trend of global industrial adjustment and transfer to build, on the basis of the labour-cost advantage, a model of industrialisation under open conditions. Here there is an important theoretical clarification: the upheaval in China's industrial structure and foreign-trade structure is, less than a 'dynamic development of comparative advantage', a change in the means of realising comparative advantage and in the industrial vehicle that carries it — the foundation of the labour-cost advantage has never changed, what changed is the vehicle supplied by the international transfer of manufacturing: the production processes of labour-intensive products and the assembly stages of capital-intensive products (assembly itself being labour-intensive) transferred to China in large numbers, so that processing trade came to dominate exports and China became 'the assembly workshop of the world factory'. The achievement of this strategy was enormous, and the cost has shown itself with the passage of time: the deterioration of the terms of trade is marked (the expansion of export volume pushes down export prices relative to import prices); resource and environmental pressure has grown; the traded-goods sector has over-developed while services lag and the long-run pressure on employment has risen; and there is the risk of a frozen comparative advantage — if factor endowment cannot be upgraded into technological and human-capital advantage, the economy may be locked at the low end of the value chain. Financial openness likewise has two faces: FDI (foreign direct investment) drives growth through three channels — the direct contribution of foreign-invested enterprise output, spillovers and trade-induced learning by doing, and the pulling along of technology introduction and imports; but under China's particular foreign-exchange management system, inflows of foreign capital are converted into foreign-exchange reserves by governmental exchange and then flow back to international financial markets — this special cycle, on the one hand complements the banking system in the domestic allocation of credit and guides the industrial and regional structure of capital formation, and on the other hand makes monetary policy under a pegged exchange rate a passive mechanism of credit allocation, bringing a 'loss of economic stability'; and the net effect of two-way cross-border capital flows on macro stability was, at the time, undecidable for want of empirical evidence. The trade-off between openness and security, between gain and cost, is thereby the core tension this chapter leaves behind.

## Core concepts

### Economic opening

Economic opening means that an economic system comes into contact with the outside world through the flow of products, services, technologies, factors and so on; its main content is the active development of all forms of external economic relation, while taking care at the same time to safeguard national economic security. Opening up to the outside world is a long-term cardinal state policy of our country.

Why is openness raised to the height of a 'cardinal state policy' rather than an ordinary policy? Because, as the arc shows, openness is not an added option of the logic of the division of labour but its intrinsic requirement — a closed economy is constrained both by the size of the domestic market (below the threshold effect the division of labour cannot deepen) and by the rate of evolution of its own stock of technology (learning by doing can only crawl along its own experience curve). The historical evidence is equally strong: the roughly thirty years of essentially closed road after the founding of the People's Republic and the growth miracle after the reform and opening up form a controlled experiment on one and the same country — openness is therefore defined as a long-term institutional arrangement the obverse and reverse of reform, not as a periodic loosening or tightening of policy.

### Economic globalisation

Economic globalisation (in the broad sense) is the process by which economic activity expands from the domestic to the global range, together with the changes that follow in the economic, social, political and life spheres; in essence it is the process by which the economic activity of each country moves from the domestic to the global and becomes socialised on the global range. Economic interaction between states is realised by four basic channels — the flows of commodities, of capital, of technology and of people — and is linked by the transfer of industries.

Why is the transfer of industries taken as the key linkage of globalisation? Because the four flows are in themselves only the surface, and their common source is the redistribution of industries between states: industrial transfer necessarily requires the accompanying flows of commodities (intermediates and re-exports), of capital (investment), of technology (equipment and process) and of people (the flow of management and technical personnel); and conversely, once industrial transfer has taken place the four flows reinforce themselves (the expansion of re-exports and of component export). Understand this point and one understands China's place in globalisation: China did not abstractly 'participate in globalisation', but concretely took over the global redistribution of manufacturing stages — and this decides both the structure of the gains from openness (employment, accumulation, technological spillover) and the structure of the costs (being locked into assembly stages, the terms of trade, the environmental load).

### The outward-oriented industrialisation strategy

The outward-oriented industrialisation strategy means using the trend of opening up and of global industrial adjustment and transfer to build, on the basis of the labour-cost advantage, a model of industrialisation under open conditions; on this common basis of the labour-cost advantage, constructing a mutually linked foreign-trade strategy and industrialisation model, fully bringing out the comparative advantage of manufacturing, so that manufacturing exports grow at high speed and industry develops rapidly under the pull of exports.

Why does this strategy deserve a name of its own? Because it describes precisely the part of the Chinese experience most often misread: the leap of the export structure from textiles to mechanical and electrical products and then to high-technology products is easily read as a dynamic upgrading of comparative advantage, when in fact the labour-intensive character of the assembly stages has not changed — what changed is the 'vehicle' supplied by the international transfer of industry. The warning value of the concept lies here: the vehicle can be upgraded, but if the advantage is not upgraded (from labour cost to technology, human capital and supply-chain network), the strategy will fail when the vehicle is exhausted — the 'risk of a frozen comparative advantage' is precisely the policy warning that grows out of inside this concept.

### The terms of trade

The terms of trade is the ratio of a country's export price index to its import price index, measuring the quantity of imports one unit of exports can bring back; a falling ratio is deterioration of the terms of trade — exporting more in order to get back less.

Why is 'deterioration of the terms of trade' the first item on the list of problems with China's trade model? Because it turns on its head the superficial success indicator of 'rapid export growth': expansion by volume to make up for price, under conditions where competition in the labour-intensive stages is fierce and supply is highly elastic, pushes prices lower the more it grows, so that the share of the gains from trade accruing to the nation actually falls — this is the echo in contemporary China of the Prebisch-Singer hypothesis. It shows that the gains from openness cannot be judged by the size of exports alone, but must also be judged by the country's bargaining position in the value chain, and this is the theoretical starting point of the later policy agenda of 'moving up the value chain'.

## Problems and analysis

### Wherein lies the inevitability of the modern economy's movement from closed to open?

The chain of logic has three steps. First step: the division of labour is the source of efficiency — the increase in productive efficiency brought by the division of labour is the source of long-run economic growth, a basic proposition repeatedly verified since Smith. Second step: exchange is a necessary condition of the division of labour — the process of division of labour endogenously generates contradictions that obstruct its development (coordination costs, the limitation of market range), which must be solved by an exchange mechanism; plan and market are the two candidate mechanisms, and both history and theory show that the market mechanism can complete large-scale exchange at lower informational cost, out of which there derive a complex market system and the institutional arrangements surrounding exchange. Third step: the market is open by nature — the market has a threshold effect in scale and liquidity, a market short of scale and liquidity will disappear under shock and the division of labour cannot be sustained; the nature of the market and its interaction with the division of labour decide that it intrinsically demands breaking through geographical and national-frontier limits — openness is the spatial unfolding of market logic. Taken together the three steps give this conclusion: closure is not another 'choice', but a high-cost state running against the intrinsic tendency of the economy of the division of labour; opening up to the outside world is therefore elevated from an instrument of policy to the road of development itself.

### How does trade openness affect economic growth?

At the theoretical level: openness means the system of division of labour crosses national frontiers from the domestic, the expansion of market boundaries satisfying the most basic requirement for the realisation of the benefits of division of labour, and at the same time letting growth break through the constraint of inputs within one country; the dynamic effects of technological spillover, learning by doing and institutional change in market competition and factor flows further magnify the push. At the empirical level: the growth performance of outward-oriented developing countries is generally better than that of inward-oriented ones, and inward-oriented countries see marked improvement in economic operation after turning to a higher degree of outward orientation; the high-speed growth of exports of manufactured goods brings six dynamic benefits — strengthening import capacity to make up the foreign-exchange gap and narrow the technological gap, guiding resources toward the sectors of comparative advantage and raising specialisation and labour productivity, overcoming the constraint of domestic market size and obtaining economies of scale, pulling up upstream industries and the formation of a domestic unified market, attracting foreign investment to relieve the savings gap and disseminating technological and management experience, and international competition stimulating continuous improvement of technology and efficiency in the traded-goods sector. At the level of conditions: trade promotes growth only conditionally — the state of external markets affects the development of trade itself; and more importantly how trade acts on the sources of growth: for developing countries, trade either directly brings capital and technology, or indirectly raises total factor productivity by affecting the mechanism of capital accumulation, the mechanism of technological progress and the process of structural adjustment. The conclusion is a judgemental one: whether trade can drive growth depends critically on whether the development of foreign trade can bring about or accompany institutional change and structural adjustment that benefit long-run growth — countries that treat trade as an engine of growth in itself, without accompanying domestic reform, will often harvest the costs of openness rather than its gains.

### How did the all-round, multi-level pattern of Chinese openness come to be formed?

The main line of policy evolution: in 1984 the *Decision of the CPC Central Committee on Economic System Reform* made opening up to the outside world explicit as a long-term cardinal state policy; in 1987 the Thirteenth Congress established the basic line of 'one central task, two basic points', and opening up to the outside world became an important element of the basic line; in 1992 the Fourteenth Congress proposed three major tasks of expanding openness (forming an all-round, multi-channel, multi-level pattern of openness; broadening the fields of using foreign investment and perfecting the investment environment; opening up international markets and expanding outward investment and transnational operation); joining the WTO in 2001 provided the opportunity to raise the level of openness all-roundly, and the 'Eleventh Five-Year' plan went further to propose participating in international economic and technological cooperation and competition on a wider range, in broader fields and at a higher level, while firmly safeguarding national economic security. The spatial path advanced step by step: in 1979–1980 the four special economic zones of Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Shantou and Xiamen were trialled as windows and experimental fields; 1984–1991 was the stage of expansion — in 1984 fourteen coastal port cities were opened (involving ten coastal provinces), in 1985 the Yangtze River delta, the Pearl River delta and the southeastern Fujian coast were set aside as coastal economic open areas, and in 1988–1990 the range was extended to the Liaodong and Shandong peninsulas and the Hainan special economic zone and the Pudong new area were established; from 1992 the opening spread out in full — five further riverine cities, thirteen inland border cities and eighteen inland provincial (capital) cities were opened, and openness extended to the border, the river and the interior; and after accession the change went from quantitative to qualitative, territory and industry were broadened throughout, and domestic institutions, policy transparency, market rules and the legal system were brought into all-round alignment with international practice, while China entered the process of multilateral rule-making. This path of 'pilot — expansion — institutionalisation' is isomorphic with the logic of gradual reform in Chapter 3: the special zones are the outward-facing version of the 'right to pilot', and the spatial diffusion of the pattern of openness is the replay in the external field of the intermediate diffusion of institutional innovation.

## Synthetic essay

### How does foreign trade affect the process of Chinese industrialisation?

First, foreign trade is an effective means of breaking through the constraints of domestic industrialisation. The fundamental aim of the outward-oriented strategy is to break through the constraints of industrialisation by openness — foreign trade accompanied the process of industrialisation, and industrialisation is the lever by which foreign trade drives economic growth; its basic content is to build, on the basis of the labour-cost advantage and using the trend of global industrial adjustment and transfer, a model of industrialisation under open conditions, with the foreign-trade strategy and the industrialisation model mutually linked and sharing the same foundation of comparative advantage. Second, foreign trade lets comparative advantage be brought into play: the evolution of the strategy unfolded always around the unchanging foundation of the labour-cost advantage, what changed being the forms in which the advantage was realised and the industrial and product vehicles that carried it — adapting to the new opportunities of the international competitive pattern and of manufacturing transfer, China adjusted its industrialisation and foreign-trade model, realised the scale expansion and structural change of trade and manufacturing, and pulled along high-speed growth. Hence the view that 'the change in China's trade structure and industrial structure reflects the dynamic development of comparative advantage' is one-sided — the change reflects rather the change in the means of realising comparative advantage and in the industrial vehicle. Third, foreign trade brought about the formation of a simple outward-oriented industrialisation strategy: in the dynamic adjustment of the international system of division of labour, the production processes of labour-intensive products and the assembly stages of capital-intensive products transferred to China and became the principal export sector; the relevant markets transferred with them, China became the chief supplier of labour-intensive products on the international market, and even the assembly stages of high-technology products transferred to China because of their labour-intensive character — so that processing trade came to dominate exports. Fourth, foreign trade also made the problems of the trade model visible: since 2000 the changes in the terms of trade, in resource consumption, in industrial structure and in macro policy have exposed a rising cost trend — deterioration of the terms of trade, greater resource and environmental pressure, over-development of the traded-goods sector alongside the lag of services, and the risk of a frozen comparative advantage. The four points taken together are a complete portrait of the strategy: the same comparative advantage is both the engine of a twenty-year growth miracle and the source of the later pressure for transition — the success of the strategy contains within it the necessity of renewing the strategy.

### Set out the evolution of China's policy of opening up to the outside world

For roughly thirty years after the founding of the People's Republic, our country followed essentially a road of closure and self-development — there were the objective constraints of external blockade, and also errors of judgement about the international situation, and the two combined left openness chronically absent, the price being that the gap with the world's advanced technological level was again widened. After 1978 policy went through five steps: in 1984 the *Decision of the CPC Central Committee on Economic System Reform* established opening up to the outside world as a long-term cardinal state policy; in 1987 the Thirteenth Congress established 'one central task, two basic points', and openness entered the basic line; in 1992 the Fourteenth Congress proposed three major tasks (an all-round pattern of openness, broadening the fields of foreign investment and perfecting the investment environment, opening up international markets and developing outward investment and transnational operation); after 1992 six marketisation reforms of external economic and trade relations advanced together — cutting tariffs, cutting quotas and licences, cutting export tax rebates, abolishing the import-substitution catalogue, relaxing the examination and approval of trading rights and eventually devolving them in full, and unifying the exchange rate with settlement and sale of foreign exchange and convertibility of the renminbi in current-account transactions; and in 2001 accession to the WTO, openness moving from policy openness to institutional openness, China entering the process of multilateral economic and trade rule-making. There are two deep layers of logic in the evolution: strategically, from import substitution (self-reliance under foreign-exchange constraint) to export orientation (the outward pull after the savings gap yielded to the foreign-exchange gap) and then to all-round openness (imports and exports, attracting investment and outward investment, market competition and rule-making given equal weight), escaping from the either-or of the two strategies; and in method, from regional pilots to national institutional alignment, mutually causal with domestic market-oriented reform — every step up in opening to the outside world pushed domestic institutions (trade, exchange rate, foreign investment, law) back into reform, and openness therefore became an accelerator of reform.

## Limitations and further work

This chapter was written in the twilight of the export-oriented strategy, and the evolution since then has seven coordinates. First, **from export orientation to dual circulation**: the global financial crisis of 2008 exposed the fragility of reliance on external demand, and in 2020 the Centre formally proposed building a new development pattern with the domestic big circulation as the mainstay and the domestic and international dual circulations promoting one another — this is both a strategic response to the problem of 'over-development of the traded-goods sector and the lag of services' and a proactive breaking of the risk of a frozen comparative advantage. Second, **the institutional upgrading of the forms of openness**: the free trade pilot zones (from Shanghai in 2013), the Foreign Investment Law (2020) with negative-list management, the Hainan Free Trade Port (2020), the China International Import Expo (2018), and 'institutional opening-up' (first proposed in 2018) mark the turning of openness from the level of factors and policy preference to the level of rules, regulations, administration and standards; and the Belt and Road Initiative (2013) together with the RCEP (signed in 2020, in force in 2022) rebuilt the open architecture at the regional level. Third, **the real occurrence of vehicle upgrading**: the share of processing trade in exports has already fallen markedly and ordinary trade is now over half (subject to the latest published figures), and among high-technology exports the share of domestic brands and of autonomous technology is rising, while around 2023 Chinese car exports leapt to first place in the world (of the order of five million units, subject to the latest published figures) — the 'lock-in as assembly workshop' that the textbook feared has been broken in some industries, but the external dependence for core components and industrial software still proves that the climb is unfinished. Fourth, **the gradual cashing-in of financial openness**: reform of the exchange-rate formation mechanism (2005, 2015), the prudent advance of capital-account convertibility, the Shanghai-Hong Kong, Shenzhen-Hong Kong and Bond Connects, the abolition of the QFII and RQFII quotas (2020), and a macro-prudential framework for cross-border capital flows — the 'foreign inflow — reserve accumulation — capital reflux' cycle proposed by the textbook and its stability cost were partly dissolved as foreign-exchange reserves fell from a peak of about four trillion dollars (around 2014, subject to the latest published figures) and exchange-rate elasticity grew; the policy combination under the 'trilemma' remains a hard constraint on the openness agenda. Fifth, **the back-swing of de-globalisation**: from 2018 the Sino-US trade friction, export controls on technology, supply-chain 'friend-shoring' and regional reconfiguration confirm this chapter's judgement that 'trade promotes growth only conditionally' — the most rigid of the conditions is geopolitical; the real constraint on the 'impulse towards openness' has shifted from economic thresholds to security boundaries, and the trade-off between openness and economic security has become a daily business of industrial policy rather than an abstract proposition. Sixth, **the entry of the green constraint**: the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM, entering its transition period in 2023) and the like make carbon emissions a new variable in the terms of trade — the governance of resource and environmental pressure (the dual-carbon goals) is now tied together with export competitiveness. Seventh, **the re-balancing of theory**: New Structural Economics (Lin Yifu) has theorised the comparative-advantage strategy, while the literature on 'useful industrial policy' and on global value chains stresses the policy agency of upgrading — this chapter's distinction of 'change in the means of realisation and in the vehicle' is precisely the Chinese footnote to the dispute between these two schools.
