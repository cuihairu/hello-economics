# Common summation formulas

> **The central question of this chapter**: how do you add up income over many periods? Interest rolling on interest, cash flows discounted period by period, interest accruing year after year — how much do they come to? And why does the "geometric structure" deserve a set of formulas all of its own?

## The arc of this chapter

The two leading actors of the summation formulas — arithmetic and geometric — are older than intuition expects. The Gauss anecdote for the arithmetic sum (adding $1 + 2 + \cdots + 100$ in a flash as a schoolboy) is widely told, but the formula itself was common knowledge among reckoners before Euclid; the geometric series is the same age as the institution of compound interest — medieval merchants' ledgers, Huygens and Jacob Bernoulli's annuity calculations in the seventeenth century, down to the present-value formulas of twentieth-century finance — all the same algebra. The summation sign $\sum$ settled into its present shape in the age of Euler (1707-1783). The problem it handles is plain and fundamental: **accumulate quantities that arrive period by period into a total**.

Economics' need for summation comes from the "many periods, many agents" structure in two directions. Along time: the accumulation of multi-period income, costs, consumption and taxes (the multi-period budget constraint of the life-cycle model), the present value of discounted cash flows (marginal efficiency of capital, bond pricing), the rolling of debt period by period (the accumulation of deficits). Across agents: the sample mean and variance (adding up $n$ observations), the sum-of-squares decompositions of statistical testing and regression. Every formula in chapter 11's foundations of statistics has this chapter's summation sign hidden in its numerator and denominator.

The route of the chapter: the two basic sets of formulas, arithmetic and geometric (with the infinite geometric series attached — the engine of discounting theory), then an inventory of common formulas such as sums of powers of the natural numbers, and finally three summation techniques (splitting into groups, shifting and subtracting, adding in reverse order) — they are not just a form of shorthand but operations that will genuinely appear in later proofs.

## Core concepts

### The arithmetic series

An arithmetic series is a sequence in which each term differs from the one before by a constant $d$ (the common difference): $a, a+d, a+2d, \ldots$. The sum of the first $n$ terms:

$$S_n = \frac{n}{2} \left(2a + (n-1)d\right) = \frac{n}{2} (a + l) \quad (l \text{ is the last term})$$

Why is the arithmetic sum intuitive by nature? The symmetry behind it is "pairing first with last gives a constant sum": in $1 + 2 + \cdots + 100$, $1+100 = 2+99 = \cdots = 101$, fifty pairs, $50 \times 101 = 5050$. The counterpart of this symmetry in economics is the total effect of an even process: along a linear growth path the accumulated quantity is "the average rate × the number of periods" — cumulative depreciation under straight-line depreciation, cumulative price adjustment under steady inflation — all direct applications of $\frac{n}{2}(a+l)$.

### The geometric series and the infinite geometric series

A geometric series is a sequence in which each term is a constant multiple $r$ (the common ratio) of the one before: $a, ar, ar^2, \ldots$. The sum of the first $n$ terms:

$$S_n = a\,\frac{1 - r^n}{1 - r} \quad (r \neq 1)$$

If $|r| < 1$, the infinite geometric series converges, with sum:

$$S = \frac{a}{1 - r}$$

Why is the geometric sum the single most important formula in economics? Because discounted cash flows, perpetuities, the Gordon growth model, the total effect of the multiplier process, the number of decaying rounds in financial-contagion models — all are aliases of the $r^n \to 0$ structure. The Keynesian multiplier $\frac{1}{1 - c}$ ($c$ the marginal propensity to consume) is literally the sum of an infinite geometric series with ratio $c$: the first round of spending $a$, then $ac$ again, then $ac^2$... total effect $\frac{a}{1-c}$. The condition $|r| < 1$ is likewise an economic condition: the marginal propensity to consume below one (otherwise the multiplier diverges and the economy is unstable), the growth rate below the discount rate (otherwise present values diverge, as chapter 2 showed).

### Sums of powers of the natural numbers

$$\sum_{k=1}^{n} k = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}, \qquad
\sum_{k=1}^{n} k^2 = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}, \qquad
\sum_{k=1}^{n} k^3 = \left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^2$$

Sums of odd and even numbers: $\sum_{k=1}^{n} (2k-1) = n^2$, and $\sum_{k=1}^{n} 2k = n(n+1)$.

Why do the power sums deserve a place? Three reasons. In statistics, the sample variance and the regression sums of squares ($ESS$, $RSS$, $TSS$) are all computations of the $\sum x_i^2$ type. Growth accounting and the error estimates of discrete quadrature use the order of the power sums ($\sum k^p$ is of order $n^{p+1}/(p+1)$ in $n$ — precisely the discrete footnote to "the integral is the limit of a sum" in chapter 5). And the beautiful structure of the sum of cubes being the square of the sum of squares, $\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^2$, is a classic showpiece of combinatorial thinking beyond its use as an exercise in mathematical induction.

### Three techniques of summation

**Splitting into groups**: cut the sequence into tractable pieces. **Adding in reverse order**: add $S$ to $S$ written backwards and the symmetric structure appears at once (this is where the arithmetic formula comes from). **Shifting and subtracting**: subtract $rS$ from $S$ and the middle terms cancel — this is the derivation of the geometric sum formula, and the source of the "write it twice, shift by one, subtract" move used when deriving perpetuities and the Gordon model.

Why learn the techniques as formal content? Because in discounting problems, shifting and subtracting is not an exam trick but a modelling move: writing the year-by-year cash flows twice, shifting one copy by a period and subtracting derives $PV = \frac{C}{r-g}$ from first principles, with no need to memorise the formula. The derivation in chapter 12's present-value section uses exactly this.

## Problems and analysis

### Why does the multi-period budget constraint always appear as a "summed" expression?

In the life-cycle model the consumer faces "the present value of consumption across all periods must not exceed the present value of income across all periods": $\sum_{t} \frac{c_t}{(1+r)^t} \le \sum_{t} \frac{y_t}{(1+r)^t}$. These two sums compress a lifetime of choices into one constraint — the foundation of chapter 12's intertemporal optimisation. The same holds in statistics: the sample mean $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i$ is the optimal summary in the "minimise squared error" sense (minimising absolute error gives the median, minimising squared error the mean) precisely because the sum quantifies "good" into a single number. Summation is the standard compressor that squeezes "many periods / many agents" into "one"; wherever a model needs a single constraint or a single objective function, it must appear.

### How do you make "the multiplier effect decays round by round" precise?

Let the marginal propensity to consume be $c = 0.8$ and the first round of government spending 10 billion yuan. The rounds of additional output are $100,\ 100c,\ 100c^2, \ldots$ (in units of 100 million yuan), for a total effect of $\frac{100}{1-0.8} = 50$ billion yuan. Note two things. First, the first few rounds deliver most of the effect (the first five rounds realise $100 \times \frac{1 - 0.8^5}{0.2} = 100 \times 3.3616 \approx 336.2$, matching the round-by-round sum $100(1+0.8+0.64+0.512+0.4096)$, about 67% of the total), so the "long-run multiplier" takes a long time to cash in. Second, if $c$ varies with income or there is a tax leakage ($c$ replaced by the marginal propensity out of disposable income $c(1-t)$), the ratio shrinks and so does the multiplier — real-world fiscal multiplier estimates run below the textbook value for exactly this reason of leakages (see the latest estimates of the central banks and fiscal research institutes for the numbers).

## Derivations and worked examples

### The shift-and-subtract derivation of the geometric sum

$$S_n = a + ar + ar^2 + \cdots + ar^{n-1}$$
$$rS_n = ar + ar^2 + \cdots + ar^{n-1} + ar^n$$

Subtract the two and all the middle terms cancel: $S_n - rS_n = a - ar^n$, which rearranges to $S_n = a\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}$ ($r \neq 1$). Let $n \to \infty$ with $|r| < 1$, so $r^n \to 0$, and $S = \frac{a}{1-r}$.

### Example 1: the present value of an annuity

Receive 30,000 yuan at each year-end for four years, at a discount rate of 10%. The present value:

$$PV = \frac{3}{1.1} + \frac{3}{1.1^2} + \frac{3}{1.1^3} + \frac{3}{1.1^4} = \frac{3}{1.1} \times \frac{1 - (1/1.1)^4}{1 - 1/1.1}$$

$\frac{1}{1.1} \approx 0.9091$ and $(0.9091)^4 \approx 0.6830$, so $PV \approx 2.7273 \times \frac{0.3170}{0.0909} \approx 2.7273 \times 3.487 \approx 9.51$, i.e. about 95,100 yuan. The perpetual version of the same structure (30,000 a year forever) uses $S = \frac{a}{1-r}$: $PV = \frac{3}{0.1} = 30$, i.e. 300,000 yuan — four periods already recover a third of the perpetual value, and the sensitivity of the present value to horizon is plain to see. (The 3 and 9.51 and 30 above are in units of 10,000 yuan.)

### Example 2: numerical check of the power sums

Sum of the squares of the first five natural numbers: $\sum_{k=1}^{5} k^2 = 1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 = 55$, and the formula gives $\frac{5 \times 6 \times 11}{6} = 55$ ✓. Sum of the first four cubes: $1 + 8 + 27 + 64 = 100$, and the formula gives $\left(\frac{4 \times 5}{2}\right)^2 = 100$ ✓. Sum of odd numbers: $1+3+5+7+9 = 25 = 5^2$ ✓. The value of these small examples is building two-way trust between "the formula" and "the hand calculation"; large-sample computation is left to statistical software, but the structure of the formula must be tangible in your own hand.

## Limits and what comes next

First, **arithmetic and geometric are only two special cases**: real economic series are neither even-paced nor constant-ratio (income distributions are long-tailed, growth comes in phases), and a general sequence can only be summed numerically or approximated through an integral — the definite integral of chapter 5 is exactly the product of "summing pieces, then taking the limit". Second, **infinite sums need a licence from convergence**: the lesson of the harmonic series in chapter 2 (terms going to zero while the sum diverges) warns that before applying a formula to any infinite-horizon discounting or accumulation, check the ratio/decay condition first; most paradoxes of "exploding utility" or "exploding debt" come from applying a summation formula to a divergent series. Third, **the extension to weighted sums**: give each term a weight $w_i$ (with $\sum w_i = 1$) and you get a weighted average — the expectation (chapter 10), price indices and input-output weights are all its avatars; the double sum $\sum_i \sum_j$ leads on to matrix multiplication (chapter 7) and to covariance and the analysis of variance (chapter 11). The summation sign is thus a thread running through all the later chapters.
