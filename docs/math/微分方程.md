# Differential equations

> **The central question of this chapter**: after an overshoot in prices, how do they retrace? Will the path of capital accumulation slide into a steady state? Can inflation be pressed down by monetary tightening, and how fast? Every economic process in which "the speed of change depends on the current state" is described — and solved — by differential equations.

## The arc of this chapter

Differential equations were born in the field of applied calculus: Newton's second law $F = m\ddot{x}$ is already a second-order equation — force determines acceleration (the rate of change of the rate of change of state), and only solving the equation yields the trajectory. Across the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries Euler, Lagrange and Cauchy developed systematic solution theory; the qualitative methods Poincaré (1854-1912) opened up in celestial mechanics (solve for no explicit solution, analyse directly how the orbits run in the phase diagram) became the mainstream of dynamical systems in the twentieth century. That turn matters enormously for economics, because most economic equations cannot be solved in closed form — but **the stability question can be answered without a closed form at all**.

Economics took up dynamics by questioning "equilibrium". Walras's process of tâtonnement describes prices adjusting toward equilibrium, but whether the adjustment converges he himself did not settle; Samuelson (1915-2009), in *Foundations of Economic Analysis* (1947), made this a methodological programme for economics: comparative-static conclusions must be compatible with dynamic stability (the correspondence principle), and to check that the dynamic equation has to be written down and examined explicitly. Ever since, Solow's 1956 growth model took its decisive step (existence and stability of the steady state), Dornbusch's (1942-2002) 1976 exchange rate overshooting model (sticky and slowly adjusting goods prices against instantaneous clearing of asset prices), Cagan's (1927-2012) 1956 dynamics of inflation linking $\dot{m}$ to expectations — all of them are differential equations, and all are economics' coming-out party in dynamics. Today's time-series models in macro and finance (DSGE, affine term structures of interest rates) still have systems of ordinary differential equations for their skeleton.

The route of the chapter: the classification of equations (order, linearity, constant versus variable coefficients); three solution methods for first-order equations (separable variables, the integrating factor, homogeneous substitution); the characteristic-equation method for second-order equations with constant coefficients — its three cases (distinct real roots, a repeated root, complex roots) map directly onto the three shapes of economic dynamics (monotone convergence, the critical case, oscillation); and finally the stability criteria and their economic applications.

## Core concepts

### Differential equations and their classification

A differential equation is an equation containing an unknown function and its derivatives; solving it means finding the functions that satisfy it. By the number of independent variables in the unknown function they split into ordinary differential equations (ODE) and partial differential equations (PDE); by the order of the highest derivative into first, second and higher order; by whether solutions add linearly into linear and nonlinear. Finding a particular solution given the equation and an initial condition (such as $y(t_0) = y_0$) is an initial value problem; given conditions at both endpoints it is a boundary value problem.

Why do economic dynamics write themselves as differential equations? Because "the speed of change depends on the current state" is the most universal structure in economics: capital accumulation $\dot{K} = sF(K) - \delta K$ (the saving flow minus depreciation), inflation adjustment $\dot{\pi} = \alpha(Y - Y^*)$ (the output gap driving the Phillips curve), price adjustment $\dot{p} = \lambda(D - S)$ (excess demand pushing the price). Economics cares about the whole path rather than a single instant, and first-order information (the current position) determines the first-order rate of change (the current speed) — which is exactly the defining structure of a differential equation.

### Methods for first-order equations

**Separable variables**: when $\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = g(x)h(y)$, rewrite as $\frac{1}{h(y)}\,\mathrm{d}y = g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$ and integrate each side. **Linear first-order equation** $\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} + p(x)y = q(x)$: multiply by the integrating factor $\mu(x) = e^{\int p(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}$ and the left side becomes a total derivative, $(\mu y)' = \mu q$; integrating gives

$$y = \frac{1}{\mu(x)} \left( \int \mu(x) q(x)\,\mathrm{d}x + C \right)$$

**Homogeneous equation** $\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = F\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)$: set $v = y/x$ (that is, $y = vx$) and it reduces to separable variables.

Why sort the methods by type first? Because each of the three routes claims one kind of structure: separable variables fits growth processes where "the source and the state can be decoupled" (population $\dot{P} = kP$, resource extraction); the integrating factor fits "stock adjustment driven linearly" (money demand converging toward a target, inflation responding to a monetary shock); homogeneous substitution fits "proportional-to-scale" structures. Recognising the structure and picking the right method matters more than memorising recipes — which is why the next section keeps the "exponential solution" on a separate shelf: it is the endpoint of every linear method.

### Second-order equations with constant coefficients and the characteristic equation

$$a\ddot{y} + b\dot{y} + cy = 0$$

Substitute the trial solution $y = e^{rx}$ and you get the characteristic equation $ar^2 + br + c = 0$; the three cases of its roots decide the shape of the solution:

1. **Distinct real roots** $r_1 \neq r_2$: $y = C_1 e^{r_1 x} + C_2 e^{r_2 x}$ — two exponential processes superimposed, converging or diverging monotonely;
2. **Repeated root** $r$: $y = (C_1 + C_2 x)e^{rx}$ — the critical case, sitting exactly on the edge of convergence;
3. **Complex roots** $r = \alpha \pm i\beta$: $y = e^{\alpha x}(C_1 \cos \beta x + C_2 \sin \beta x)$ — an oscillation whose amplitude moves as $e^{\alpha x}$; $\alpha < 0$ is a damped oscillation (convergence), $\alpha > 0$ a divergent one.

Why complex roots mean oscillation is no mystery: the real part of the complex exponential $e^{i\beta x} = \cos \beta x + i \sin \beta x$ (Euler's formula) is the cosine — oscillation is the cosine component of a complex root with negative real part. The real part $\alpha$ inside the multiplying factor $e^{\alpha x}$ governs the fate of the amplitude. Translated into economic language this criterion reads: **if every real part is negative, equilibrium is stable** (disturbances decay); if any real part is positive, it is unstable (disturbances amplify) — the same machinery as the eigenvalue stability criterion in chapter 8, where you will see that eigenvalues are just the characteristic equation taken seriously.

### Variable coefficients and higher-order equations

Variable-coefficient linear equations, $\ddot{y} + p(x)\dot{y} + q(x)y = f(x)$, generally have no uniform elementary method; they need reduction of order from a known particular solution, power series or numerical methods. Higher-order equations with constant coefficients extend the characteristic-equation idea to the case of $n$ roots. When the non-homogeneous term $f(x) \neq 0$, the general solution = the homogeneous general solution + any particular solution (find the particular solution by undetermined coefficients or by variation of parameters).

Why does something you cannot solve still belong in the textbook? Because qualitative analysis (the Poincaré tradition) does not depend on a closed form: linearise the equation at the equilibrium point, read the signs of the real parts of the characteristic roots, and stability is decided on the spot. Almost every economic model is a non-linear system (the production function of the Solow model, the joint adjustment of IS-LM); a closed-form solution is a matter of luck. "Linearise + eigenvalues" is therefore the standard workflow of macro dynamics, and chapter 8's matrix tools are its engine.

## Questions and analysis

### Why does a process with constant growth rate always turn up as an exponential?

Let the growth rate be constant at $k$: $\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t} = kP$. Separate variables: $\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{P} = k\,\mathrm{d}t$ — integrate to get $\ln P = kt + C$, i.e. $P(t) = P_0 e^{kt}$. Once "constant growth rate" is written as a differential equation, the solution is exponential automatically — not a coincidence but a restatement of the definition (the exponential is precisely the function whose derivative is proportional to itself). Compound interest, population growth, technological progress, the depreciation of money under inflation: every process that "replicates itself in proportion" is the same structure. Its link with the discrete version (the $e = \lim(1+1/n)^n$ of chapter 2) closes here: the gap between continuous and discrete compounding is exactly the gap between a differential equation and a difference equation.

### Can tightening "press down" inflation? — how to read a linear adjustment model

Write the dynamics of inflation as $\dot{\pi} = -\beta(\pi - \pi^*) + \gamma u$ ($\pi^*$ is target inflation, $u$ is a monetary shock, $\beta > 0$). It is a linear equation of the integrating-factor type, and its general solution is

$$\pi(t) = \pi^* + (\pi(0) - \pi^*)e^{-\beta t} + \gamma \int_0^t e^{-\beta(t-s)} u(s)\,\mathrm{d}s$$

Three readings: the speed at which inflation converges to target is set by $\beta$ (the time to fall by half is $\ln 2 / \beta$, and the slowness of disinflation is thereby a question of political economy); the contemporaneous effect of a shock is amplified by $\gamma$ and then decays as $e^{-\beta t}$ (the mathematical shape of monetary policy lags); and if expectations are brought in ($\pi^e$ moving with policy), the static $\pi^*$ becomes a moving target — which is precisely the target of the Lucas critique (1976): the parameters $\beta$ and $\gamma$ are not institutional invariants. The model gives the intuition for direction and speed, while the "policy invariance" of the parameters has to be answered by macroeconomics itself.

## Derivations and worked examples

### Example 1: an initial value problem for population growth

$\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t} = kP$, with $P(0) = P_0$. Separating variables and integrating gives $\ln P = kt + \ln P_0$, hence $P(t) = P_0 e^{kt}$. Put in numbers: an annual growth rate of $k = 3\%$ (roughly the order of magnitude of population growth in some sub-Saharan economies in the 2020s; the exact reading should be taken from the latest release of the UN Population Division), doubling time $t = \ln 2 / 0.03 \approx 23$ years — the "rule of 70" ($70/k\% \approx$ years to double) is the log approximation of this solution. Note that the model must go wrong once $P$ gets large: resource constraints push the real growth rate down, and the logistic equation $\dot{P} = kP(1 - P/K)$ ($K$ is the carrying capacity) takes its place in population and environmental economics — it has a closed-form solution, and the stability of the equilibrium $K$ can be read off at a glance.

### Example 2: three fates of one second-order equation

Solve $a\ddot{y} + b\dot{y} + cy = 0$ with $a = 1$. Three parameter choices give three economic shapes:

- $\ddot{y} - 3\dot{y} + 2y = 0$: characteristic roots $1, 2$, $y = C_1 e^{t} + C_2 e^{2t}$ — both roots positive, disturbances amplify monotonely (a bubble-type divergence);
- $\ddot{y} + 3\dot{y} + 2y = 0$: characteristic roots $-1, -2$, $y = C_1 e^{-t} + C_2 e^{-2t}$ — both roots negative, monotone decay (a stable adjustment);
- $\ddot{y} + 0.4\dot{y} + 1.04y = 0$: discriminant $0.16 - 4.16 < 0$, complex roots $r = -0.2 \pm 1.0i$, $y = e^{-0.2t}(C_1 \cos t + C_2 \sin t)$ — a damped oscillation (the path of output in the multiplier-accelerator model under particular parameters, Samuelson's 1939 analysis).

The three paths share one and the same equation frame, and their fate is decided entirely by the coefficients (the structural parameters) — "changing the parameters changes the fate" is what structural reform and the design of policy rules look like in mathematics.

## Limits and what follows

First, **the assumption of certainty**: the equations of this chapter carry no stochastic term, whereas economic data are full of noise — stochastic differential equations (a Wiener process entering $\mathrm{d}P$) are the common language of finance (the Black-Scholes equation of option pricing) and of macro (shock propagation in DSGE), beyond the scope of this section. Second, **the boundary of linearisation**: the stability criteria hold only in the neighbourhood of an equilibrium point; far from equilibrium the non-linear terms take over — crises, regime switches and multiple equilibria (Keynesian coordination failure, the operating range of the Diamond-Dybvig bank run model) happen precisely in that "far from equilibrium" territory, and global analysis remains a hard problem. Third, **from the continuous to the discrete, and their complementarity**: quarterly data are discrete by nature, and difference equations (the discrete dynamics of chapter 2) are not always equivalent to continuous models in their stability conditions (the stability region of a discrete system is smaller), so empirical modelling must fix the time structure first. Fourth, **where economics goes next**: optimal control (Pontryagin's maximum principle, the continuous-time version of the Ramsey problem) upgrades "solving an equation" into "choosing the optimal solution of an equation", and dynamic programming (the Bellman equation) is its discrete twin — so differential equations are not only a tool of description but also the floor of modern macro policy evaluation (welfare analysis, and the argument between rules and discretion).
