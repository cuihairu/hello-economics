# Foundations of probability theory

> **The central question of this chapter**: when the future is uncertain, does "choice" still make sense? What mathematics lies behind buying insurance, allocating assets and funding a project? And why need not the fluctuation in a sample be a fact — how does "the random" get tamed into a quantity one can compute with?

## The arc of this chapter

Probability theory was born at the gambling table. In 1654 Chevalier de Méré wrote to Pascal asking the "problem of points" (how to divide a stake fairly when a game is interrupted), and the correspondence between Pascal and Fermat produced the concept of expected value — the first systematic literature of probability theory is a bundle of letters. Across the next two centuries Jakob Bernoulli (1655-1705)'s law of large numbers (published posthumously in 1713) turned "frequencies stabilise at probabilities" into a theorem for the first time, De Moivre and Laplace advanced the normal curve, and Gauss (1777-1855) rediscovered it in error analysis. But *what probability is* remained hotly contested (frequency? belief? a physical propensity?) — in 1933 Kolmogorov (1903-1987) axiomatised it with measure theory: probability is a function satisfying three axioms, and randomness became from then on a mathematical object that can be handled rigorously.

Economics needs probability because almost all of its decision problems are bets placed on the unknown. In 1738 Daniel Bernoulli (1700-1782), resolving the St Petersburg paradox (infinite expected value yet nobody willing to pay to play), proposed "utility decreasing in wealth, evaluated by expected utility", planting the seed of risk preference; in 1944 von Neumann and Morgenstern axiomatised it as expected utility theory, and in 1979 Kahneman (1934-2024) and Tversky (1937-1996) corrected it again with prospect theory — over three centuries probability theory and economics shaped each other: expectation, variance, conditional expectation (information), stochastic dominance, every concept corresponds to a class of economic decisions. The statistics foundation (the next chapter) is probability's return visit: inferring the parameters of a probability model from data.

The route of the chapter: the axioms of probability and the sample space; conditional probability and independence (the mathematical form of information); random variables, distributions, expectation and variance; a checklist of common distributions (from the Bernoulli to the normal — why the normal is everywhere); the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem (how randomness shows regularity).

## Core concepts

### The probability space and the three axioms

The **sample space** $S$ is the set of all possible outcomes, an **event** is a subset of the sample space, and **probability** $P(A)$ is a measure of how likely an event is, taking values in $[0,1]$. The three axioms: non-negativity ($P(A) \geq 0$), normalisation ($P(S) = 1$), countable additivity (for pairwise disjoint events, $P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} P(A_i)$). The commonest two-event version: $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$ (with $A$ and $B$ disjoint).

Why axioms rather than a definition? Because the philosophical dispute over "what probability is" (frequentist versus Bayesian, still unresolved) does not interfere with the uniqueness of "how probability is computed" — Kolmogorov's scheme deliberately sidesteps ontology and sets down the rules of operation. The payoff for economic modelling: default, recessions, whether a bill passes — so long as numbers satisfying the three axioms can be assigned, the whole machinery of expected utility, risk premia and option pricing comes online. Note "only disjoint events add": in reality, if "the benefit of policy A" and "the benefit of policy B" are not disjoint (both happen), their overlap has to be deducted before adding — probability's subtraction rule $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$ is precisely the step that repairs "the overlap being counted twice".

### Conditional probability and independence

The conditional probability is the probability of $A$ given that $B$ has happened:

$$P(A|B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \quad (P(B) > 0)$$

Independence: $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$, equivalent to $P(A|B) = P(A)$ — knowing that $B$ occurred does not change the likelihood of $A$.

Why is conditional probability "the mathematical form of information"? Because obtaining information = shrinking the sample space: from $S$ down to $B$, with every probability recomputed. Bayes' updating in finance (asset prices jumping after an earnings release), signal extraction (in Lucas's monetary illusion model the worker cannot tell an aggregate shock from a relative price shock, using exactly the conditional expectation $E(X|information)$), and credit approval (how the default probability changes once income is known) are all working floors of conditional probability. The contrary lesson of independence matters more: **mistaking correlation for independence is the classic way a risk model dies** — in the 2008 financial crisis the correlation of subprime defaults was systematically underestimated, and the independence the copula model assumed collapsed when house prices fell across the board. That is the most brutal lesson between this chapter's axioms and financial reality.

### Random variables, expectation and variance

A random variable is a function mapping every outcome of the sample space to a real number (discrete: the points on a die; continuous: a height). A discrete distribution lists the probability of each value (die: $P(X=x) = 1/6$, $x \in \lbrace 1,\ldots,6 \rbrace$); a continuous distribution is described by a probability density function, such as the normal density

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}$$

Expectation is a weighted average: $E(X) = \sum_i x_i P(X=x_i)$ (discrete) or $E(X) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$ (continuous). Variance measures dispersion: $\mathrm{Var}(X) = E[(X - E(X))^2]$, whose square root is the standard deviation.

Why is expectation "the north star of decision" and variance its shadow? Under expected utility theory a rational decision maximises expected utility — expectation is the first-order yardstick for comparing options; but it speaks only of the centre and not of the fluctuation, so at equal expectation a risk-averse agent picks the smaller variance (Markowitz's 1952 portfolio selection theory: find the efficient frontier in the mean-variance plane, with the Sharpe ratio = excess return / standard deviation). Note that expectation's rule $E(aX + bY) = aE(X) + bE(Y)$ holds unconditionally (a portfolio's expected return is a weighted average, whether or not the constituents are correlated), whereas the variance rule $\mathrm{Var}(aX + bY)$ carries an extra covariance term — **the mathematical root of diversification reducing risk is exactly that covariance term shrinking when the constituents are not perfectly correlated**, and in one sentence that is modern portfolio theory.

### A checklist of common distributions

Discrete: the **Bernoulli** (one success/failure draw, $P(X=1)=p$), the **binomial** (the number of successes in $n$ independent Bernoulli trials, $P(X=k) = \binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$), the **Poisson** (events per unit time, $P(X=k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}$). Continuous: the **uniform** (equally likely over an interval, $f(x) = \frac{1}{b-a}$), the **normal** (bell-shaped, the limiting shape of the central limit theorem), the **exponential** (the interval between events, $f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}$, $x \geq 0$, memoryless).

Why does this list cover most of economics' needs? Because each one claims a class of mechanisms: the Bernoulli claims "yes/no" decisions (employed or not, defaults or not — the base of discrete choice models); the binomial claims "the aggregation of repeated trials" (sample shares, the distribution of voters); the Poisson claims "counts of rare events" (bankruptcies, patent applications, supply interruptions); the uniform claims "ignorance as equal likelihood" (priors, random numbers); the normal claims "many small factors added together" (next section); the exponential claims "waiting times" (the duration of unemployment, the gap between two trades). Picking a distribution when modelling is not looking up a table; it is declaring "what mechanism generated the data".

### The law of large numbers and the central limit theorem

The **law of large numbers**: under a large number of independent repetitions the sample mean converges to the expectation (in a weak and a strong form). The **central limit theorem**: the sum of independent identically distributed random variables (standardised) converges to a normal — even when the original distribution is not normal.

Why are these two theorems the reason empirical research can hold at all? The law of large numbers answers "why a sample mean is trustworthy" — the credibility of survey sampling, A/B tests and historical volatility is all staked on it; the central limit theorem answers "why errors always end up bell-shaped" — measurement errors, approximate returns, the normal assumption for regression disturbances all come from "many independent small disturbances added together", not from "the world really being normal". Each theorem also draws a red line: the law of large numbers does not guarantee "small samples are stable too" (the gambler's fallacy is exactly the law misapplied to the short run); the central limit theorem requires independent identical distributions with finite variance — financial returns have fat tails and volatility clustering, so the normal assumption underestimates extreme risk, and "the black swan" is the statistical recipe of abusing the central limit theorem (on fat tails, see the next chapter and the section on limits).

## Questions and analysis

### What weakness of expectation does the St Petersburg paradox expose?

The game: flip a coin; if the first head appears on the $n$-th flip you win $2^{n-1}$. The expectation is $\sum_{n} \frac{1}{2^n} 2^{n-1} = \sum_n \frac{1}{2} = \infty$, yet nobody is willing to pay much to enter. Bernoulli's answer: people do not maximise expected money but **expected utility** — utility decreases in wealth (with a log utility the game's expected utility is finite). How far-reaching this step is: diminishing marginal utility (the second-order condition of chapter 4) is simultaneously **the source of risk aversion** — under a concave utility, a certain 500,000 is better than a coin flip between 0 and 1,000,000, and the difference is the risk premium; the insurance market (trading away a large risk for a small premium) and venture capital (trading risk for a premium) sit in one and the same explanatory frame. Expectation's weakness thereby becomes one of economics' most productive concepts.

### How do you decompose "was this run luck or skill?" with conditional probability?

Suppose a fund manager has a real stock-picking ability $p$ (the probability of beating the benchmark), and you observe $k$ consecutive years of beating it. For a manager with no ability ($p = 0.5$) the probability of $k$ straight wins, $0.5^k$, falls exponentially in $k$ — but watch the **multiplicative effect on the base rate**: among ten thousand managers, pure luck will batch-produce "five-peat champions". The conditional probability $P(\text{has ability} \mid \text{consecutive wins})$ depends on the ratio of prior (the share with real ability) to likelihood, not on the streak itself. This is the standard application of Bayesian updating, and the mathematical form of survivorship bias: count only "the managers still alive" and the denominator has been quietly withdrawn (selection bias, chapter 11). The discipline for empirical research: for any "impressive result", ask about the denominator first — how many peers tried the same thing.

## Derivations and worked examples

### Example 1: three layers of insurance pricing

A policy with annual loss probability $p = 0.02$ and loss $L = 500{,}000$ on a claim. **Expected loss** $= 0.02 \times 50 = 1$ ten-thousand — the floor of the pure premium. **Fair premium** = expected loss: with a large book of policies the insurer's receipts and payouts balance out (the law of large numbers guarantees the fluctuation is thinned out: the standard deviation of total payouts over $n = 10{,}000$ policies is $\approx \sqrt{n p(1-p)} \cdot L \approx \sqrt{196} \times 50 = 7{,}000{,}000$, only 7% of the 100-million mean). **Actual premium** > 1 ten-thousand, the difference covering administrative costs, capital occupation and a margin against adverse selection. The policyholder is willing to pay more than 1 ten-thousand because under a concave utility "a certain small loss" beats "a 2% chance of a large loss" (the risk premium). The three layers map neatly onto three roles: probability theory (expectation), statistics (fluctuation), preference theory (concave utility) — one insurance contract, half of risk economics.

### Example 2: the binomial distribution and "how big a sample is enough"

An opinion poll of $n = 1000$ respondents, with the support rate estimated at $\hat{p}$ and standard deviation $\approx \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}$ (at $p \approx 0.5$, $\approx \frac{0.5}{\sqrt{1000}} \approx 0.0158$). The $95\%$ interval is roughly $\hat{p} \pm 1.96 \times 0.0158 \approx \hat{p} \pm 3.1$ percentage points — whence the industry convention of "±3%"; only quadrupling the sample ($n = 4000$) halves the error (the $1/\sqrt{n}$ law), so "precision is expensive when bought by piling up sample" is immediately computable. The variance formula $\mathrm{Var}(\hat{p}) = \frac{p(1-p)}{n}$ is itself the direct consequence of the binomial variance divided by $n$ — sampling theory's interface is already laid down here.

## Limits and what follows

First, **independent identical distributions are an idealisation**: economic time series have volatility clustering (large events following large events), fat tails (extremes far beyond the normal's predictions) and structural breaks — the GARCH family (Engle 1982, Nobel 2003), fat-tailed distributions (Pareto tails, power laws) and regime-switching models are the standard corrections of empirical work. Second, **expected utility has been corrected by experiment**: the Allais paradox (1953) and the Ellsberg paradox (1961) hit independence and ambiguity aversion respectively, and prospect theory (1979) rewrote the axioms of choice as "reference dependence + loss aversion + probability weighting" — for which Kahneman took the 2002 Nobel Prize; "people are expected-utility maximisers" is thereby demoted from an axiom to a benchmark. Third, **the Bayesian-frequentist dispute** remains the deep fault line of statistical inference (the opening move of the next chapter); and principal components and factor models (the probability face of chapter 8's eigenvalues) turn "extracting latent variables from correlation structure" into a workhorse of empirical macro and asset pricing. Fourth, **the deep water of measure theory**: continuous-time finance (Brownian motion, Itô integrals) and rigorous probability (the axiomatic definition of conditional expectation) lie beyond this chapter, but the direction is set — every concept here is rewritten one level deeper without changing its essentials.
