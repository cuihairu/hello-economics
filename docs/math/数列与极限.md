# Sequences and limits

> **The central question of this chapter**: an economy evolves in periods — the capital stock accumulates year by year, debt rolls over term by term, prices adjust round by round. Does it settle down, or does it drift further and further away? Why can an infinite series of discounted terms converge to a finite number? One tool is enough to answer these: the limit.

## The arc of this chapter

The idea of a limit is much older than its rigour. Zeno's paradox (Achilles never overtakes the tortoise) put the puzzle of "the sum of an infinite process" on the table as early as the fifth century BC, but mathematicians only tamed it in the nineteenth century: Cauchy (1789-1857) and Weierstrass (1815-1897) supplied the $\epsilon$-$N$ language, turning "getting closer and closer" from a description of intuition into a proposition that can be proved. For the two centuries before that, calculus worked on the fuzzy intuition of infinitesimals, and Bishop Berkeley mocked infinitesimals in 1734 as "ghosts of departed quantities" — rigour is not pedantry, it is what earns an intuitive conclusion immunity from ridicule.

Economics needs limits because almost all of its central questions are long-run questions: where does capital accumulation stop (the steady state), does a rolling debt converge, is an infinite stream of discounted revenues finite. The economic variables in these questions evolve in periods by nature — $a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots$ — and a sequence is the language of discrete-time dynamics. The long-run behaviour of the capital accumulation equation in Solow's (1924-2023) growth model, and the possibility in Ramsey's (1903-1930) 1928 infinite-horizon maximisation problem that "infinitely many terms add up to a finite value", are both direct applications of this chapter's tools.

The route of the chapter: define a sequence and its two ways of writing it (explicit and recursive — a recursion is exactly the shape of an economic dynamic equation), then give the strict characterisation of convergence and divergence with their rules of operation, and finally catalogue the kinds of limit economics uses most often (geometric decay, the harmonic sequence, the compound-interest limit $e$).

## Core concepts

### Sequences and how to write them

A sequence is a list of numbers arranged in a definite order, written $a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots$ or $\lbrace a_n \rbrace$, where $a_n$ is the $n$-th term; it may be finite or infinite. There are two ways to write one: **explicitly**, by giving the general term directly, as in $a_n = n^2$; or **recursively**, by defining the current term from earlier ones, as in the Fibonacci sequence $F_1 = 1$, $F_2 = 1$, $F_n = F_{n-1} + F_{n-2}$ ($n \geq 3$).

Why is almost all economic dynamics written as a recursion? Because economic agents can only decide "how this period goes" on the basis of "where last period was": the capital accumulation equation $K_{t+1} = sF(K_t) + (1-\delta)K_t$, adaptive expectations $p^e_{t+1} = p^e_t + \lambda(p_t - p^e_t)$ — both are recursions. The explicit solution (writing $a_n$ as a function of $n$) is usually hard to find and unnecessary, **the recursion is written down as it is, and a convergence analysis happens to need only the recursive structure**. That is where the language of sequences is especially friendly to economics.

### Convergence and divergence

The limit of a sequence $\lbrace a_n \rbrace$ is $L$ if $a_n$ approaches the constant $L$ without bound as $n$ tends to infinity, written $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = L$. The formal definition: for every positive $\epsilon$ there exists a positive integer $N$ such that $|a_n - L| < \epsilon$ for all $n > N$. If such a limit exists the sequence is said to converge, otherwise it diverges — $\lbrace 1/n \rbrace$ converges to 0, while $\lbrace n \rbrace$ diverges.

Why does the $\epsilon$-$N$ definition earn a place in an economist's toolkit? Because it turns "it will settle down in the long run" from an adjective into a theorem. The $N$ in the definition answers "how many periods before the error is negligible" — for a discounted sum it tells you how large the error is when you truncate at period fifty; for policy discussion, a claim like "the economy will return to potential output" is only a wish, not a conclusion, if no limit $L$ can be written down for it. Judging divergence matters just as much: if the adjustment process is divergent, a disturbance near the equilibrium point is amplified, so the so-called equilibrium is never actually reached in reality — that is where the whole meaning of dynamic stability comes from.

### Rules of operation for limits and the squeeze theorem

If a limit exists it is unique. Let $\lim a_n = A$ and $\lim b_n = B$; the four operations may be applied term by term:

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} (a_n \pm b_n) = A \pm B, \qquad
\lim_{n \to \infty} (a_n \cdot b_n) = A \cdot B, \qquad
\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{a_n}{b_n} = \frac{A}{B} \ (B \neq 0)
$$

Squeeze theorem: if $a_n \leq b_n \leq c_n$ for all $n$ and $\lim a_n = \lim c_n = L$, then $\lim b_n = L$.

Why remember the squeeze theorem separately? Because it is the universal vice-grip for "a complicated quantity squeezed between two simple ones": proving that "the growth rate of output per head is squeezed between the rate of technical progress and the growth rate of population", that "the real interest rate is squeezed between the nominal rate and the nominal rate minus inflation", and the key step in the convergence of a discounted sum below (the discount factor $(1+r)^{-n} \le 1$ keeps the infinite sum inside a finite multiple) — all use it.

### The harmonic sequence and the divide between sequences and series

The sequence $\left\lbrace \frac{1}{n} \right\rbrace$ converges to 0, but the **series** $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n}$ diverges. There is no contradiction: the first question is "does the $n$-th term go to zero", the second is "is the sum of infinitely many terms finite" — terms going to zero is a necessary condition for convergence, not a sufficient one.

Why is this divide so important for economics? Because it separates "the effect of one item is negligible" from "the overall effect is negligible": pollution in one period, a one-off deficit, a single bank's risk exposure can all tend to zero like $1/n$, but if it keeps happening every period, the sum (the series) may still diverge — that is exactly the structure of cumulative environmental costs across generations and of rolling public debt. To judge whether an infinite process has a finite answer you must always ask about the series, not only about the sequence.

### The compound-interest limit and the constant $e$

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \left( 1 + \frac{1}{n} \right)^n = e$$

This is the mathematical identity of "continuous compounding": at an annual rate $r$, the principal-and-interest factor with interest credited $n$ times a year is $\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^n$, which tends to $e^r$ as $n \to \infty$. Two related limits in common use: $\lim \frac{n}{n+1} = 1$; $\lim \frac{1 + 2 + \cdots + n}{n^2} = \frac{1}{2}$ (substitute the summation formula $n(n+1)/2$ for the numerator and it falls out at once).

Why is $e$ everywhere in economics? Because every "interest-on-interest" structure — continuous compounding, exponential growth $P(t) = P_0 e^{kt}$, the continuous-time discount factor $e^{-\rho t}$ — is a variant of this limit. Converting between discrete and continuous models uses it as the bridge too (continuous compounding at 8% a year is equivalent to $e^{0.08} \approx 1.0833$, i.e. about 8.33% annualised, read on a compounding basis).

## Problems and analysis

### Why can an infinite-horizon discounted sum possibly be finite?

Let each period's revenue be 1 and the discount factor be $(1+r)^{-1}$ ($r > 0$); the infinite sum is $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (1+r)^{-n}$. There are infinitely many terms, but each decays geometrically — the $n$-th term goes to 0 and decays fast enough. This is exactly the case for the infinite geometric series formula of the next chapter, $S = \frac{a}{1-r'}$ (ratio $r' = \frac{1}{1+r} < 1$): the sum is $\frac{1}{r}$ — a perpetuity of 1 a year at 5% has a present value of exactly 20. The key to convergence lies not in finitely many terms but in **decaying faster than the critical rate**; if revenue grows at a fixed rate $g$, the sum still converges as long as $g < r$ (the condition for applying the Gordon model $P = \frac{D_1}{r-g}$), and as soon as $g \ge r$ it diverges — "the growth rate cannot stay above the discount rate" is not financial dogma, it is the direct requirement of series convergence.

### How do you tell whether a recursive dynamic process converges to a steady state?

Take adaptive expectations $p^e_{t+1} = p^e_t + \lambda(p_t - p^e_t)$, with $p_t$ already settled at $\bar{p}$. It is a geometric decay structure on the forecast error $(p_t - p^e_t)$: each period shrinks the error by the factor $(1-\lambda)$, so the forecast converges to the actual value when $|\;1-\lambda\;| < 1$ (that is, $0 < \lambda < 2$). The procedure generalises: linearise the recursion near the steady state and check whether the absolute value of the scaling factor is below 1 — that is the discrete-time prototype of the stability criterion in chapter 6's differential equations and chapter 8's eigenvalue analysis. Linearisation can distort (large departures hand control to the nonlinear terms), so "local stability" and "global stability" must be claimed separately — a caution that recurs throughout the later chapters.

## Derivations and worked examples

### Example 1: the critical interest rate for the debt ratio

Let the deficit be a share $d$ of GDP (new debt each period is $d \cdot Y$), output grow at $g$, and the interest rate be $r$. The evolution of the debt ratio $b_t = B_t / Y_t$ is:

$$b_{t+1} = \frac{(1+r)\,b_t + d}{1+g}$$

This is an affine recursion in $b_t$; the steady state is $b^* = \frac{d}{1+g - (1+r)} = \frac{d}{g - r}$ (requiring $g > r$). The steady-state expression shows: when $g > r$ the debt ratio converges to a finite value; when $g \le r$ no finite steady state exists and the debt ratio climbs without limit. The gap between $r$ and $g$ in the major advanced economies of the 2020s has swung around zero (take the latest figures from the IMF Fiscal Monitor), which is why the "$r - g$ debate" has become the central parameter of the contemporary literature on fiscal sustainability — its mathematical root is this one line of recursion.

### Example 2: the limit of the discount factor

Verify that the discount-factor sequence $\lbrace (1 + r)^{-n} \rbrace$ ($r > 0$) converges to 0: for any $\epsilon > 0$ we need $(1+r)^{-n} < \epsilon$; taking logs on both sides gives $n > \frac{-\ln \epsilon}{\ln(1+r)}$. So it suffices to take $N = \left\lceil \frac{-\ln \epsilon}{\ln(1+r)} \right\rceil$. Read off the practical meaning: at 5% interest, $\ln(1.05) \approx 0.049$, and requiring the discount factor to fall below 0.01 (that is, treating effects beyond one year as negligible) gives $N \approx \frac{4.6}{0.049} \approx 94$ periods. That "94 years before it is negligible" is long by intuition, and it is a warning that the trade-off over long-horizon projects cannot rest on truncation alone — it needs the complete discounted sum.

## Limits and what comes next

First, **a limit is only applicable when there really are enough periods**: the $N$ in the $\epsilon$-$N$ definition can be large (94 periods in the example above), and forcing an infinite limit onto a finite-horizon problem distorts it — the overlapping-generations models (Samuelson 1958, Diamond 1965) exist precisely because "an infinite-horizon agent" and "overlapping finite-lifetime generations" can give qualitatively different conclusions. Second, **the discrete-continuous fork**: this chapter evolves period by period, and the continuous-time version is taken over by chapter 6's differential equations; the two differ in their stability criterion (discrete time looks at the absolute value of the factor, continuous time at the sign of the real part), and mixing them yields the wrong stability condition. Third, **limits under uncertainty**: the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem (chapter 10) answer "does the average of a random sequence converge", which is closer to real data than the deterministic convergence of this chapter; stochastic dynamic programming (the Bellman equation) combines the recursion with an expectation operator and becomes the standard language of modern macro (real business cycles, DSGE). The summation formulas of the next chapter fill in "period by period accumulation", forming the other hand of the discrete dynamics that this chapter's "period by period evolution" makes up.
