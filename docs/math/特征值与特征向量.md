# Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

> **The central question of this chapter**: when a dynamic economic system is disturbed, does it glide back to equilibrium smoothly, oscillate and damp out, or drift further and further away? The answer is not in the trajectory itself — it is hidden in a few "special directions" of the system matrix. An eigenvector is a direction that change does not rotate; an eigenvalue is the scaling rate along that direction. How do you find them, and how do you read stability and oscillation from their signs?

## The historical position of this chapter's core concepts

The eigenvalue problem was born at two physical work sites of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Studying the rotation of rigid bodies, Euler found that every rigid body has "principal axes"; Cauchy (1789-1857) proved that the eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix are all real and that its eigenvectors can be taken orthogonal — and principal-axis theory was thereby settled. Jordan and Frobenius (1849-1917) completed the canonical forms for general matrices. The theorem that matters most for economics is the Perron-Frobenius theorem, established by Perron (1880-1975) and Frobenius between 1907 and 1912: **a non-negative matrix always has a non-negative largest eigenvalue (the spectral radius) with a corresponding non-negative eigenvector** — and the matrices of economic systems (input-output, transition probabilities, multiplier-accelerator structures) happen to be entirely non-negative, so this theorem guarantees that a "dominant eigenvalue" always exists in an economic model and always has economic meaning. The solvability of the Leontief input-output model (spectral radius below 1) rests directly on this theorem, and Samuelson's 1939 multiplier-accelerator model tells the story of the business cycle with eigenvalues; as for computing the eigenvalues of large matrices, the standard solution is numerical iteration (the QR algorithm and its kin).

## The arc of this chapter

The definition of the eigenproblem is naïve enough to fit in one sentence: for a square matrix $A$, if there exists a non-zero vector $\mathbf{v}$ and a scalar $\lambda$ with

$$A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$$

then $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue and $\mathbf{v}$ the corresponding eigenvector — $A$ acting on $\mathbf{v}$ only scales, without rotating it. Why is this single property enough to govern dynamic analysis? Because the general solution of a linear system expands completely along these special directions: the solution of $\mathbf{x}_{t+1} = A\mathbf{x}_t$ is $\mathbf{x}_t = \sum_k c_k \lambda_k^t \mathbf{v}_k$ — the fate of every component is governed only by the corresponding $\lambda_k$: $|\lambda_k| < 1$ decays, $= 1$ stays, $> 1$ explodes; complex eigenvalues give oscillation. So "is the system stable?" becomes "is the spectral radius below 1?" — a judgement about a single scalar.

The route of the chapter: the characteristic equation and a three-step algorithm (build — find roots — solve for vectors); a property checklist (trace, determinant, similarity invariance, positive-definiteness criteria); the special status of symmetric matrices; and finally the two home grounds of economics — dynamic stability (discrete and continuous) and the qualitative test of quadratic forms (the second-order condition of optimisation).

## Core concepts

### The characteristic equation and the three-step solution

Finding eigenvalues requires solving the characteristic equation

$$\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$$

($I$ the identity matrix); for each eigenvalue $\lambda$, solve the homogeneous linear system $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ to obtain the eigenvector. Three steps: build the determinant, solve the polynomial equation, substitute back to solve for the vector.

Why is the fact that the characteristic equation is a polynomial good enough? A $2 \times 2$ matrix gives a quadratic, a $3 \times 3$ a cubic — the degree grows with the order of the matrix, and beyond the fifth degree there is in general no root formula (Abel-Galois theory), which is exactly why numerical methods (QR iteration) take over high-dimensional problems. In today's high-dimensional economic models, eigenvalues are computed by hand only in small systems (two sectors, two variables), but **working a small system by hand is the only channel into understanding the numerical output of a large one** — the eigenvalues a piece of software prints out need someone who knows what they mean.

### Trace, determinant and eigenvalues

The sum of the eigenvalues equals the trace of the matrix (the sum of the diagonal elements), and their product equals the determinant: $\sum \lambda_i = \mathrm{tr}(A)$, $\prod \lambda_i = \det(A)$. Similar matrices ($B = P^{-1}AP$) have the same eigenvalues; a symmetric matrix $A$ is positive definite if and only if all its eigenvalues are positive.

Why do these two "small properties" show up so often in economics? Because they let a stability judgement skip solving the equation altogether: a $2 \times 2$ discrete system $\mathbf{x}_{t+1} = A\mathbf{x}_t$ is stable (spectral radius below 1) if and only if $|\mathrm{tr}(A)| < 1 + \det(A) < 2$ (the Schur three-determinant criterion) — look at only two numbers, the trace and the determinant, and never solve the characteristic equation. The positive-definiteness test is the matrix version of chapter 12's second-order condition: testing an extremum of a function of several variables asks whether the Hessian matrix (of second partial derivatives) is positive or negative definite, and "all eigenvalues have the same sign" is precisely "concave in every direction, or convex in every direction".

### The privileges of symmetric matrices

The eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix are all real, and there exists an orthogonal set of eigenvectors (the spectral theorem); the rotation matrix $R(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$ has no real eigenvector when $\theta \neq 0, \pi$ (it rotates everything), its eigenvalues over the complex numbers being $e^{\pm i\theta}$.

Why is symmetry worth a whole theorem? Because a covariance matrix (chapter 11) is necessarily symmetric — and all of principal component analysis (PCA) draws its legitimacy from the spectral theorem: the main directions of data variation are exactly the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, and the contributions are exactly the eigenvalues; "dimensionality reduction" = keeping the directions of the large eigenvalues and discarding the directions of the small ones. The rotation matrix is the reminder of the other face: **having no real eigenvectors does not mean the system ceases to exist**, only that there is no direction which "does not rotate" — the economic meaning of an oscillating system (complex eigenvalues) unfolds in the next section.

### Spectral radius and the Perron-Frobenius theorem

The spectral radius of a matrix is $\rho(A) = \max_k |\lambda_k|$. The spectral radius of a non-negative matrix (all elements $\geq 0$) is necessarily an eigenvalue, with a non-negative eigenvector (strictly positive when irreducible).

Why is this theorem tailor-made for economics? Economic matrices (input-output coefficients, Markov transition matrices, the intertemporal structure of overlapping generations) are naturally non-negative, so "the largest eigenvalue" is automatically real, non-negative, and paired with a non-negative "dominant mode": in input-output theory $\rho(A) < 1$ is equivalent to the system being solvable (each unit of output consumes at most one unit of intermediate goods — an economic necessity); in a Markov chain $\rho = 1$ corresponds to the long-run distribution (the steady-state probability vector being its eigenvector); the periodicity of the multiplier-accelerator model is driven by the sub-dominant eigenvalue (the complex conjugate pair). Everything more complicated beyond the Perron root recedes into the secondary modes.

## Questions and analysis

### How do the stability criteria for discrete and continuous systems differ?

Discrete $\mathbf{x}_{t+1} = A\mathbf{x}_t$: stability requires every $|\lambda_k| < 1$ (scaled down every period); continuous $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = A\mathbf{x}$: stability requires every $\mathrm{Re}(\lambda_k) < 0$ (continuous decay as $e^{\lambda t}$). The same coefficient matrix moved into a different time structure faces a different criterion — the discrete stability region is strictly smaller. The economic reading: the frequency of adjustment itself affects stability — with the same market structure, "repricing daily" may be stable while "repricing quarterly" oscillates (the cobweb model is the standard example: supply lags by one period, and the convergence condition is supply elasticity below demand elasticity, which becomes precisely $|\lambda| < 1$ once the coefficients are written down). Asking "what is the time structure?" before empirical modelling is not pedantry; it is a question of whether you picked the right criterion.

### Where does oscillation come from? — the multiplier-accelerator example

Samuelson's 1939 model writes national income as $Y_{t+1} = cY_t + v(Y_t - Y_{t-1})$ ($c$ the marginal propensity to consume, $v$ the accelerator). Converted to a second-order difference equation, the characteristic equation is $\lambda^2 - (c+v)\lambda + v = 0$. Apply the Jury criterion (the trace-and-determinant version of the three stability conditions): $1 - (c+v) + v > 0$, $1 - v > 0$, $1 + (c+v) + v > 0$ (the last holds automatically). Take $c = 0.8$ and $v = 0.5$: the first gives $1 - 1.3 + 0.5 = 0.2 > 0$ ✓, $1 - v = 0.5 > 0$ ✓, so the system is stable; the characteristic roots are $\lambda = \frac{1.3 \pm \sqrt{1.69 - 2}}{2} = 0.65 \pm 0.278i$, with modulus $\sqrt{0.5} \approx 0.71 < 1$ — a **damped oscillation**: the multiplier and the accelerator push each other and generate a cycle, and damping makes it converge. Push the parameters past the threshold ($v$ growing so that $1 - v$ turns negative, and the modulus crosses 1), and the oscillation amplifies instead — the hypothesis of an endogenous business-cycle mechanism grows exactly on this parameter plane.

## Derivations and worked examples

### A complete $2 \times 2$ computation

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$$

Characteristic equation: $\det(A - \lambda I) = (2-\lambda)^2 - 1 = \lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = 0$, giving $\lambda_1 = 1$ and $\lambda_2 = 3$. Check trace and determinant: $2 + 2 = 4 = 1 + 3$ ✓, $4 - 1 = 3 = 1 \times 3$ ✓.

$\lambda = 1$: $(A - I)\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} \Rightarrow \mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$; $\lambda = 3$: $\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} \Rightarrow \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

The economic reading: take $\mathbf{v}_1$ and $\mathbf{v}_2$ as two kinds of "input combination" and $A$ as the technology. Along $\mathbf{v}_1$ (a hedged combination in which one input rises as the other falls) output is unchanged ($\lambda = 1$), while along $\mathbf{v}_2$ (a combination that moves together) output is tripled ($\lambda = 3$) — the long-run fate of the system is dominated by the direction of the largest eigenvalue, which is the Perron root in concrete form: **find the principal direction and you have found the system's amplifier**.

### The qualitative test of a quadratic form

The quadratic form $q(x, y) = 2x^2 + 2xy + 2y^2$ has the matrix $H = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ (the same as in the previous example). Its eigenvalues, 1 and 3, are all positive, so $H$ is positive definite and $q > 0$ (away from the origin) — if $q$ is the second-order approximation of some function, that stationary point is a minimum. The dividend of symmetry is cashed in right here: no need to solve the equation at all, the leading principal minors $2 > 0$ and $4 - 1 = 3 > 0$ (Sylvester's criterion) already settle positive definiteness — the standard check in chapter 12's second-order condition for optimisation.

## Limits and what follows

First, **the locality of linearisation**: the eigenvalue criteria apply only to linear systems (or to a linearisation near an equilibrium point); in non-linear regions far from equilibrium there may be multiple attractors or chaos (the one-dimensional logistic map $x_{t+1} = rx_t(1-x_t)$ enters chaos through period-doubling bifurcations as $r$ grows), so the binary "stable or unstable" is not enough in the non-linear world. Second, **time variation and randomness**: when the coefficients change over time (technological change, institutional change) the eigenvalues are no longer constants, and one needs time-varying systems and random matrix theory; the Blanchard-Kahn condition used to solve DSGE models is in essence a counting requirement on the number of eigenvalues of a forward-looking system. Third, **numerical work at high dimension**: modern applications (thousand-sector input-output, large networks) rely on iterative algorithms and randomisation, and the "interpretation of meaning" of eigenvalues (which is the dominant mode, how strong the damping, how long the period) remains the economist's job. Fourth, the link to the next chapter: the spaces spanned by eigenvectors and the geometry of similarity transformations receive their coordinate-system-level explanation in "Linear spaces and linear transformations" — eigendecomposition is not a trick, it is a change of basis.
