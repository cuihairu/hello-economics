# Foundations of statistics

> **The central question of this chapter**: data contain facts, and also noise — how do you describe without distorting? When may an observed relationship be taken as real (rather than a random coincidence)? And does a significant regression coefficient mean a causal claim has been established?

## The arc of this chapter

The two streams of statistics met in the nineteenth century. One was **errors and estimation**: Legendre published least squares in 1805 and Gauss (1777-1855) followed with the theory of normal errors and optimal estimation — the mathematical tradition of "averaging away the errors, approaching the truth". The other was **social data**: Quetelet (1796-1874) applied the normal curve to human height and crime rates (the "average man"), Galton (1822-1911), studying the heights of parents and children, discovered "regression toward the mean" and invented the correlation coefficient, and Pearson (1857-1936) systematised correlation and the chi-square test. In the twentieth century Fisher (1890-1962), in agricultural experiments, established the tradition of hypothesis testing, analysis of variance and significance, and Neyman (1894-1981) and E. Pearson added the alternative hypothesis and the two kinds of error — the inference framework of today's textbooks was already in place by the 1930s.

Economics' take-up came later than one would guess: only in the 1930s did Tinbergen (1903-1994) and Frisch (1895-1973) put statistical methods to systematic use in testing economic theory (they shared the first Nobel Prize in economics in 1969, and Frisch coined "econometrics"); Haavelmo's (1911-1999) 1944 doctoral thesis established probability theory as the foundation of econometrics (the identification problem, random disturbances entering structural equations), winning the Nobel in 1989. From then on, economics completed the turn from "diagrams and examples" to "models and tests" — today every empirical paper has this chapter for a skeleton: description → inference → regression.

The route of the chapter: descriptive statistics (data types, central tendency, dispersion, graphs); inferential statistics (estimation, hypothesis testing, the two errors, the common tests); regression analysis (simple and multiple linear regression, least squares); and finally a straight answer to the sharpest question in economics — the distance between significant and causal.

## Core concepts

### Data types and descriptive statistics

Qualitative data describe categories (gender, industry); quantitative data describe quantities (income, output). Central tendency: the mean $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$, the median (the middle value when sorted; with an even count, the average of the middle two), the mode (the most frequent). Dispersion: variance and standard deviation ($\mathrm{SD}(X) = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)}$), the range $\max - \min$. Graphs: histograms (frequencies per bin) and box plots (median, quartiles Q1/Q3, extremes and outliers).

Why must the mean and the median be read as a pair? Because in a long-tailed distribution such as income, the right tail pulls the mean up while the median holds its ground in the middle — the public argument over "average wages rose while most people's did not" is, at bottom, a disagreement between two summary statistics being treated as a dispute over facts. Hence the reporting norm: for skewed data report the median (or both), always with a measure of dispersion; a news item that reports only the mean has flattened a whole distribution into a point. And the difference between the population and sample formulas for variance ($\frac{1}{n}$ and $\frac{1}{n-1}$) should not be treated as mysticism either: $\frac{1}{n-1}$ (Bessel's correction) compensates for the one degree of freedom lost by substituting the sample mean for the population mean, making the estimate unbiased — you will meet that ledger of degrees of freedom again in chapter 12's constrained optimisation.

### Estimation and hypothesis testing

Point estimation estimates a population parameter by a sample statistic (a sample mean estimating a population mean); interval estimation gives an interval covering the parameter at a stated confidence level (a confidence interval). Hypothesis testing: the null $H_0$ (the data are consistent with the hypothesised model) against the alternative $H_1$; the p value is "the probability of obtaining data as extreme as, or more extreme than, the observed data if $H_0$ is true", and below the significance level (by convention 0.05) $H_0$ is rejected. The two kinds of error: type I (a true $H_0$ rejected — a false alarm) and type II (a false $H_0$ not rejected — a miss).

Why is the p value "the most misread number in the world"? Three commandments: the p value is not "the probability that $H_0$ is true" (only a Bayesian posterior is that); "not significant" is not "no difference" (it may be insufficient power, i.e. a type II error); and p < 0.05 does not mean "truth discovered", it means "worth pursuing". The magnified lesson of empirical economics is the multiple-testing problem: test 20 variables in one study and pure luck will "significantly" find about one of them ($1 - 0.95^{20} \approx 64\%$) — the soil from which p-hacking and the replication crisis grew (after economics' large-scale replication wave of the 2010s, leading journals came to require pre-registration and multiple-comparison corrections such as the Romano-Wolf test). A checklist of common tests: the t test (differences of means; one-sample / independent / paired), the chi-square test (fitting categorical distributions), analysis of variance ANOVA (comparing means across three or more groups) — all share the one logic: the statistic's distribution under $H_0$ is known, and extreme values mean rejection.

### Simple and multiple linear regression

The model $Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \epsilon$ ($\epsilon$ the error term); least squares estimates $\beta_0, \beta_1$ by minimising the residual sum of squares; the multiple version is $Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \cdots + \beta_p X_p + \epsilon$. Uses: prediction and identifying relationships between variables.

Why is regression the "main weapon" of empirical economics? Because it does three jobs at once: measurement (one marginal effect — $Y$ moves by how much when $X$ moves one unit; the empirical version of chapter 4's derivative), control (in multiple regression $\beta_1$ is the effect "holding other variables constant" — the computable realisation of ceteris paribus), and prediction (extrapolating the fitted surface). But its boundary must be written on one's forehead too: **a regression coefficient is a conditional correlation, not a cause** — the coefficient of years of schooling on earnings is mixed up with "ability", the confounder leaking in. To identify causation you need a randomised experiment (RCT — the field of the 2019 Nobel Prize) or a quasi-experimental design (instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, difference-in-differences — the field of the 2021 Nobel Prize). "Significant" answers "is this relationship distinguishable from noise"; "causal" answers "would intervening on $X$ change $Y$" — and the gulf between those two questions is the entire subject matter of the contemporary literature on methodology.

## Questions and analysis

### When may an observed relationship be taken as real?

Pass four gates in turn. First, **statistical significance** — with the coefficient's standard error in hand the t statistic is large enough and the p value clears the bar (otherwise even "correlation" cannot stand). Second, **economic significance** — the magnitude of the coefficient means something ("one more year of education adds 0.0001% to earnings" is trivial however significant it is), and magnitudes must be read in the units of the business. Third, **robustness** — change the sample, change the specification, add controls: does the conclusion survive (a coefficient significant in only one specification is mostly luck). Fourth, **identification** — have confounders, reverse causation and selection bias been dealt with? Angrist's and Imbens's (Nobel 2021) methodological revolution can be summarised as turning the fourth gate from "begging the referee to believe" into "a replicable design". One honest lesson beyond the four gates: in real research, conclusions that clear all four are still revised as the data are updated — "real" means temporarily real, with a stated level of confidence.

### How does survivorship bias distort economic data?

Count only the individuals that survived to the sampling moment and the denominator loses everyone who dropped out on the way — mutual fund returns (failed funds are liquidated and disappear, so the industry average is systematically overestimated; academic estimates put it at around 0.5-1 percentage point a year; take the exact reading from the latest research), the analysis of bullet holes in returning WWII aircraft (Professor Wald: the parts to reinforce are the ones *without* holes — planes hit in a vital spot never came back), and studies of entrepreneurial success (counting only the unicorns that lived). What to do about it: track from the moment of entry into the sample (an entry cohort) so that exits become visible; or model the selection equation explicitly with Heckman's two-step method (1979) — which is itself a regression technique built to "pay for the bias". Whenever data are recorded as a "current stock" rather than a "historical flow" (firms, loans, marriages, app users), ask first: where did the dead ones go?

## Derivations and worked examples

### Example 1: the least squares slope formula and how to read it

The slope estimate of simple regression:

$$\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2} = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(x, y)}{\mathrm{Var}(x)}$$

It splits "slope" into two pieces: covariance (how they co-move) divided by the variance of the regressor (a standardisation). Numerical example: a survey of commute times and reported happiness in ten communities of one locality (simulated data), with $\mathrm{Cov} = -2.4$ and $\mathrm{Var}(x) = 1.2$, gives $\hat{\beta}_1 = -2$ — each extra hour of commuting goes with a happiness score 2 points lower on average (other things equal). Note the three requirements of a correct reading: the units ("points per hour"), the condition ("other variables constant"), and the direction (the negative sign). $\hat\beta_0 = \bar{y} - \hat\beta_1\bar{x}$ gives the intercept. A large denominator $\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2$ (ample variation in the regressor) makes the estimate more stable — "the variation of the explanatory variable is the fuel of identification" is a sentence that becomes literal truth in the instrumental variables lesson further down.

### Example 2: a complete t test

Policy evaluation: after a training programme, the treated group of 25 has an average hourly wage of 32 with a standard deviation of 6; the control group of 25 has 29 with a standard deviation of 5. Test $H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2$. The pooled standard error:

$$SE = \sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}} = \sqrt{\frac{36}{25} + \frac{25}{25}} = \sqrt{2.44} \approx 1.56$$

$t = \frac{32 - 29}{1.56} \approx 1.92$, with degrees of freedom $\approx 48$ and a critical value (0.05, two-sided) $\approx 2.01$ — so $|t| = 1.92 < 2.01$ and $H_0$ is not rejected: at conventional significance levels the difference cannot stand. But economic significance cautions otherwise: 3 per hour (about 10%) is not a small gap, and the likelier reading is that **the sample is too small** (underpowered — a high risk of type II error) rather than "no effect". Enlarging the sample or moving to a stronger design (random assignment) is the correct next step, not declaring "training is useless". The whole lesson of this example: when a test comes back insignificant, compute the power before you conclude.

## Limits and what follows

First, **normality and independence are routinely violated**: economic data are heavy-tailed, correlated and subject to structural breaks, so robust standard errors (White/HAC), clustered standard errors and the bootstrap are standard fittings of the modern regression table — the credibility of the "stars" depends on whether the standard errors were computed correctly. Second, **prediction and causation part ways**: machine learning methods (regularisation, random forests, deep networks) crush linear regression at pure prediction and have already entered economics (constructing proxy variables, discovering heterogeneous effects), but causal interpretation still rests on design rather than algorithms — Athey's and Imbens's causal machine learning is the emblem of that merging line. Third, **big data is not automatically good data**: the mechanism by which one enters the sample (who uses the app, who rates things online) creates new kinds of bias, and the representativeness of digital footprints (readings to be taken from the latest research) is measurement economics' most urgent task. Fourth, **from correlation to structure**: structural econometrics (estimate the model, then run counterfactual simulations), experimental economics (lab and field experiments) and natural-experiment identification are the three great roads of "inferring causation" — this chapter's least squares is their common starting point and their common point of comparison.
