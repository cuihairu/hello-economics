# Linear spaces and linear transformations

> **The central question of this chapter**: bundles of goods, price vectors, combinations of inputs — the thousands of "multidimensional quantities" under an economist's pen, on what grounds may they be added, compared and transformed together? Once "vector" is abstracted from an arrow into an axiom, what does economics gain?

## The arc of this chapter

The axiomatisation of the linear (vector) space is one of the flagship results of the abstraction movement at the end of the nineteenth century and into the twentieth. Grassmann's (1809-1877) *On the Extension of Linear Space* of 1844 put forward the first systematic theory of $n$-dimensional vectors, forty years ahead of its time and unread for decades; Peano (1858-1932) gave, in 1888, an axiomatisation close to the modern one; in the twentieth century Banach (1892-1945) and Hilbert (1862-1943) pushed the idea of space to infinite dimensions (a function is also a vector), and functional analysis was born. That stretch of abstraction, "from arrow to axiom", is the history of mathematics widening its toolkit: **for any collection of objects satisfying the eight axioms, every theorem of linear algebra applies at once**, whether the elements are pairs of numbers, polynomials or probability distributions.

Economics was among the first disciplines to profit from that abstraction. Arrow's and Debreu's 1954 existence proof for general equilibrium defined the "commodity space" as the finite-dimensional positive orthant $\mathbb{R}^n_+$: price vectors, consumption bundles, endowments and net trade are all points in this space, and preferences and budget sets are its subsets — every sentence of general equilibrium theory is spoken in the language of linear spaces. Convex analysis (the separating hyperplane theorem) then exposed the essence of "price" as a linear functional: a hyperplane supporting a convex preference set has as its normal vector exactly the equilibrium prices — the mathematical core of the second welfare theorem, and the spatial-level explanation of "shadow prices" (chapter 12). Von Neumann's 1937 growth model, Leontief's sector spaces and the state spaces of state-contingent securities (Arrow securities) are the same story.

The route of the chapter: first establish the axioms together with subspaces, bases and dimension; then linear transformations — a matrix is only the "coordinate projection" of such a transformation once a basis is chosen, which explains every rule of matrix arithmetic in chapter 7; and finally the kernel and the image (which directions are flattened, which directions are reachable), plus similarity and change of basis (one transformation, different coordinates).

## Core concepts

### The linear space

A linear space is a set $V$ (whose elements are called vectors) equipped with vector addition and scalar multiplication, satisfying eight axioms: closure and commutativity of addition ($\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}$), associativity $(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})+\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{u}+(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w})$, existence of a zero vector ($\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{u}$), existence of negatives ($\mathbf{u}+(-\mathbf{u})=\mathbf{0}$), closure under scalar multiplication together with the two distributive laws $c(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})=c\mathbf{u}+c\mathbf{v}$ and $(c+d)\mathbf{u}=c\mathbf{u}+d\mathbf{u}$, and the unit law $1\cdot\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}$.

Why loosen the definition of "vector" so far into the abstract? Because **the payoff of axiomatisation is free transfer of theorems**: once a class of objects satisfies the eight axioms, everything linear algebra concludes (existence of a basis, invariance of dimension, rank-nullity) applies immediately. Economics' "commodity space" $\mathbb{R}^n_+$: how much of each of two goods you buy can be added (combining into a new bundle) and can be scaled proportionally (scaling up), so bundles are vectors by nature. One level deeper, consumption plans under uncertainty (maps from a state space to a commodity space) and infinite-horizon consumption flows (sequence spaces) are still linear spaces at higher levels of abstraction — and it is built exactly that way that the functional-analytic version of equilibrium theory (infinite-dimensional commodity spaces) gets constructed.

### Subspaces

A subset $W$ of a linear space $V$ is called a subspace if, under the original operations, it is itself a linear space. Three checks suffice: $\mathbf{0} \in W$, closed under addition, closed under scalar multiplication.

Why are subspaces the natural shape of economic constraints? The budget set (the bundles affordable given a price vector) is not a subspace (doubling an affordable bundle need not stay affordable — it fails closure under scalar multiplication for arbitrary $c$), whereas "the zero-net-trade set", "the cone of feasible technological combinations" and "the solution set of input-output balance" all are subspaces or variants of them (cones). **Whether something is a subspace, whether it is convex, whether it is a cone** — those three questions determine the analytic properties of the constrained optimisation problem, and they are where the answer is written down for when chapter 12's Lagrange multiplier method is usable.

### Bases and dimension

A basis is a set of linearly independent vectors that spans the whole space; the dimension is the number of vectors in a basis. Example: all two-dimensional vectors $\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}$ form a two-dimensional space (standard basis $\mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2$); polynomials of degree at most $n$ form an $(n+1)$-dimensional space (basis $1, x, \ldots, x^n$).

Why should an economic model care about "dimension"? Because it counts **degrees of freedom**: the space of $n$ goods has dimension $n$, so a consumer's freedom of choice is $n$ (reduced by the budget constraint to the "dimension of relative prices", $n-1$); the counting argument in general equilibrium that "$n$ markets need only $n-1$ relative prices" is, in essence, dimension arithmetic. In the input-output model, "how many sectors' outputs can be determined independently" is set by the rank of the space in which Leontief's inverse lives — when the freedom is insufficient (the rank is deficient), the system is underdetermined and equilibrium is not unique. Dimension is also the measure by which "dimensionality reduction" becomes legitimate: PCA keeping the first $k$ principal components (chapter 8) is exactly the projection of a high-dimensional space onto a $k$-dimensional subspace, and the loss is what the discarded dimensions pay for.

### Linear transformations and their matrix representation

A linear transformation $T: V \to W$ satisfies two linearity conditions: $T(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}) = T(\mathbf{u})+T(\mathbf{v})$ and $T(c\mathbf{u}) = cT(\mathbf{u})$. Once a basis is fixed, every linear transformation corresponds to a matrix $A$ with $T(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x}$; the transformation is invertible if and only if the matrix is invertible ($T^{-1}$ gives $T(T^{-1}(\mathbf{y})) = \mathbf{y}$ and $T^{-1}(T(\mathbf{x})) = \mathbf{x}$).

Why are matrices "merely" the coordinate projection of a linear transformation? Because **the transformation itself does not depend on the coordinate system, but the matrix does** — the same rotation written in the standard basis and in a rotated basis takes two different faces, yet it is one and the same transformation. That resolves every question of chapter 7: why matrix multiplication is defined that way (composition of transformations), why it is not commutative (rotate-then-translate differs from translate-then-rotate), why associativity holds (composition can be grouped arbitrarily). The economic reading: technology (the rule from an input vector to an output vector), the tax system (the map from pre-tax to post-tax income), and expectation-formation mechanisms (the rule from historical data to a forecast) are all, in essence, linear transformations; a matrix is their bookkeeping form under a chosen accounting system (basis) — **changing the accounting system (a change of basis) changes no economic substance, only how the matrix is written**, and it is that fact the similarity transformation $B = P^{-1}AP$ states (chapter 8's "similar matrices share eigenvalues" falls out of it at a glance: the nature of the transformation does not change with the coordinates).

### The kernel and the image

The kernel of a transformation $T$ is the set of all inputs mapped to the zero vector: $\mathrm{ker}(T) = \lbrace \mathbf{x} \in V \mid T(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{0} \rbrace$; the image is the set of all attainable outputs: $\mathrm{im}(T) = \lbrace T(\mathbf{x}) \mid \mathbf{x} \in V \rbrace$. The rank-nullity theorem balances the books on both sides: $\dim(\mathrm{ker}) + \dim(\mathrm{im}) = \dim(V)$.

Why do kernel and image correspond to "loss and reachability"? The kernel is the direction that is non-zero yet gets flattened to zero — the "kernel" of a tax system is the tax-free range (a change in income that changes no tax liability), and the kernel of an input-output matrix is the redundant combinations of "producing more without creating more demand"; the image is every destination reachable after the transformation — the image of the policy toolkit is "the policy-reachable set", and an insufficient dimension of instruments (the image's dimension below the dimension of the targets) is precisely the linear-algebraic statement of Tinbergen's rule (the number of targets must not exceed the number of independent instruments). The rank-nullity theorem thereby has an economic translation: **the degrees of freedom the instruments spend (the image) plus the degrees of freedom that get flattened away (the kernel) always equal the dimension of the system — there are no free degrees of freedom**.

## Questions and analysis

### Why is a price a "hyperplane"?

In the budget constraint $\mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{x} = I$, $\mathbf{p}$ as a vector defines a plane (hyperplane) through the commodity space: all the bundles that spend the budget exactly. The geometric proof of the second welfare theorem: the "upper contour set" of an optimal allocation is convex, and it is separated from the budget hyperplane — the separating hyperplane theorem supplies the normal vector, and **normalised, the normal vector is the equilibrium price**. The economic definition of a price (a rate of exchange) and its geometric identity (the normal of a hyperplane) merge here; the mathematical form of the celebrated line "prices carry scarcity information" is "the price vector is the coefficient vector of a supporting hyperplane". That is also why shadow prices (chapter 12) look like prices at every step of constrained optimisation — they are the same kind of object.

### Why does "similarity" matter in economic accounting?

$B = P^{-1}AP$ says that $A$ and $B$ are the matrices of one and the same transformation in two different bases. The accounting counterpart is a change of classification system: switching the industry classification from the 2017 GB/T edition to the 2022 one, or the statistical treatment from production-based to expenditure-based — the data matrix changes ($B$) while the economy ($A$) does not. The economic meaning of invariance of eigenvalues under similarity: the economy's inner structural parameters (growth rates, stability, amplification factors) do not change with the way books are kept; only the "coordinates" change, that is, the layout of the statements. Recognising which quantities are "the nature of the transformation" (eigenvalues, rank, the absolute value of the determinant) and which are "products of the coordinates" (the individual elements) is the first lesson in reading any cross-country or cross-period comparison.

## Derivations and worked examples

### Example 1: using the kernel to find the blind spots of a progressive tax

A simplified tax: the taxable base is $t(x) = T(x) - x_0$ (with $x_0$ the tax-free allowance), linearised as the map $T: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, $T(x) = \tau(x - x_0)$ ($\tau$ the marginal rate). The kernel: $\mathrm{ker}(T) = \lbrace x \mid \tau(x - x_0) = 0 \rbrace = \lbrace x_0 \rbrace$ — only the single allowance point maps to zero. If "deductions" are widened into a subspace (education, mortgage interest deductible), the kernel grows in dimension: each additional dimension of $\dim(\ker)$ flattens one degree of freedom out of the tax base. The value of this toy model is to demonstrate the language: **tax preferences = a deliberately constructed kernel**, and the dimension of the kernel = the degrees of freedom by which the tax base erodes — which is exactly what "tax expenditure" estimates (published annually by finance ministries in various countries; the readings should be taken from the latest bulletin) monetise when they price that kernel.

### Example 2: a change of basis in a two-dimensional commodity space

The commodity space $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the standard basis $\mathbf{e}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ (metres) and $\mathbf{e}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ (kilograms). Switch to a "basket basis": $\mathbf{b}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{b}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}$ (two standard consumption bundles). The coordinates of the price vector $\mathbf{p} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 7 \end{pmatrix}$ in the basket basis: solve $c_1\mathbf{b}_1 + c_2\mathbf{b}_2 = \mathbf{p}$, that is $2c_1 + c_2 = 5$ and $c_1 + 3c_2 = 7$, giving $c_1 = \frac{8}{5}$ and $c_2 = \frac{9}{5}$. One price, two sets of coordinates — the CPI's "basket" idea is precisely this change of basis: converting the price coordinates of $n$ goods into the coordinates of $k$ representative consumption bundles ($k < n$), and every technical difficulty of compiling the index (which basket to choose, when to update it) is a question of "does changing basis distort?".

## Limits and what follows

First, **linearity is an approximation**: real preferences and technologies are non-linear, and the linear space is the tangent space near an equilibrium point — global analysis needs non-linear methods (fixed point theorems, homotopy methods), and the completion of the Arrow-Debreu proof rests on topology rather than on linear algebra. Second, **the boundary of finite dimensions**: intertemporal choice and uncertainty push the commodity space to infinite dimensions (continuous-time consumption flows), where inner products, duality and separation theorems all have to be re-examined (the difference between Banach and Hilbert spaces); the rigorous development of asset pricing theory (the Hansen-Richard variance relation and the like) is carried out at this level. Third, **convexity displaces linearity**: what economics really cannot do without is not linearity but convexity — separation theorems for convex sets supply prices and subdifferentials of convex functions supply shadow prices; linearisation is only the simplest special case of convex analysis. Fourth, **the turn to computation**: sparse matrices, random projections and tensor decomposition turn "dimension" from a curse into a resource (the great flip of the curse of dimensionality in big data and of "dimension as information" in high-dimensional econometrics), and it is the mathematical floor of contemporary empirical methods (machine learning entering economics). Chapter 8's eigenvalues and this chapter's change of basis meet here: the spectral theorem tells us what a good basis looks like — take the eigenvectors as the basis and a complicated system decomposes into a bundle of independent one-dimensional problems.
