# Chapter 14 · Inflation theory

> **The central question of this chapter**: How is inflation measured? What separates demand-pull from cost-push? Is there a trade-off between inflation and unemployment?

## The arc of this chapter

For a long time inflation mattered very little to modern macroeconomics: the classical quantity theory of money left the price level to the quantity of money and unemployment to real factors, and the two had nothing to do with each other. The turning point came in 1958 — Phillips found in nearly a century of British data a stable negative correlation between unemployment and the growth rate of money wages; Samuelson and Solow (1960) ported it to the United States and rewrote it as a trade-off between inflation and unemployment, and Keynesians were handed a policy "menu": a little more inflation seemed to buy a little less unemployment. The 1960s debate turned on the two ends of that menu: was inflation demand-pull (then constrict demand) or cost-push (then hold prices down)? The taxonomy in the first two sections of this chapter is the legacy of that fight.

Stagflation in the 1970s shattered both ends at once: unemployment and inflation rose together, and the menu itself stopped working. Friedman and Phelps diagnosed a missing term in the equation — expectations: in the short run one can still trade "unforeseen" inflation for employment, but once people adjust their expectations the unemployment rate returns to its natural rate and the Phillips curve becomes vertical. This chapter's price adjustment equation is the crystallisation of that debate: inflation rate = expected inflation + output-gap pressure. It folds demand-pull (the gap term) and cost-push (expectations and wage setting) into a single frame, and it hands you the arithmetic of fighting inflation — engineer a recession.

## Core concepts

### The consumer price index

The consumer price index (CPI) is the abbreviation for the consumer price level index. It reflects the movement in the price level of consumer goods (including services) and is normally compiled by weighted average.

Expressed as a formula, the consumer price index is:

$$CPI=\frac{\text{the value of a fixed basket of goods at current prices}}{\text{the value of the same fixed basket at base-period prices}}\times 100\%$$

The consumer price index is one of the indicators used to measure the degree of inflation and deflation. Its strengths are that it can quickly reflect the relative position of supply and demand for consumer goods, the data are easy to collect, and it shows price trends bearing on household life quickly and directly. Its weakness is a narrow coverage: it includes only the consumer-goods part of the final output of society, and so cannot speak to the full picture.

Beyond the narrow coverage, a fixed "basket" carries substitution bias: when prices rise, consumers switch to cheaper substitutes, so a fixed basket overstates the rise in the cost of living. The Boskin Commission's 1996 estimate put this bias at about one percentage point a year — do not dismiss it, since social security and taxation are both indexed to the CPI, and a one-point error compounding over decades is an astronomical sum.

### Demand-pull inflation

Demand-pull inflation refers to a sustained and marked rise in the general price level caused by an increase in aggregate demand.
Because aggregate demand shows up as a quantity of money, demand-pull inflation is also described as "too much money chasing too few goods." Increases in consumer demand, investment demand, government demand and foreign demand, together with government policies that stimulate those demands, all push up aggregate demand and raise the inflation rate to varying degrees. A sustained increase in aggregate demand generates demand-pull inflation.

### Cost-push inflation

Cost-push inflation, also called cost inflation or supply inflation, refers to a sustained and marked rise in the general price level caused by higher costs of supply. The cost-push factors mainly include pushes from wages and from profits. Whether it is wages or profits, if they rise faster than the general price level, they exert pressure on further increases in the prices of goods and services. Excessive pursuit of wages by trade unions and of profits by monopolists, together with the rigidity of money wages and prices, are all held to be able to bring about cost-push inflation.

### The price adjustment equation

**[Notation]**
| Symbol | English full name | Meaning |
|------|----------|----------|
| $\pi_t$ / $\pi$ | Inflation Rate | the inflation rate in period $t$ |
| $\pi_t^e$ | Expected Inflation Rate | the expected inflation rate for period $t$ |
| $Y_t$ | Actual Output / Aggregate Demand | actual output / aggregate demand in period $t$ |
| $Y_f$ / $Y^*$ | Potential Output / Full-Employment Output | potential output / full-employment output |
| $h$ | Adjustment Coefficient | the coefficient on the effect of the output gap on the inflation rate |
| $\frac{Y_t - Y_f}{Y_f}$ | Output Gap | the output gap (the deviation of aggregate demand from potential output) |

---

**Price adjustment equation**:

$$\pi_t = \pi_t^e + h \cdot \frac{Y_t - Y_f}{Y_f}$$

**[What the equation means]**

$$\text{inflation rate} = \text{expected inflation rate} + \text{the effect of the output gap}$$

| Component | Reading | Economic meaning |
|----------|------|----------|
| $\pi_t^e$ | expected inflation rate | if people expect high inflation, actual inflation rises (self-fulfilling) |
| $h \cdot \frac{Y_t - Y_f}{Y_f}$ | the effect of the output gap | demand pressure acts directly on the price level |

---

**[Output gap and inflation pressure]**

| Case | Output gap | Inflation pressure | Economic meaning |
|------|----------|----------|----------|
| **Overheating** | $Y_t > Y_f$ (positive gap) | $\uparrow$ | aggregate demand above potential output → prices rise |
| **Recession** | $Y_t < Y_f$ (negative gap) | $\downarrow$ | aggregate demand below potential output → prices fall |
| **Normal** | $Y_t = Y_f$ (no gap) | no extra pressure | actual output equals potential output |

---

**[Relation to the Phillips curve]**

Because the output gap and the unemployment rate move in **opposite directions**:

$$\text{output gap} \uparrow \leftrightarrow \text{unemployment rate} \downarrow$$

The price adjustment equation can therefore also be written as a **trade-off** between the inflation rate and the unemployment rate (the Phillips curve):

$$\pi_t = \pi_t^e - \beta(u_t - u_n)$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
|------|------|
| $u_t$ | the actual unemployment rate in period $t$ |
| $u_n$ | the natural rate of unemployment |
| $\beta$ | the coefficient on the effect of unemployment on the inflation rate |

This equation compresses the whole history of the Phillips curve: Phillips (1958) saw only a negative correlation between unemployment and wage growth in the raw data; Samuelson and Solow (1960) read it as a policy menu to choose from; Friedman and Phelps (1967–1968) pointed out that the menu was missing the term expectations — once $\pi_t^e$ adjusts to what actually happens, unemployment is pinned at $u_n$ in the long run, and the business of buying employment with inflation is closed.

### Income indexing

Income indexing is a measure a government takes when it faces cost-push inflation.
What is meant by income indexing is linking wages to some price index by a rule laid down in advance, so that when prices rise wages rise with them. For example, a government may decree that the growth rate of workers' wages equals the inflation rate plus the growth rate of output. In practice, the function of income indexing is to reduce the effect of inflation on the distribution of income.

Indexing treats the symptom and not the disease: it does not lower inflation, only makes it hurt less. The price is that inflation may get "welded" into every wage contract, making deceleration harder — which is also why central banks are cautious about across-the-board indexing.

## Problems and analysis

### How can inflation be reversed by engineering a recession? What separates a cold turkey from gradualism?

**[Notation]**
| Symbol | English full name | Meaning |
|------|----------|----------|
| $\pi_t$ | Inflation Rate at time $t$ | the inflation rate in period $t$ |
| $\pi_t^e$ | Expected Inflation Rate | the expectation for the inflation rate in period $t$ |
| $\pi_{t-1}$ | Last Period Inflation Rate | the inflation rate in the previous period |
| $Y_t$ | Actual Output at time $t$ | actual output in period $t$ |
| $Y_f$ / $Y^*$ | Potential Output | potential output |
| $h$ | Adjustment Coefficient | the coefficient on the effect of the output gap on inflation |
| $\frac{Y_t - Y_f}{Y_f}$ | Output Gap / GDP Gap | the output gap (a negative value measures the depth of recession) |

---

**[Price adjustment equation]**

$$\pi_t = \pi_t^e + h \cdot \frac{Y_t - Y_f}{Y_f}$$

**Expectation formation**: $\pi_t^e = \pi_{t-1}$ (adaptive expectations)

---

- (1) In the view of western scholars, one important cause of inflation is excess aggregate demand.
Therefore one way to bring down the inflation rate is to engineer a recession by hand. Engineering a recession both eases aggregate demand's pressure on the general price level and helps push people's expectations of inflation down.

- (2) Suppose an economy's initial inflation rate is 10%, the government wants it down, and so engineers a 5% recession.
Further suppose that people habitually form their expectations for the coming year from last year's inflation rate, and that the recession's coefficient of effect on the inflation rate is 0.4. Then the economy's price adjustment equation can be written in general form as:

$$\pi_t=\pi_t^e + h\frac{(Y_t -Y_f)}{Y_f}$$

where $\pi_t$ is the inflation rate in period $t$, $\pi_t^e$ is people's expectation of the inflation rate in period $t$, equal to $\pi_{t-1}$, $\frac{(Y_t - Y_f)}{Y_f}$ is the deviation of aggregate demand in period $t$ from the potential output level, equal to 5%, and $h$ equals 0.4.

So, under the government's policy, in the first year the expectation of inflation is 10% but the recession brings the inflation rate down to $\pi_1 = 10\% - 0.4 \times 5\% = 8\%$. By the second year, the expectation of inflation falls from 10% to 8%. If the government continues to engineer a 5% recession, that year's inflation rate is $\pi_2 = 8\% - 0.4 \times 5\% = 6\%$. Continuing in this way, the government can bring inflation to zero in five years by engineering a 5% recession each year.

- (3) The example above also shows that the larger the recession the government engineers, the faster the inflation rate falls.
This raises the question of the guiding idea behind curing inflation. The plan of achieving low inflation by engineering a large recession is vividly called a "cold turkey"; reversing inflation by engineering a gradual recession is called gradualism.

## Synthetic essay

### What causes inflation, and how should each cause be treated?

**[The three causes of inflation and how to treat them]**

| Type | Also called | Core cause | How it shows up | Treatment |
|------|------|----------|----------|----------|
| **Demand-pull** | excess-demand inflation | aggregate demand > aggregate supply | "too much money chasing too few goods" | **Engineer a recession**<br>(constrict fiscal / monetary policy) |
| **Cost-push** | supply inflation | rising costs | higher wages, costlier raw materials, monopoly profits | **Income policy**<br>(wage-price controls, indexing) |
| **Structural** | - | differences in productivity across sectors | wages in fast sectors pull slow sectors up to them | **A policy mix**<br>(coordinated adjustment) |

---

**[In detail]**

#### (1) Demand-pull inflation

**Definition**: a sustained and marked rise in the price level caused by aggregate demand exceeding aggregate supply.

**Root cause**: "too much money chasing too few goods"

| Factors that expand aggregate demand | How they show up |
|---------------------|----------|
| **Real factors** | consumer demand ↑, investment demand ↑, government spending ↑, tax cuts, net exports ↑ → the IS curve shifts right |
| **Monetary factors** | money supply ↑, money demand ↓ → the LM curve shifts right |

**Treatment**: **engineer a recession**
- lower aggregate demand → more unemployment, less output → the inflation rate falls
- the deeper the recession, the faster inflation falls

---

#### (2) Cost-push inflation

**Definition**: a rise in prices caused by higher costs of supply, in the absence of excess demand.

**Sources of rising costs**:
- rising wage levels (pushed by trade unions)
- costlier raw materials and energy
- firms chasing monopoly profits

**Treatment**: **income policy**

| Policy tool | Specific measure | Pros and cons |
|----------|----------|--------|
| **Wage-price controls** | direct controls, indicative targets, moral suasion | effective short run, but limits market mechanisms over time |
| **Income indexing** | linking wages to the price index | removes inflation's effect on the distribution of income |

---

#### (3) Structural inflation

**Definition**: inflation arising from the institutional and structural features of the economic control system.

**Core mechanism**: wages grow at different rates in sectors where productivity rises fast and where it rises slow, yet the slow sectors demand wages on a par with the fast ones → society-wide wage growth > productivity growth → inflation

**Treatment**: **use several policies together**

---

**[Summary]**

$$\text{the causes of inflation usually intertwine} \to \text{treatment must combine policies}$$

| Policy type | Where it fits | Typical tools |
|----------|----------|----------|
| **Constrictive policy** | demand-pull | raising interest rates, cutting government spending, raising taxes |
| **Income policy** | cost-push | wage-price controls, income indexing |
| **Structural adjustment** | structural | industrial policy, labour market reform |

## Derivation and worked example

### Suppose an economy's initial inflation rate is 18%. If the recession's coefficient of effect on inflation is $h = 0.4$, how can the government engineer a 10% recession to keep inflation at or below 4%?

Solution:

Take potential money income at 100 money units. Because the government engineers a 10% recession, actual income is pushed down to 90 money units.

From the price adjustment equation, $\pi_t=\pi_t^e + h\frac{(Y_t -Y_f)}{Y_f}$,

we get:

$$\pi_2=18\% + 0.4(90-100)/100=14\%$$

$$\pi_3=14\%+0.4(90-100)/100=10\%$$

$$\pi_4=10\%+0.4(90-100)/100=6\%$$

$$\pi_5=6\%+0.4(90-100)/100=2\%$$

So after four years — that is, by the fifth year — the economy can bring the inflation rate below 4%.

## Limits and what follows

- **The sacrifice ratio dispute.** How much does it cost to buy low inflation with a recession? Empirically it is measured by the "sacrifice ratio" — the share of annual GDP lost per percentage point of inflation removed, usually estimated between 2 and 5. Volcker's anti-inflation 1979–1982 confirmed that the cost is real; but Sargent's (1982) study of how four post–World War I hyperinflations ended found that once the fiscal-monetary regime changed credibly, inflation could collapse with almost no recession — the size of the cost turns on whether expectations are persuaded, which is exactly the complexity this equation erases in a single line, $\pi_t^e = \pi_{t-1}$.
- **A microfoundation for sticky prices.** Why do prices not adjust at once to demand pressure? Mankiw's (1985) "menu costs" and Akerlof and Yellen's (1985) "near rationality" offer one answer: for a single firm the savings from not adjusting are small, but aggregated across society they can produce large fluctuations. This supplies the microfoundation for the price adjustment equation and opens the line of research on the New Keynesian Phillips curve.
- **A flatter curve and anchored expectations.** From the 1990s onward countries moved one by one to inflation targeting, and once expectations were "anchored" the Phillips curve clearly flattened: in the 2010s the US unemployment rate fell to its lowest in half a century while inflation stayed put; after 2021 inflation returned, and the argument over the "transitory" misjudgment and de-anchoring of expectations resumed. The expectations term moved from a decoration in the equation to the leading character, and central bank credibility became the most important asset in fighting inflation.
