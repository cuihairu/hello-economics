# Chapter 6 · General equilibrium and welfare economics

> **The core question of this chapter**: can all markets reach equilibrium at the same time? What kind of resource allocation counts as "optimal"? Can efficiency and equity both be had?

## The arc of this chapter

Each of the previous five chapters looked at one market at a time: one commodity market, one factor market. But prices have never been determined in isolation — a rise in the price of beef pushes demand onto pork, and a rise in the price of pork pushes it back onto mutton. Walras took a bold step in 1874: writing **all** markets into a single system of simultaneous equations, so that all prices are solved for at once. This is general equilibrium. It is magnificent, and also dangerous: whether a system of equations has a solution, Walras himself only fobbed off vaguely with "the number of equations equals the number of unknowns" — this question of existence hung open for eighty years, until Wald (1930s) gave a preliminary proof and Arrow and Debreu (1954) settled it thoroughly with a fixed-point theorem (the technique of the proof was borrowed precisely from Nash equilibrium).

General equilibrium says whether markets "can all clear at once"; welfare economics then asks "and so what if they do" — is the equilibrium worth pursuing? Pareto gave a deliberately conservative criterion: there is no room left for an improvement that makes someone better off without making anyone worse off, and that is optimal. The reason this criterion caught on is precisely that it avoids interpersonal comparisons: there is no need to say how many of B's utils are worth one of A's, only to ask "is there any free improvement left?" This chapter first treats general equilibrium (Walras's law, the Edgeworth box), then the three conditions for Pareto optimality, and finally how the two join up: the general equilibrium of perfect competition is exactly Pareto optimal — Smith's "invisible hand" only took on the form of a rigorous theorem after 1951 (the two fundamental theorems of welfare economics).

## Core concepts

### Partial equilibrium and general equilibrium

- (1) Partial equilibrium refers to the market equilibrium of a particular product or factor, under the assumption that other markets are unchanged. Partial equilibrium analysis studies a single (product or factor) market, and its method is to "extract" the market under consideration from the whole set of interrelated markets in the economic system and study it in isolation.

Partial equilibrium is the equilibrium state of a single consumer, a single commodity market or factor market, a single firm or a single industry within the economic system. Partial equilibrium analysis considers only how the various factors contained in that partial segment interact and mutually influence one another, and how equilibrium is finally reached. In studying the equilibrium of a given product market, for instance, one may assume that the supply, demand and prices of all other products are unchanged, and consider only how the price and quantity sold of that product reach equilibrium through the opposing forces of its own supply and demand.

- (2) General equilibrium refers to the state in which supply and demand in all markets of an economic system reach equilibrium simultaneously. General equilibrium analysis proceeds from the behaviour of microeconomic agents and examines the conditions required for the supply and demand of every product and every factor to reach equilibrium simultaneously, together with the corresponding equilibrium prices and the values that equilibrium supply and demand quantities should take.

According to general equilibrium analysis, the price of a given commodity depends not only on its own supply and demand, but is also affected by the prices and supply-and-demand conditions of other commodities. Hence the price of a given commodity, and its supply-demand equilibrium, can be realized only when the prices and supply-demand of all commodities simultaneously reach equilibrium.

**[Partial equilibrium vs general equilibrium: a comparison]**

| Dimension | Partial equilibrium | General equilibrium |
|----------|----------|----------|
| Scope of study | A single market (product or factor) | All markets (products + factors) |
| Assumptions | Other markets assumed unchanged | Interlinks and mutual influence between markets taken into account |
| Method of analysis | "Extract" the single market from the whole and study it alone | Analyse all markets as a whole |
| Price determination | Price determined by own supply and demand only | Price determined jointly by supply and demand in all markets |
| Equilibrium condition | Single market: supply = demand | All markets simultaneously: supply = demand |
| Complexity | Simple, easy to analyse | Complex, requires a system of simultaneous equations |
| Realism | Approximate analysis, applicable when links between markets are weak | Closer to reality, but hard to solve |
| Applications | Analysis of the impact of policy on a single market | Economy-wide policy, tax reform, trade analysis |

**Illustrative examples**:

| Situation | Partial equilibrium analysis | General equilibrium analysis |
|------|--------------|--------------|
| Rise in the price of beef | Analyse the beef market only: demand↓ → price↑ | Analyse simultaneously: beef↑ → demand for pork↑ → pork↑ → demand for mutton↑… |
| A tax on petrol | Analyse the petrol market only: price↑ | Analyse simultaneously: petrol↑ → demand for cars↓ → demand for steel↓ → … |

Partial equilibrium sees "only the trees"; general equilibrium sees "both the trees and the forest". Marshall's supply-and-demand diagram is the instrument of the former, Walras's system of equations that of the latter — chapters 1 through 5 used the former throughout, and this chapter completes it.

### Walras's law

Walras's law, also called Walras's rule, is an identity proposed by the economist Walras within his theoretical system of general equilibrium for perfectly competitive markets.
Its basic content is: in a perfectly competitive market system, at any price level, the expenditure of all members of the social economy on buying goods and services must equal the income they obtain from selling goods and services, and the sum of excess demands for all commodities in the markets is zero.

$$\sum_{i=1}^{k+r} p_i \cdot x_i = \sum_{i=1}^{k+r} p_i \cdot y_i$$

The significance of this identity for general equilibrium is that it shows that, whether or not the economy is in general equilibrium, the price of one commodity can always be expressed in terms of the prices of the other commodities: when all other markets are in equilibrium, one further market must also be in equilibrium. Hence general equilibrium analysis cannot yield the (absolute) prices of all markets. But if one commodity is designated as the general equivalent (numéraire), its price is 1, and only then can general equilibrium have a determinate solution. It follows that, since this law holds, general equilibrium analysis can yield only relative prices.

It follows from Walras's law that if an economic system contains $n$ commodity markets and $n-1$ of them are in equilibrium, then the $n$-th commodity market must also be in equilibrium. Walras's law not only shows that in an exchange system the sum of consumers' excess demands for all commodities is zero at any price level; it can likewise be proved that Walras's law applies not only to a pure system of economic exchange, but also to an economic system with both production and exchange, and to a monetary economy.

### Pareto optimal states

A Pareto optimal state, also called economic efficiency, is a state in which no one can improve their own situation without making the situation of someone else worse; the state at that point is called a Pareto optimal state. When the allocation of resources in an economic system reaches a Pareto optimal state, the economy is said to be operating efficiently. Conversely, an economic outcome that does not satisfy Pareto optimality is inefficient.

The cleverness of this criterion lies in its minimalism: it does not claim that the optimal allocation is the "best", only that there is no longer a "free lunch" — every way of making someone better off has been used up, and every further improvement must be paid for by someone. This is also why it is a criterion of efficiency rather than of equity: an allocation giving all the wealth to one person can equally be Pareto optimal.

### The condition for exchange to be Pareto optimal

The condition for exchange to be Pareto optimal means that, on the side of exchange, the marginal rate of substitution between any pair of commodities is equal for every individual using the two commodities, i.e. $RCS_{12}^{A} = RCS_{12}^B$; at that point society has reached Pareto optimality in the allocation of products, and efficiency of exchange is thereby achieved. In the Edgeworth box representing pure exchange, exchange is Pareto optimal when the indifference curves of the two consumers are tangent.

### The condition for production to be Pareto optimal

The condition for production to be Pareto optimal means that, for an economy with many individuals, many commodities and many factors of production, equilibrium requires that, on the side of production, the marginal rate of technical substitution between any pair of factors be equal in the production of every commodity made using the two inputs, i.e. $RTS_{LK}^{1} = RTS_{LK}^2$; at that point society has reached a Pareto optimal state, and efficiency of production is thereby achieved. Of course, general equilibrium in production is achieved at the same time. In the Edgeworth box representing production, production is Pareto optimal when the isoquants of the two products are tangent.

### The condition for exchange and production to be Pareto optimal simultaneously

The condition for exchange and production to be Pareto optimal simultaneously means that the marginal rate of substitution between any two of all commodities equals the marginal rate of transformation between those two commodities in production, i.e. $RCS_{XY}=RPT_{XY}$. If all markets (product markets and factor markets) are perfectly competitive, the ultimate effect of the market mechanism will bring production resources to their optimal allocation. In this ideal state of Pareto optimality, limited production resources are allocated most efficiently, output is highest, and the allocation of products maximizes the overall welfare of the members of society.

If a good in the economy can be used both in production and in consumption, the condition for the economy to reach a Pareto optimal state is that the marginal rate of transformation between any two commodities equals the marginal rate of substitution between those two commodities.

### Marginal rate of transformation

The marginal rate of transformation is the rate at which the output of some commodity must be reduced in order to increase the output of another commodity.
If the change in the output of $X$ is set at $\Delta{X}$ and the change in the output of $Y$ at $\Delta{Y}$, then the absolute value of their ratio, $\left|\Delta{Y}/\Delta{X}\right|$, measures the rate at which one unit of commodity X is transformed into commodity Y.

The limit of that ratio is defined as the marginal rate of transformation of commodity X with respect to commodity Y, $MRT$, that is:

$$M R T=\lim_{\Delta x \rightarrow 0}\left|\frac{\Delta Y}{\Delta X}\right|=\left|\frac{\mathrm{d} Y}{\mathrm{d} X}\right|$$

The marginal rate of transformation reflects the opportunity cost of transforming products. On the production possibility frontier, the marginal rate of product transformation appears as the absolute value of the slope of the frontier.

## Problems and analysis

### What is the basic idea of Walrasian general equilibrium? Where does the "existence" problem get stuck?

- (1) General equilibrium analysis studies all the interrelated markets as a single whole, and its basic idea is to generalize the equilibrium condition of a single market to multiple markets. The first problem that general equilibrium theory sets out to solve is: does there exist a set of prices at which all markets are simultaneously in equilibrium — the so-called question of the existence of general equilibrium.

- (2) The French economist Walras built, in 1874, the analytical framework that later generations call Walrasian general equilibrium. This framework assumes that the whole economy contains $k$ kinds of product and $r$ kinds of factor of production. The economy then contains $k$ product markets and $r$ factor markets.

- ① Examining the equilibrium of product markets: demand for products comes from consumers.
  For a given commodity, consumers' demand depends on the price of the commodity itself, the prices of other commodities and consumers' income, among other things. But consumers' income in turn derives from the prices of factors of production. A commodity's market demand therefore depends on the prices of all products and factors, i.e.:
    $$x_i=x_i(p_1,\cdots,p_k,p_{k+1},\cdots,p_{k+r}),\ (i=1,\cdots,k)$$

  The supply of products comes from firms. A firm's supply likewise depends on the price of the commodity itself, the prices of other commodities and costs, while the firm's costs are in turn determined by factor prices. A product's market supply therefore also depends on the various prices, i.e.:

  $$y_i=y_i(p_1,\cdots,p_k,p_{k+1},\cdots,p_{k+r}),\ (i=1,\cdots,k)$$

  When market demand equals market supply, the market for a product is in equilibrium, i.e.:
  $$x_i=y_i,\ (i=1,\cdots,k)$$

- ② Examining the equilibrium of factor markets. Demand for factors is the firms' derived demand; it depends not only on the prices of the various factors but also on the prices of other products, i.e.:
  $$x_j=x_j(p_1,\cdots,p_k;p_{k+1},\cdots,p_{k+r}),\ j=k+1,\cdots,k+r$$

  The supply of factors comes from consumers, and is a generalization of utility maximization:
  $$y_j=y_j(p_1,\cdots,p_k;p_{k+1},\cdots,p_{k+r}),\ j=k+1,\cdots,k+r$$

  When market demand equals market supply, the factor market is in equilibrium, i.e.:
  $$x_j=y_j,\ j=k+1,\cdots,k+r$$

- ③ If there exist $p_1,\cdots,p_k$ and $p_{k+1},\cdots,p_{k+r}$ such that the equilibrium conditions of the above $k+r$ markets hold, then general equilibrium exists. The problem reduces to whether the $k+r$ equations can be solved for the $k+r$ prices. Walras originally believed this depended on whether those $k+r$ equations were independent. But for the economy as a whole, however high prices may be, the sum of all income must equal the sum of all expenditure:
  $$\sum_{i=1}^{k+r}{p_i}{x_i}=\sum_{i=1}^{k+r}{p_i}{y_i}$$

  This identity is what is called Walras's law. Because Walras's law holds, there cannot be $k+r$ mutually independent equilibrium equations — among the $k+r$ markets, only $k+r-1$ are genuinely independent. But by designating one commodity as the "general equivalent" (setting its price at 1) and using relative prices in place of absolute prices, the problem is resolved. On this basis, Walrasian general equilibrium theory asserts that a system of relative prices that clears all markets simultaneously does exist.

  It is worth knowing that Walras's own "count-the-equations" argument was not mathematically rigorous, and that a genuinely rigorous proof of existence had to wait eighty years (see "Limits and what follows" in this chapter).

### Use the Edgeworth box to explain general equilibrium in a pure exchange economy.

(1) The Edgeworth box is a box-shaped diagram, named after the British economist Edgeworth, for studying the optimal allocation of resources.
The box reveals how resources are to be allocated when the total quantities of everything consumed, or of the inputs used in economic activity, are fixed. Suppose the economy contains only consumers 1 and 2, and commodities 1 and 2; its Edgeworth box is shown in Figure 6-1.

![](../img/exercises/micro/6-1.png)

(2) A pure exchange economy is one with exchange but no production. Suppose that in a pure exchange economy two consumers exchange two commodities, that the quantity initially owned by consumer $i$ of commodity $j$ is $w_{ij}$, and that the quantity the consumer chooses to consume is $x_{ij}$.

For the economy as a whole, the quantities of the two commodities owned by society are:

$$w_1=w_{11} + w_{21}$$

and

$$w_2=w_{12}+w_{22}$$

Suppose the prices of the two commodities are $p_1$ and $p_2$. A consumer maximizing utility decides the optimal consumption quantities at the given price level. Note that the consumer's income here derives from the initial endowment of wealth. The first consumer's behaviour can be summarized as:

$$\begin{cases}
\max & u_{1}\left(x_{11}, x_{12}\right) \\
x_{11}, x_{12} & \\
\text { s. t. } & p_{1} x_{11}+p_{2} x_{12}=p_{1} \omega_{11}+p_{2} \omega_{12}
\end{cases}$$

The satisfaction this consumer obtains from consuming different combinations of commodities can be represented by a series of indifference curves convex toward the origin (the lower-left corner). The consumer's income constraint line $p_{1}x_{11} +p_{2}x_{12}=p_{1}w_{11}+p_{2}w_{12}$ is a straight line with slope $(-p_1/p_2)$ passing through the initial point $(w_{11},w_{12})$. At the given prices, the consumer chooses on this budget constraint the combination that maximizes utility, namely the consumption bundle $(\bar{x_{11}},\bar{x_{12}})$ at the point where the budget line is tangent to an indifference curve; the consumer is then in a state of partial equilibrium. The second consumer has an analogous partial equilibrium.

Clearly, in the Edgeworth box the second consumer's budget line coincides with the first consumer's. Thus, when $(\bar{x_{11}}-w_{11})+(\bar{x_{21}}-w_{21})=0$, the market for the first commodity is in equilibrium, and the optimal points chosen by the two consumers then coincide. The same holds for the second commodity. And as the diagram readily shows, as soon as the market for the first commodity is in equilibrium, the market for the second commodity must also be in equilibrium — this is exactly Walras's law in the two-commodity case. The price so determined (in fact a relative price) is the general equilibrium price.

### What is a Pareto optimal allocation? What are its main conditions?

- (1) If, for a given state of resource allocation, no Pareto improvement exists at all — that is, at that state no change can make at least one person better off without making any other person worse off — then that state of resource allocation is called a Pareto optimal state.

- (2) The conditions that must be satisfied to reach a Pareto optimal state are called the conditions for Pareto optimality; they comprise the optimality condition for exchange, the optimality condition for production, and the optimality condition for exchange and production together.

① The condition for Pareto optimality in exchange: on the side of exchange, the marginal rate of substitution between any pair of commodities is equal for every individual using the two commodities, i.e. $RCS_{XY}^A = RCS_{XY}^{B}$.

② The condition for Pareto optimality in production: on the side of production, the marginal rate of technical substitution between any pair of factors is equal in the production of every commodity made with the two inputs, i.e. $RTS_{LK}^{X}=RTS_{LK}^{Y}$.

③ The condition for Pareto optimality in exchange and production: the marginal rate of transformation in production between any pair of commodities equals the marginal rate of substitution of every individual consuming the two commodities, i.e. $RCS_{XY}=RPT_{XY}$.

The three conditions each govern one thing: efficiency of exchange sends commodities to those who value them most, efficiency of production sends factors to the producers who will use them best, and efficiency of exchange and production together makes the economy's output mix exactly the mix people want.

### Why can a perfectly competitive market be in a Pareto optimal state?

- (1) A Pareto optimal state is the general criterion for judging the efficiency of the market mechanism.
A Pareto optimal state means: at a given state of resource allocation, no change can make at least one person better off without making any other person worse off — that is, no Pareto improvement exists — and that state of resource allocation is then called a Pareto optimal state.
For an economy to attain a Pareto optimal state, three necessary conditions must be satisfied:
- ① the marginal rate of substitution between any two commodities must be equal for all consumers using the two commodities;
- ② the marginal rate of technical substitution between any two factors must be equal for all producers using the two factors;
- ③ the marginal rate of substitution of any two commodities for consumers must equal the marginal rate of transformation of those two commodities for producers.

- (2) The reason perfectly competitive markets can always attain a Pareto optimal state can be explained separately through the three necessary conditions for Pareto optimality. There is only one key mechanism: **perfect competition makes everyone face the same prices**, so that the outcome of each person "adjusting their marginal quantities according to price" automatically becomes "everyone's marginal quantities are equal".
- ① From the optimality condition for exchange: under perfect competition, individual consumers are all price takers, and each consumer adjusts demand for commodities so as to satisfy $RCS_{XY}=\frac{P_X}{P_Y}$ and thereby maximize utility. Since all consumers are price takers, the quantities of any two commodities each consumer buys must be such that their marginal rate of substitution equals the common price ratio faced by all consumers. Hence the marginal rate of substitution between any two commodities is equal for all consumers.

- ② From the optimality condition for production: under perfect competition, individual producers are all price takers for factors, and each producer adjusts demand for factors so as to satisfy $RTS_{LK}=\frac{P_L}{P_K}$ and thereby maximize profit. Since all producers are price takers for factors, the quantities of any two factors each producer buys and uses must be such that their marginal rate of technical substitution equals the common factor price ratio faced by all producers. Hence the marginal rate of technical substitution between any two factors is equal for all producers.

- ③ From the optimality condition for production and exchange: the marginal rate of transformation in producing any two products is the ratio of their marginal costs, and each consumer's marginal rate of substitution for any two commodities equals their price ratio. Under perfect competition, the price of any product equals its marginal cost; hence the marginal rate of substitution of any two products equals their marginal rate of transformation.

- (3) In summary, under perfect competition all three necessary conditions for Pareto optimality can be satisfied. Put differently, under the market mechanism of perfect competition the economy as a whole can attain a Pareto optimal state, and such an economy is necessarily one of optimal efficiency.

### How does Western microeconomics argue for the "invisible hand" principle?

From Adam Smith onward, economists have held that in a perfectly competitive market economy the market mechanism acts like an "invisible hand", constantly adjusting people's economic behaviour so that the allocation of resources in the whole social economy reaches a Pareto optimal state.

- (1) Product markets

- ① For consumers, the goal of behaviour is to maximize utility. In pursuing maximum utility, under the effect of the "invisible hand", consumers will in the end use their limited income to choose among commodities so as to obtain maximum utility, yielding a demand curve that slopes downward to the right; every point on the curve is an equilibrium point of maximum consumer utility.
- ② For producers, the goal of behaviour is to maximize profit. In pursuing maximum profit, producers are bound to arrive at the optimal combination of factors of production and thus to produce at the lowest cost, in the end yielding a supply curve that slopes upward to the right. Every point on the supply curve is an equilibrium point of maximum profit in the market.
- ③ The joint action of the demand curve and the supply curve determines the equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity of the product market. At this point consumers have maximally satisfied their wants, and firms have obtained maximum profit.

- (2) Factor markets

- ① For firms, in pursuit of maximum profit, firms employ factors of production on the principle that the value of marginal product equals the marginal factor cost. On this principle, a factor demand curve sloping downward to the right is obtained.
- ② For suppliers of factors, that is, consumers, in pursuit of maximum utility at a given factor price level, suppliers of factors must set the marginal utility of the resources "supplied as a factor" equal to the marginal utility of the resources "retained for their own use"; on this principle, a factor supply curve sloping upward to the right is obtained.
- ③ The intersection of the factor supply curve and the factor demand curve determines the optimal factor price and the optimal quantity of the factor employed.

- (3) General equilibrium theory

The preceding analysis of product markets and factor markets was grounded in the single market, and the market equilibrium state was partial equilibrium. There, the price of a product or factor of production is affected only by its own demand and supply conditions, and the prices of other commodities and factors are treated as unchanged. In general equilibrium theory, the prices of all commodities and factors of production influence one another. Under certain assumptions, a general equilibrium exists for perfectly competitive markets, at which all product and factor markets simultaneously reach equilibrium.

- (4) Welfare economics

The foregoing argument only shows that the effect of the "invisible hand" brings the whole economy to an equilibrium state. But is this equilibrium state economically efficient? Welfare economics proposed the Pareto criterion for judging the level of welfare. At the same time, the general equilibrium of perfectly competitive markets is Pareto optimal: no one retains any possibility of improving their welfare without affecting others. So in a perfectly competitive capitalist market economy, while people pursue their own private ends, they are guided by an "invisible hand" to realize the social end of advancing social welfare.

From Smith's prose (1776) to Pareto's criterion (around 1906), and then to the rigorous proof of this set of theorems (the 1950s), the "invisible hand" took nearly two hundred years to pass from intuition to theorem — and every premise on which the theorems hold (perfect competition, no externalities, complete information) is also precisely where the later disputes were fiercest.

## Derivation and worked examples

### What is the condition for a pure exchange economy to be Pareto optimal? Prove your conclusion.

- (1) A pure exchange economy is an economy with exchange but no production.
The condition for a pure exchange economy to be Pareto optimal is: the marginal rate of substitution between any pair of commodities is equal for every individual using the two commodities, i.e.:

$$RCS_{XY}^{A}=RCS_{XY}^{B}$$

- (2) If the above condition is not satisfied (that is, $RCS_{XY}^{A} \ne RCS_{XY}^{B}$), then, with the total quantities of the two commodities given, the two consumers can still, by exchanging, improve the situation of at least one person without affecting others.
Suppose $RCS_{XY}^{A} =2$ while $RCS_{XY}^{B} =1$.
This means that, in the first consumer's eyes, 1 unit of X can be substituted for 2 units of Y, while in the second consumer's eyes, 1 unit of X can be substituted for 1 unit of Y. If the second consumer now gives up one unit of X, they need one unit of Y to remain at their original utility level. Hand this unit of X to A, and A is willing to give up 2 units of Y for it. Compensating B with one of those units leaves, with A and B both keeping their original utility levels, 1 unit of Y for A and B to divide. There is therefore room for a Pareto improvement. This shows that only when the two consumers' marginal rates of substitution are equal for any pair of commodities is a Pareto optimal state achieved, leaving no room for a Pareto improvement.

## Limits and what follows

- **Existence was proved; convergence was not.** Arrow and Debreu (1954) proved that a perfectly competitive equilibrium exists under broad conditions — provided one allows strong assumptions such as "futures markets" and "contingent commodities". But existence does not mean attainability: in the theory the equilibrium is found only through a fictional "auctioneer" repeatedly calling prices in a trial-and-error process (the tâtonnement), and whether this trial-and-error process always converges to the equilibrium was answered with a non-convergent counterexample by Scarf (1960). The equilibrium "exists" in mathematics; how markets "find" it still has no fully satisfactory answer.
- **Efficiency and equity were cut apart by the theorems, and stitched back together by reality.** The second fundamental theorem of welfare economics says that any Pareto optimal allocation can be reached through a one-shot redistribution combined with perfect competition — that is, efficiency and equity can both be had. But one-shot redistribution requires knowing each person's endowment and type, while in reality only distorting taxes are available, so equity still exacts a price in efficiency. More fundamentally, the Pareto criterion is entirely silent on "who should get what"; and Arrow's impossibility theorem (1951) shows that no perfect rule for aggregating individual preferences into a social preference exists at all — "social optimality" is bound to import value judgements, and economics cannot make that decision on behalf of society.
- **From the blackboard to policy.** General equilibrium was long regarded as a beautiful castle in the air, until computable general equilibrium (CGE) models turned it into a policy tool after the 1970s: assessing the economy-wide impact of tax reform or trade liberalization uses exactly a numerical version of Walras's system of equations. Meanwhile everyday policy analysis still relies heavily on partial equilibrium — the more important the spillovers between markets, the less the general equilibrium account can be dispensed with.
