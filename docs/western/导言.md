# Introduction

> **The core question of this chapter**: on what grounds does economics count as an independent discipline? Where do the boundaries between micro and macro, between positive and normative, come from? What stages must an economic theory pass through from being proposed, to being tested, to being revised?

## The shape of this chapter

Every discipline must first answer two questions: what do I study, and by what method? Economics' first answer was once thoroughly confused — some said it was the science of wealth, others the art of statecraft — until in 1932 Lionel Robbins gave the famous definition that anchors the discipline to "scarcity and choice," and only then was the object of study made uniform. The second answer was assembled fight by fight: the boundary between positive and normative, the meaning of equilibrium, the status of models and assumptions — behind every tool stands a specific quarrel.

This chapter offers no computable formula, but it is the shared coordinate system of the site's fifteen chapters: every later theory can be read back against it — which choice problem under scarcity does it answer, which analytical method does it use, and what evidence has since corrected it?

## A discipline stands on one question

### Scarcity: economics' permanent condition

Human economic activity has a few features that outlast eras: it is social (only division of labour and cooperation make it efficient), it needs rules (property rights and institutions), and — the decisive point — it always feels that resources are not enough. This is not because people do not try hard, but because wants themselves escalate: after subsistence comes comfort, after comfort comes respectability. Economics divides goods into two kinds: **free goods**, obtainable at no cost (air, sunlight), and **economic goods**, obtainable only at a cost (bread, housing, a doctor's time). That second class of goods, and the resources used to produce them, are always insufficient relative to human wants — that is **scarcity**. It denotes a relative relation, not an absolute quantity: no matter how much oil there is, there will be a day it runs out, while wants do not.

Scarcity is economics' starting point because it forces **choice**: resources used here cannot be used there, so "what to produce, how to produce, and for whom to produce" becomes a question every society must answer. Economics studies how people and society answer those three questions.

### The Robbins definition: economics as the science of choice

In 1932 Lionel Robbins (1898–1984) gave, in *An Essay on the Nature and Significance of Economic Science*, the definition still quoted again and again: economics is the science which studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses.

Why does that definition matter? Before it, the answers to "what does economics study" ran wild — the production and distribution of wealth, household management, the nature and causes of national wealth — and each answer covered only part of the discipline. Once Robbins switched the object to **choice**, everything from a consumer's trade-offs under a budget constraint to a government's weighing of deficit against inflation fits inside one analytical frame: given ends, how are scarce means allocated. The definition also draws the discipline's boundary: choices that do not involve scarce means — pure aesthetic judgment, falling in love — are not economic problems. A discipline can stand by exclusion as well as by definition.

### Opportunity cost and the six basic questions

The price of a choice needs a precise name. Of the other uses given up when a resource is applied to producing something, the one with the highest value is that production's **opportunity cost**. The opportunity cost of university is not tuition, but tuition plus the income forgone over those four years; when a government builds an airport on a plot of land, its cost is the hospital or farmland that plot could have become. Opportunity cost is where the economist's thinking parting company with the accountant's: what is not on the books is not necessarily not a cost.

From this one can list every economic question a society faces, and the list is worth reading item by item, because it is itself a map of the discipline:

- **What to produce? How much?** — the structure of goods and services (micro)
- **How to produce?** — which technology, which combination of factors (micro)
- **For whom to produce?** — how output is distributed among society's members (micro)
- **Are all resources being used?** — whether there is idleness and waste, that is the unemployment problem (macro)
- **Is the purchasing power of money stable?** — the inflation problem (macro)
- **Is productive capacity growing?** — the long-run growth problem (macro)

The first three ask "is the resource being used in the right place," the last three ask "is the resource being used at all." That dividing line is exactly the line between micro and macro.

## Micro and macro: a divide torn open by a depression

**Microeconomics** studies individual economic units — consumers, firms, single markets — and examines the determination of individual quantities: the price of one commodity, the output of one firm, the return on one factor. Its hub is price. This thread runs from Smith's *Wealth of Nations* in 1776, through the marginal revolution of the 1870s by Jevons, Menger and Walras, to Marshall's *Principles of Economics* in 1890 integrating supply-and-demand analysis, and it makes up the first half of the modern textbook.

**Macroeconomics** takes the aggregates of the whole national economy as its object: total output, total employment, the general price level. It appeared as an independent half very late — in 1936, when John Maynard Keynes (1883–1946) published *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*. Its circumstances of birth set its character: during the Great Depression a quarter of the labour force was unemployed with no sign of recovery for years, and the micro theory of the day had nothing to say about it — by Say's law, "supply creates its own demand," general overproduction and long-term unemployment should not be able to happen at all. Keynes showed that even when every individual acts rationally at the micro level, the economy can still be trapped in deficient effective demand. Individual rationality does not add up to macro health — that "fallacy of composition" is the reason macroeconomics exists (unpacked from Chapter 8 of this site).

The two halves are not two separate sciences. Macro conclusions cannot contradict micro behaviour — when policy changes, people adjust their expectations and actions, and the parameters estimated on old data become invalid; that is the core of the Lucas critique of 1976, and the reason all macro theory since has been rewritten in forms that are "micro-founded." In the other direction, micro analysis cannot do without macro constraints — the interest rate and aggregate demand stand behind every firm's cost curve. I read the two halves together: the seven micro chapters beginning with [supply and demand](/western/需求与供给) tell how price allocates resources, and the eight macro chapters beginning with [macroeconomic activity and macroeconomics](/western/宏观经济活动与宏观经济学) tell why aggregates break and how they get managed.

## Positive and normative: the edge of science

**Positive economics** answers "what is": how the economy stands, why it stands that way, how it may change. Its propositions are objective — whether a conclusion is right can be tested against empirical fact. **Normative economics** answers "what ought to be": on the basis of some value judgment, it puts forward standards of good and bad for economic things, as theoretical premise and policy basis. Its conclusions cannot be tested against fact, because the disagreement lies in values, not in facts.

The "value judgment" here needs unpacking: it is not value in the commodity-value sense, but the **social value** of economic things — whether something is good or bad. A few examples make it plain:

- "Raising the minimum wage reduces employment for low-skilled workers" — a positive proposition. It may be true or false, but data can settle it; the fast-food comparison between New Jersey and Pennsylvania was designed for exactly this.
- "The minimum wage ought to be raised" — a normative proposition. The two sides may be using the very same positive findings; what divides them is that one weights low-income earners' gain more and the other weights the unemployed's loss more — a question of the weight given to values, where facts cannot help.

Why is economics so insistent on this divide? Because for a discipline to claim to be science, its propositions must be capable of being refuted by fact. Mix a normative judgment into positive analysis and the argument can never close — you are talking about fact, I am talking about position. But the two are not severed: decisions rest on positive findings and are guided by normative ones — "can it be done" cannot answer "ought it to be done," yet an "ought" with no "can" underneath it is just talk. The substantive content of both the micro and the macro halves belongs to positive economics, which is precisely why they can be tested and are continually revised.

## How a theory is built

Positive analysis works like this: propose a theory that explains a phenomenon, test it against fact, then make predictions on the basis of the theory. A theory is built of five parts:

- **Definitions**: a precise meaning is fixed for the variables being studied.
- **Variables**: quantities that can take different values. Those determined by forces inside the economic system are **endogenous variables**, those determined by forces outside it (politics, climate, technology) are **exogenous variables**; by their time character they split into **stocks** (a value at one point in time, such as the capital stock at year end) and **flows** (a value arising over a period, such as annual investment — investment changes the stock, the stock constrains the flow, and this pair is the star of growth theory).
- **Assumptions**: the conditions under which the theory applies. "Other things being equal" is the most used assumption there is.
- **Hypotheses**: a statement about the relation between two or more variables — a theory not yet tested.
- **Predictions**: inferences about the future drawn from the hypothesis. A testable prediction is the line between a theory and chatter.

Theoretical structures that organise these parts to describe the interdependence of economic variables are **economic models**. A model must be simpler than reality, and "the assumptions are not true" has always been the charge outsiders bring against economics. In *The Methodology of Positive Economics* (1953) Milton Friedman (1912–2006) gave the reply that still casts a long shadow: a theory's worth lies in its predictive power, not in whether its assumptions are realistic — a billiards player solves no differential equations, yet the untrue assumption that "the billiards player is like a physicist well versed in geometry" predicts his next shot well. That defence bought economics the freedom to use bold assumptions; the bill for it was paid half a century later (see "Limits and what follows").

The analytical toolbox holds three more pairs of contrasts. **Equilibrium analysis**: equilibrium is a state of "no further need to change" — not that change is impossible, but that any further change would only be worse; one that returns to itself after a disturbance is a stable equilibrium, one that does not return is unstable. Looking at one market while assuming the others unchanged is Marshall's **partial equilibrium**; admitting that all markets affect each other and clear simultaneously is Walras's **general equilibrium** — the latter waits until Chapter 6, [general equilibrium and welfare economics](/western/一般均衡论和福利经济学). **Static, comparative-static and dynamic**: static analysis looks at the equilibrium state itself and the conditions for reaching it; comparative-static analysis compares "the two equilibria before and after a change in conditions" (this site's analysis of tax incidence is its drill ground); dynamic analysis tracks the actual path of variables through time (growth theory is its home turf). **Marginal analysis**: the marginal revolution of 1870 brought calculus into economics — rational decision-making happens at the margin, comparing the cost and benefit of "one more unit." Behind it stands the sharpest and most contested assumption in the whole discipline, the **homo economicus assumption**: the driving force of an economic agent's behaviour is maximising benefit, and the governing rule is optimisation under a given objective. Note that this assumption speaks to the **logic** of behaviour (acting in a way consistent with a goal); it does not guarantee that the outcome is optimal, and it is not the same as claiming the goal itself is right.

## Market and plan: two solutions to one problem

The way a society answers "what to produce, how, and for whom" is the question of the **economic system**. The family of human systems runs roughly four ways: the **natural economy** (allocation by habit and need, self-sufficiency), the **market economy** (allocation guided by price), the **planned economy** (allocation by central directive), and the **mixed economy** (market-led with government as assistant, the contemporary normal case).

Why discuss systems separately? Because the same scarcity problem gets entirely different answers under different systems: the market economy hands the three questions to price — price is at once a signal (a notice of how scarce something is) and an incentive (the producer's motive); the planned economy hands them to the planning authority — in theory a more direct channel, in practice two hurdles of information and incentive to clear. Mises (1881–1973) put the quarrel plainly in 1920: abolish the market in the means of production and there are no real relative prices, so the planner has no way of computing how resources should be allocated; in 1936 Lange (1904–1965) answered with "planners simulating the market by trial and error," and the socialist calculation debate became one of the most important quarrels of twentieth-century economic theory — together with the *General Theory* published that same year, it forms the twin nodes of 1936 on the [theory timeline](/timeline).

Twentieth-century history read like one giant experiment in systems: the Great Depression shook faith in the market, the early industrialisation of planned economies displayed another possibility, and the turn to the market at the century's end pushed the answer back again. But the quarrel did not end, it changed form — where the border between government and market should be drawn, how property rights are to be protected, how far redistribution should go, these remain core questions of contemporary economics, and this site returns to them again and again in [market failure and microeconomic policy](/western/市场失灵和微观经济政策) and in the public finance and socialist economic theory courses.

## Limits and what follows

- **The counter-attack on the rational-agent assumption**: Friedman's defence line — "predictive power outranks the realism of assumptions" — was broken from inside by behavioural economics. The experiments of Kahneman and Tversky show that human departures from rationality are not random but systematic — loss aversion, anchoring, framing effects all reproduce steadily in the laboratory. If the biases are systematic, then irrationality can go into the model: behavioural economics did not overturn the rationality assumption, it drew an edge around where it applies. The 2002 Nobel Prize went to the psychologist Kahneman (1934–2024), the largest concession the discipline has made to its own foundations.
- **The long rebuilding of micro-foundations**: after the Lucas critique, macro theory was required to be derived step by step from individual optimisation, and the shape of this site's eight macro chapters is precisely the product of that rebuilding. But the mainstream DSGE models failed together in the 2008 financial crisis, which exposed the limit of the rebuild — between the "rational individuals" inside the models and real behaviour in a crisis there still stands the chasm behavioural economics points at.
- **Duality: ideology and empirical analysis**: as capitalism's understanding of itself, western economics is both a tool of empirical analysis and a carrier of value presuppositions — the defaults toward property rights, markets and efficiency sit at the start of the argument, not at its conclusion. The duality that Gao Hongye's textbook warns about is exactly why every chapter here ends with a "Limits and what follows": know what a theory answers, and know what it does not answer.
- **The convenience and the cost of slogans**: Mankiw's *Principles of Economics* compresses the introduction into "the ten principles of economics" (trade-offs, opportunity cost, marginal thinking, responding to incentives…) — an entry-level book's way of organising, easy to remember. But every slogan conceals an unresolved argument — "trade makes everybody better off" holds in aggregate terms and does not hold automatically in distributional terms, and that sentence can only be fully understood in the chapters on comparative advantage and international trade theory. Ten slogans as signposts is fine; as conclusions it is not.
