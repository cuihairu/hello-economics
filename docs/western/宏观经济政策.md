# Chapter 11 Macroeconomic policy

> **Core question of this chapter**: How does government use fiscal and monetary policy to stabilize the economy? What part do automatic stabilizers and discretion play? How are policy effects assessed?

## Chapter roadmap

The *General Theory* closes with a famous line: the ideas of economists and political philosophers, whether right or wrong, are more powerful than is commonly understood — "practical men, who believe themselves to be quite exempt from any intellectual influence, are usually the slaves of some defunct economist". What this chapter tells is how that set of ideas became a toolbox: Chapters 9 and 10 showed that aggregate demand determines short-run output, and so "steering aggregate demand against the wind" became the default operation of postwar governments. The US Employment Act of 1946 wrote "promoting full employment" into law, and Keynesian demand management thereby entered the seat of official practice.

The toolbox has two layers. The fiscal layer: discretionary spending and taxation, plus the "built-in stabilizers" that run without anyone's signature — taxes and unemployment benefits stretch and shrink counter-cyclically. The monetary layer: the central bank's three traditional instruments (the reserve ratio, rediscounting, open market operations), whose principles all come from Chapter 10's supply and demand for money. The chapter answers in passing "where does the money come from": the commercial banking system creates derived deposits through fractional reserves, and what the central bank controls is exactly the valve of that amplifier. As for the crisis of confidence this policy framework later met (stagflation, lags, expectations), that is left for Chapters 14 and 15; the end of this chapter first draws the map.

## Core concepts

### The fiscal system

The fiscal system is a set of norms of conduct laid down by the state for fiscal work; it reflects the laws of fiscal revenue and expenditure and is a directive document with legal force.
The fiscal system covers the government's systems of expenditure and of revenue.

### Automatic stabilizers

The automatic stabilizer of the fiscal system, also called the built-in stabilizer, is a mechanism inherent in the economic system itself that reduces the impact of all kinds of disturbance on national income — curbing inflation automatically in a boom and softening depression automatically in a recession, without government taking any action.

In social and economic life the factors that usually act as automatic stabilizers chiefly include:
- Personal and corporate income taxes
- Unemployment benefits and other welfare transfer payments
- Support prices for farm products, along with corporate saving and household saving.

For example, in a depression personal income and corporate profits fall, so government income-tax receipts fall automatically, which correspondingly raises consumption and investment. Meanwhile, as unemployment rises, government unemployment benefit and various welfare outlays necessarily increase, which again stimulates personal consumption and promotes investment.

But the power of automatic stabilizers is limited. They can only steady the economy in support of demand management, and are not by themselves enough to keep it fully stable; they can only ease or soften the degree of recession or inflation, not change their overall direction. Hence stronger fiscal and monetary measures are still needed.

Why need a stabilizing mechanism that "requires no one to make a decision"? Because discretion is too slow: spotting a recession, passing legislation, executing a budget all take time. Automatic stabilizers compress the response time to zero — the moment income falls, taxes are collected more sparingly and unemployment benefits flow out more freely, with no signature required.

### Fiscal policy

Fiscal policy is the choice made over the levels of government spending, taxation and borrowing in order to raise the level of employment, soften economic fluctuations, prevent inflation and achieve stable growth — or the decision taken over the levels of government revenue and expenditure.

The instruments of fiscal policy include changing government purchase expenditure, altering government transfer payments, and varying taxation and public debt. Fiscal policy uses the government budget (taxation and government spending) to influence aggregate demand and so attain the aim of stabilizing the economy. Its feature is that government uses the administrative budget to control directly the totals of consumption and investment and to regulate the nation's level of demand, bringing aggregate demand and aggregate supply to the desired equilibrium, thereby promoting full employment and controlling inflation.

Fiscal policy is one of the principal means of demand management. In content it includes the fiscal revenue policy and the fiscal expenditure policy; the instrument of the former is chiefly tax rates, that of the latter chiefly government purchases (spending).

### The full-employment budget surplus

The full-employment budget surplus is the government budget surplus that a given budget would produce at the full-employment level of national income — that is, at the potential level of national income; it is the difference between the government revenue corresponding to potential national income and budgeted government expenditure. It was instituted on the basis of the Keynesian idea of functional finance. Taking it as the standard removes the influence of cyclical fluctuations in income on the budget position, and so reflects the stance of fiscal policy more accurately. If the full-employment budget surplus rises, or the deficit falls, fiscal policy is contractionary; otherwise the policy is expansionary. It also lets policymakers set fiscal policy with full employment as the objective.

Its use is to answer a common policy dispute: is this year's deficit the result of government choosing to spend, or the result of recession dragging tax receipts down? Recompute the budget at the full-employment level of income and the cyclical influence is wiped out; what remains is the stance of policy itself.

### Monetary policy

Monetary policy is the conduct, provided for through the banking system, by which the central bank controls the quantity of money supply and thereby regulates the interest rate, so as to influence investment and the whole economy and reach its economic objectives.

Monetary policy likewise divides into expansionary and contractionary. In execution it also works against the economic wind.
Its main instruments include changing the legal reserve ratio on deposits, altering the rediscount rate and open market operations, together with auxiliary means such as moral suasion, loosening credit conditions, and easing the quantity of mortgage loans and credit quotas.

### The legal reserve system

The legal reserve system is the institution by which the central bank requires commercial banks and other depository institutions to hold a certain proportion of savings deposits as reserves, to prevent liquidity difficulties when depositors come to cash out.

Under the legal reserve system, the ratio of deposit reserves to the total of deposits is called the legal reserve ratio. The ratio differs with the type of bank, the kind of deposit, and its term and size. By adjusting the reserve ratio the central bank can influence the credit-supplying capacity of financial institutions and thereby indirectly regulate the quantity of money supplied. Raising the legal reserve ratio is a measure of credit contraction; lowering it is a measure of credit expansion; both affect in the end how much money is supplied. The legal reserve ratio is one of the central bank's three traditional policy instruments for regulating the money supply, and the most violent of monetary policy tools.

### The rediscount system

The rediscount system is the institution laid down by the central bank to govern its own lending to commercial banks and other financial institutions.

**[What is rediscounting?]**

When a commercial bank takes eligible securities to the central bank for discounting or collateralized loans, the rate the central bank charges the commercial bank is called the **rediscount rate**.

---

**[Transmission mechanism of the rediscount rate]**

$$\text{Rediscount rate} ↑ → \text{cost of borrowing} ↑ → \text{borrowing} ↓ → \text{base money} ↓ → \text{money supply} ↓$$

$$\text{Rediscount rate} ↓ → \text{cost of borrowing} ↓ → \text{borrowing} ↑ → \text{base money} ↑ → \text{money supply} ↑$$

**Table comparison**:

| Change in the rediscount rate | Cost of borrowing | Conduct of commercial banks | Base money | Money supply | Character of the policy |
|--------------|----------|--------------|----------|----------|----------|
| **Raised** | Rises | Borrow less from the central bank | Falls | Falls | **Contractionary** |
| **Lowered** | Falls | Borrow more from the central bank | Rises | Rises | **Expansionary** |

---

**[Discounting vs rediscounting]**

| Concept | Meaning | Parties |
|------|------|--------|
| **Discounting** | A firm takes a bill to a commercial bank for cash | Firm → commercial bank |
| **Rediscounting** | A commercial bank takes a bill to the central bank for cash | Commercial bank → central bank |

**In one line**: the rediscount rate is the central bank's "lending rate" toward commercial banks — when borrowing is dear, banks borrow less and money shrinks; when borrowing is cheap, banks borrow more and money grows.

### Open market operations

Open market operations are the policy conduct by which the central bank openly buys and sells government bonds on the securities market to control the money supply and the interest rate.
When the economy is depressed the central bank buys government bonds, to raise the reserves of commercial banks and the community's quantity of money; conversely, the central bank can sell government bonds to carry out a contractionary monetary policy. It is today the most important and most frequently used instrument by which Western central banks regulate the money supply and attain their policy objectives.

---

**[The three monetary policy instruments compared]**

The central bank's "three treasures" for controlling the quantity of money:

| Instrument | English | Operates on | Mechanism | Force | Speed | Frequency of use |
|------|------|----------|----------|------|------|----------|
| **Legal reserve ratio** | Required Reserve Ratio | Commercial banks | Changes the money multiplier | **Most violent** | Slower | Very rare |
| **Rediscount rate** | Rediscount Rate | Commercial banks | Changes the cost of borrowing | Milder | Slower | Occasional |
| **Open market operations** | Open Market Operations | Financial markets | Buying and selling bonds to take in and release base money | Flexible and precise | **Fastest** | **Every day** |

---

**[Expansionary vs contractionary monetary policy operations]**

| Policy objective | Reserve ratio | Rediscount rate | Open market operations |
|----------|----------|----------|--------------|
| **Expansionary** (more money) | **Lower** | **Lower** | **Buy bonds** |
| **Contractionary** (less money) | **Raise** | **Raise** | **Sell bonds** |

---

**[The three instruments in metaphor]**

| Instrument | Metaphor |
|------|------------|
| Reserve ratio | Adjusting "the bore of the pipe" — changing the banking system's capacity to create money |
| Rediscount rate | Adjusting "the price of loans" — changing banks' willingness to borrow from the central bank |
| Open market operations | "Buying and selling bonds" every day — taking in and releasing base money |

---

**[Transmission mechanisms summarized]**

$$\text{Reserve ratio} ↓ → \text{money multiplier} ↑ → \text{money supply} ↑$$

$$\text{Rediscount rate} ↓ → \text{cost of borrowing} ↓ → \text{base money} ↑ → \text{money supply} ↑$$

$$\text{Central bank buys bonds} → \text{base money} ↑ → \text{money supply} ↑$$

**Conclusion**: the three instruments reach the same end by different roads — all of them control the quantity of money supplied by working on base money or on the money multiplier.

---

### The money creation multiplier

The money creation multiplier, also called the money multiplier or the money expansion multiplier, is the multiple effect that one deposit has on the quantity of money supplied through the banking system.
Under the rules of the banking system, a commercial bank must set aside a share of the deposits it takes in as reserves at the legal reserve ratio, while the remainder can be lent to customers, further increasing the money supply. Continuing in this way, the maximum total of deposits formed across the whole banking system (including the initial $\Delta D$) is:

$${\Delta M}=\frac{\Delta D}{r_d}$$

where $\Delta{D}$ is the deposit first added in the banking system and $r_d$ the legal reserve ratio on deposits.

Why can one deposit become a multiple of money? Because no one requires the bank to lock deposits in a vault: the early goldsmith bankers found that customers never all came to withdraw at once, so they lent most of the deposits out for profit — and the fractional reserve system was born, "money creation" being only its algebraic result. When the central bank adjusts $r_d$ it is adjusting the magnification of this amplifier.

## Questions and analysis

### Why can the commercial banking system magnify an original deposit several times over, and by what means?

- (1) Of the deposits a commercial bank takes in, a certain proportion must be kept as reserves. This proportion is set by the central bank in law, hence the name legal reserve ratio. The legal reserve ratio is what lets the banking system create money supply a multiple at a time.

- (2) Suppose the legal reserve ratio is 10%. If a commercial bank takes in a new deposit of 100 yuan, it can lend out 90 yuan. If the customer deposits those 90 yuan in their own account, the bank holding that account can lend out 81 yuan. And so on; the total of deposits added in the banking system is: $100+100(1-10\%)+100(1-10\%)^2+\cdots =1000$ (yuan)

- (3) The deposit money created by commercial banks is called derived deposits — deposits that arise when commercial banks extend loans on the basis of original deposits, in excess of the initial part of the deposits. It is what commercial banks obtain by turning liabilities in the form of loans into deposit money on the foundation of their excess reserves, and it is the main part of commercial bank money.

The capacity of commercial banks to create derived deposits is limited by the original deposits they hold (the part of base money in commercial banks' hands) — that is, by their capacity to pay or to settle. The central bank therefore often tightens or loosens the money supply according to the needs of monetary policy, absorbing or releasing base money and changing the capacity to create derived deposits through adjusting the legal reserve ratio, the rediscount policy and open market operations. The theoretical limit formula of deposit creation is:

$$\text{Total deposits}＝\text{original deposits}×\frac{1}{\text{reserve ratio}}，\qquad \text{Derived deposits}＝\text{total deposits}－\text{original deposits}＝\text{original deposits}×\left(\frac{1}{\text{reserve ratio}}－1\right)$$

Hence the multiple of total deposits is in theory (setting aside cash leakage and other factors) the reciprocal of the reserve ratio, and the multiple of derived deposits is that reciprocal minus one (as in the example above: original deposits 100, reserve ratio 10%, total deposits 1000, of which derived deposits 900).

In general, if deposits initially added in the banking system are $\Delta D$, and reserves are held at the legal reserve ratio $r_d$, the maximum quantity of money the whole banking system can create is

$$\Delta M = \frac{\Delta D}{r_d}$$

The multiple of deposit expansion above is conditional on all the added deposits circulating in full within the banking system.

### By what mechanisms do fiscal and monetary policy each regulate the macroeconomy?

In the Keynesian view, by changing the government's levels of revenue and expenditure and the quantity of money supplied in the economy, one can influence the level of aggregate expenditure and thereby the macroeconomic objectives of employment, the general price level, economic growth and equilibrium of the balance of payments. In practice this takes the form of fiscal and monetary policy regulating the macroeconomy.

- (1) Fiscal policy is the decision government takes over the levels of its spending, taxation and borrowing in order to raise employment, soften economic fluctuations, prevent inflation and achieve stable growth. By varying its revenue and expenditure, fiscal policy works a multiplier effect on national income and so attains the aim of regulating the macroeconomy.

Fiscal policy works against the economic wind, and its main instruments of regulation include:

- ①Tax rates.
	>When the economy is in depression, government lowers tax rates, so the disposable income of persons and firms rises, consumption and investment rise, aggregate demand and national income expand, and recession is dispelled; when inflation is severe, government raises tax rates, so disposable income falls, consumption and investment fall, aggregate demand and national income contract, and inflation is dispelled.

- ②Government purchases.
	>When the economy is in depression, government increases its purchases, consumption and investment rise, aggregate demand and national income expand, and recession is dispelled; when inflation is severe, government reduces its purchases, consumption and investment fall, aggregate demand and national income contract, and inflation is dispelled.

- ③Transfer payments.
	>When the economy is in depression, government increases transfer payments, so the disposable income of persons and firms rises, consumption and investment rise, aggregate demand and national income expand, and recession is dispelled; when inflation is severe, government reduces transfer payments, so disposable income falls, consumption and investment fall, aggregate demand and national income contract, and inflation is dispelled.

- (2) Monetary policy is the conduct by which the central bank regulates the interest rate by controlling the quantity of money, thereby influencing investment and the whole economy so as to reach a given economic objective. Its main instruments of regulation include:

- ①Open market operations.
>Open market operations are the policy instrument by which the central bank buys or sells government bonds on the open market so as to increase or reduce the reserves of commercial banks. They are the instrument of economic stabilization the central bank uses most often, and also the most flexible. Open market operations work against the economic wind.

- ②Rediscount rate policy.
>Loans made by the central bank to commercial banks are usually called rediscounting, and the interest rate the central bank charges commercial banks on such loans the rediscount rate. The central bank may change the rediscount rate according to economic conditions. When the monetary authority judges aggregate expenditure deficient and unemployment tending to keep rising, it lowers the rediscount rate and enlarges the volume of rediscounting, to encourage commercial banks to lend and to stimulate investment.

- ③Changing the bank reserve ratio.
>The bank reserve ratio is the ratio of bank reserves to deposits; because the ratio is set in law it is also called the legal reserve ratio. The central bank may change the reserve ratio on commercial banks' demand deposits within the legal range to regulate the supply of money and credit; to break beyond the upper or lower legal limit of the reserve ratio it must ask the legislature to grant that power. Changing the legal reserve ratio is regarded as a powerful instrument, used infrequently because its effect is too violent. The central bank changes the bank reserve ratio against the economic wind.

Besides the three important instruments above — open market operations, changing the rediscount rate and changing the bank reserve ratio — the central banks of Western countries sometimes also adopt secondary instruments, for example moral suasion, selective controls and the control of securities credit, as well as the control of instalment credit and mortgage credit.

## Extended discussion

### How does demand management policy stabilize the economy? And what limits does it face?

- (1) Demand management is the macroeconomic policy that stabilizes or stimulates the economy by regulating the aggregate demand of the macroeconomy, so as to realize full employment and price stability. Its theoretical basis is the Keynesian doctrine that aggregate demand determines national income. According to Keynesian theory, the key to depression lies in deficient aggregate demand, and the problems arising in the running of the macroeconomy should likewise be addressed with aggregate demand management policy.

- (2) The basic principles of demand management are:
	- When aggregate demand falls short of the full-employment aggregate supply, adopt expansionary fiscal or monetary policy to stimulate the economy and eliminate unemployment;
	- When aggregate demand exceeds the full-employment aggregate supply, adopt contractionary fiscal or monetary policy to restrain inflation.

The schools of economics differ in their attitude to demand management policy. The Keynesian school values it, while holding that demand policy should be combined with supply policy or other policies. Monetarists and the rational expectations school advocate relying on the market's own adjustment to achieve economic stability, and oppose demand management policy.

- (3) The objectives of aggregate demand management policy are generally held to be full employment, price stability, sustained balanced economic growth and equilibrium in the balance of payments; in a closed economy the short-run objectives are chiefly full employment and price stability. In the short run, for reasons of aggregate demand, the economy can be in depression or in boom.
At such times government can adjust aggregate demand to hold the economy at its potential output level. In the long run the economy settles at potential output and government's demand management policy is ineffective. That is to say, although a capitalist economy can be in full-employment equilibrium in the long run, short-run depression and excessive boom are both unavoidable. Hence it is necessary to carry out Keynesian demand management policy to iron out economic fluctuations. In Keynes's view, the aggregate demand management policy aimed at deficient effective demand is a discretionary short-run policy.

- (4) Aggregate demand management policy also faces a series of limiting conditions:
	- ①The difficulty of judging the state of the economy and the degree of the problem;
	- ②The lag in the course of implementing policy;
	- ③The uncertainty of macroeconomic policy's effects.
	Still, although Western demand management policy is not omnipotent, it cannot be denied that it has a certain effect on the stability of the economy's working. Drawing on these theories and policies under China's socialist market economy would be beneficial.

## Limits and what comes next

- **Lags and the dispute of "rules versus discretion".** Friedman pointed out that monetary policy works with "long and variable lags": the valve turned today changes the flow a year and a half later — an adjustment against the wind can turn out to be a push along with it. Kydland and Prescott (1977) went further: even a well-intentioned government fails under discretion because of "time inconsistency" — the public anticipates that government will loosen after the fact, inflationary expectations fulfil themselves, and employment improves not at all. The remedy is to act by rule: Taylor's interest rate rule (1993), central bank independence and inflation targeting (first adopted by New Zealand in 1990) thereby became the standard equipment of modern monetary policy.
- **The Lucas critique.** This chapter's multipliers and transmission chains are all estimated from historical data, and Lucas (1976) pointed out: once the policy rule changes, people's expectations and behaviour change with it, and all the old parameters are void — design a new policy with a multiplier estimated under the old regime, and what you hold may be a map that no longer works. How this critique reshaped macroeconomics is taken up in Chapter 15.
- **The toolbox expanded after 2008.** With the interest rate down near zero, open market operations — the most used of the three treasures — lost its lever: the central bank turned to unconventional instruments such as quantitative easing, paying interest on excess reserves, and building an interest rate corridor; on the fiscal side, automatic stabilizers were leaned on again in the recessions of 2008 and 2020, and fiscal policy returned from supporting role to the front of the stage. The instruments changed; this chapter's question did not: who, under what conditions, turns which valve.
