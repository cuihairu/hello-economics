# Western economics

This group of chapters is not about memorising scattered concepts, it is about answering one overarching question:

**how consumers, firms and governments in a market interact through price, output and policy to decide the allocation of resources and the fluctuation of the economy.**

---

## The story of western economics' development

Before going into the individual chapters, knowing how the discipline evolved helps explain where each theory came from.

### Act one: mercantilism — the age of obsessing over gold and silver (16th–18th centuries)

In 16th-century Europe states, like miserly hoarders, held that **wealth is gold, silver and jewels**. The mercantilist idea was simple: sell more to foreigners and buy less from them, and you earn gold and silver. That opened the road to early theories of trade protection.

### Act two: Adam Smith and the "invisible hand" — the birth of economics (1776)

In 1776 Adam Smith published *The Wealth of Nations* and put forward the revolutionary claim: **division of labour creates wealth, and the "invisible hand" guides the working of the market**.

> "It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest."

Each person pursues his own interest, yet as if guided by an invisible hand he unknowingly promotes the interest of the whole society. **Let the government interfere little and let the market run on its own!**

### Act three: Ricardo and "comparative advantage" (1817)

David Ricardo put forward the **theory of comparative advantage**: even if you are better at everything than the other party, you should specialise in what you do best and then trade. That became the theoretical foundation of free trade.

### Act four: Say's law — supply creates demand (early 19th century)

Say claimed: "goods create their own demand." The money one person earns will in the end be spent and become somebody else's income, so **general overproduction cannot occur**. That view ruled economics for over a century.

### Act five: the marginal revolution — economics becomes a real science (1870s)

Jevons, Menger and Walras nearly thought of the same problem at the same time: **why is a diamond dearer than water?**

The answer is **marginal utility** — value depends on the satisfaction gained from one more unit. From then on economics adopted marginal analysis and calculus, and began studying economic problems by scientific method.

### Act six: Marshall — inventor of the supply-and-demand diagram (1890)

Marshall integrated earlier theory and invented the famous **crossed supply-and-demand diagram**. Where the supply and demand curves intersect is the equilibrium price. That framework is still the core of microeconomics today.

### Act seven: the Great Depression and the Keynesian revolution — economics' "great split" (1936)

The Great Depression of 1929 shattered the dream that "the market adjusts itself." Keynes pointed out:

> "In the long run we are all dead."

His core claims:
- **wages and prices are "sticky"** and do not adjust immediately
- **"deficient effective demand"** causes depression
- **government should intervene**, stimulating the economy through fiscal policy

After the **Keynesian revolution**, economics split into the two branches of micro and macro.

### Act eight: the monetarist counter-stroke — Friedman's challenge (1970s)

"Stagflation" in the 1970s (stagnation plus inflation) put Keynesian theory in difficulty. Friedman led the monetarist school in counter-attack:

> "Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon."

Government interference too heavy instead throws the economy into disorder; monetary policy should follow a "single rule" — let the money supply grow steadily.

### Act nine: the rational expectations revolution (1970s–80s)

New classical macroeconomics, with Lucas as its figurehead, proposed **rational expectations**: people use all available information to forecast the future rationally and adjust their behaviour, so **systematic macroeconomic policy is ineffective**.

### Act ten: New Keynesianism — using both hands (1980s–present)

New Keynesianism admits that people are rational, but that markets are not perfect. Price stickiness, wage stickiness, information asymmetry and other factors cause market failure, so **short-run government policy still works, but must be used with care**.

**The core quarrel is still running**: should government intervene in the market? Can the market fail? How do you balance efficiency and fairness?

Economics today is no longer an either-or argument, but a state of **schools borrowing from each other and developing together**. Every revolution is at once an inheritance and a superseding of what came before.

## I. The questions this group keeps returning to

- Why the demand curve slopes down to the right, and why elasticity matters.
- How a firm maximises profit under a cost constraint.
- What perfect competition, monopoly and market failure each mean.
- How national income, the interest rate and the general price level come to equilibrium in macro terms.
- How fiscal and monetary policy affect aggregate demand, aggregate supply and economic growth.

## II. How the theories were born and tested

Nearly every important theory here was born in a crisis, and every one of them has been corrected by empirical testing:

- **The Phillips curve (1958)**: Phillips found in a century of British data that unemployment and the wage growth rate are negatively correlated; in the 1960s it became a policy menu — take more inflation and you buy employment. Stagflation in the 1970s (high inflation and high unemployment together) falsified that menu outright, and Friedman and Phelps, after adding expectations, corrected it to "downward-sloping in the short run, vertical in the long run." One counterexample drove the whole expectations revolution in macroeconomics.
- **The Volcker tightening (1979–1982)**: Fed chair Volcker tested the quantity theory of money almost experimentally — tightened the money supply hard, inflation fell from 13% to 3%, the price being an unemployment rate past 10%. Monetarism was half right: inflation really is a monetary phenomenon, but the cost (the sacrifice ratio) was larger than the aggressive version predicted.
- **The Lucas critique (1976)**: model parameters estimated on historical data become invalid once the rule of policy changes — because people adjust their expectations. After that, macro models had to be rewritten entirely in micro-founded form, an example of a theory being corrected by logic rather than by data.
- **Agricultural elasticity**: "cheap grain hurts the farmer" is a classic verification of demand price elasticity below 1: a bumper harvest cuts the price by more than the extra volume adds, so total revenue falls.
