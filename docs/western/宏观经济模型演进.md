# The evolution of macroeconomic models

**A note on angle:** this page unfolds along each model's assumptions, mechanisms and explanatory edges; for the wider thread of economic thought and its subject lines see the [chronology of western economics](/western/History). The two pages are organised from different angles.

> **The core question of this page**: why does macroeconomics need a whole set of models rather than one "universal" model? From the 45° line to IS-LM, to AD-AS, to the Phillips curve, to growth models and the open economy — what question did each generation of models answer, and what new problem did it expose, that the next generation had to step in?

The chapters record the models in textbook order; this page strings them into one line of evolution. My observation: every model was born to answer a question the previous model could not answer, and every relaxation of an assumption (bringing in the interest rate, bringing in price, bringing in expectations, bringing in time, bringing in the foreign sector) was both a step closer to reality and a fresh exposure. Reading back through the chapters along this line makes it easier to see what each model is responsible for explaining.

## The main line of evolution, one diagram

```
凯恩斯革命（1936）
   │  问题：大萧条为什么出现大规模失业？
   ▼
45° 线模型 ── 只看产品市场，价格与利率都固定
   │  暴露：利率由什么决定？货币政策有用吗？
   ▼
IS-LM（希克斯 1937）── 两个市场联立，利率内生
   │  暴露：价格固定，无法分析通胀与供给
   ▼
AD-AS ── 价格内生，需求与供给会师
   │  暴露：通胀的动态过程与预期从哪来？
   ▼
菲利普斯曲线（1958 → 1968 预期革命）
   │  暴露：这只解释波动，长期增长由什么决定？
   ▼
增长模型（哈罗德-多马 1939 → 索洛 1956 → 内生增长 1980s）
   │  暴露：以上全是封闭经济
   ▼
蒙代尔-弗莱明（1962-1963）── 开放经济与汇率制度
```

One idea runs through the whole line: **models are not divided by right and wrong, but by division of labour** — there is no best model, only the model best suited to the question in front of you.

## Stop one: the 45° line model — the product market's short cycle

When the *General Theory* came out in 1936, Keynes did not have the diagram that today's textbooks show. Drawing "effective demand determines output" in its simplest form is a later textbook convention: keep only the product market, fix the price ($P=1$), take the interest rate as given from outside, and let the economy have idle resources.

The **equilibrium condition** is that total output equals total expenditure:

$$Y = AE = C + I + G$$

Substituting the linear consumption function $C = \alpha + \beta Y_d$ (with $\beta$ the marginal propensity to consume), equilibrium income is determined by exogenous expenditure. This model's sharpest output is the **multiplier**: a one-yuan rise in investment raises income by more than one yuan — because the new income becomes the next round of consumption.

$$\Delta Y = \Delta C + \Delta I = \beta\,\Delta Y + \Delta I \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta Y = \frac{\Delta I}{1-\beta} = \frac{\Delta I}{MPS}$$

The multiplier is the first step that turns "demand determines output" from a slogan into a computable proposition. But the model's price showed up immediately: the interest rate is assumed given, so the question "can a central bank cut rates to stimulate the economy" does not exist inside the model at all; the price is fixed, so inflation does not exist either. Those two gaps are the doorway to the next stop.

## Stop two: IS-LM — two markets solved jointly

Hicks's 1937 paper *Mr. Keynes and the "Classics"* compressed the *General Theory* into two markets and two curves: equilibrium in the product market gives the **IS** curve (interest rate and income negatively related), equilibrium in the money market gives the **LM** curve (interest rate and income positively related).

**Where IS comes from**: writing the savings–investment equilibrium $S(Y) = I(r)$ in linear form. The consumption function $C = \alpha + \beta Y$ yields the savings function $S = -\alpha + (1-\beta)Y$, and the investment function is $I = e - dr$; equating the two:

$$Y = \frac{\alpha + e}{1-\beta} - \frac{d}{1-\beta}\,r$$

Lower interest rate → more investment → magnified through the multiplier into a larger income. That is the whole content of IS's downward slope.

**Where LM comes from**: Keynes's theory of liquidity preference splits money demand into transaction and precautionary demand (rising with income) and speculative demand (falling with the interest rate): $L = kY - hr$. Money supply is exogenous, $\frac{M}{P}$; equating demand and supply:

$$Y = \frac{h}{k}\,r + \frac{1}{k}\cdot\frac{M}{P}$$

The higher the income, the larger transaction demand for money, so with money supply fixed only a higher interest rate can "crowd out" speculative demand to restore balance — that is why LM slopes up to the right. LM has two extreme regions: at a very low interest rate speculative demand is infinite (the **liquidity trap**, LM horizontal, monetary policy ineffective); at a very high interest rate speculative demand is zero (the **classical region**, LM vertical, monetary policy fully effective).

**The intersection of the two curves** determines both the interest rate and income at once. Policy analysis became clear for the first time inside this frame:

- **Fiscal policy** shifts IS: a rise in $G$ moves IS right, $Y$ rises and $r$ rises — the higher interest rate suppresses part of private investment, the **crowding-out effect**;
- **Monetary policy** shifts LM: a rise in $M$ moves LM right, $Y$ rises and $r$ falls.

IS-LM ruled post-war macroeconomics for thirty years. But its key assumption — fixed price — could no longer hold against the inflation of the 1970s: there is no price variable in the model, so there is no supply, and no place for stagflation.

## Stop three: AD-AS — letting price and supply in

Turn the price in IS-LM from "1" into a variable and the **aggregate demand curve** emerges on its own: price $P$ rises → real money supply $\frac{M}{P}$ falls → LM shifts left → the interest rate rises → investment and income fall. That is why AD slopes down to the right. Solving the linear IS-LM system simultaneously and eliminating $r$ gives an explicit aggregate demand function:

$$Y = \frac{h(\alpha+e)}{kd + h(1-\beta)} + \frac{Md}{\left[kd + h(1-\beta)\right]P}$$

$P$ sits in the denominator of the second term — the higher the price, the lower equilibrium income. Three parallel mechanisms lie behind it: the Keynes effect (the interest rate channel), the Pigou effect (the real balances channel, where a rising price dilutes wealth and depresses consumption), and in the open economy the Mundell–Fleming effect (a rising price weakens export competitiveness).

The **aggregate supply curve** is what AD-AS truly adds relative to IS-LM, and its shape depends on the flexibility of money wages:

- **Short run** (wages rigid downwards): production function $Y = AN^{\alpha}$, the firm hires up to $\frac{W}{P} = MP_N$. When the price $P$ rises and $W$ does not move, the real wage falls and employment and output rise — short-run AS slopes up to the right, writable as $Y = \gamma P^{\theta}$ (with $\theta = \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha} > 0$).
- **Long run** (wages fully flexible): price changes are eventually matched by money wages, the real wage and employment return to the full-employment level, and output locks onto potential output $Y^*$ — long-run AS is a vertical line.

**The short-run/long-run adjustment mechanism** is thereby clear: when output is below its potential level, unemployment exceeds the natural rate, and downward wage pressure shifts short-run AS gradually back to $Y^*$; when it is above, the reverse happens. Fiscal and monetary policy shift AD in the short run (changing output and price) and change only the price in the long run — the textbook version of "money is neutral in the long run."

**Stagflation** is the framework's most famous application: an adverse supply shock (the oil crises of the 1970s) shifts AS left, so output falls while price rises — demand management is stuck between horns, stimulating demand to protect output worsens inflation, squeezing inflation deepens the recession. It was precisely this dilemma that pushed "expectations" to centre stage.

## Stop four: the Phillips curve — the trade-off between inflation and unemployment, and its rise and fall

In 1958 Phillips found, in British data from 1861 to 1957, that the unemployment rate and the growth rate of money wages move steadily in opposite directions; in 1960 Samuelson and Solow rewrote it as a trade-off between inflation and unemployment — a "menu" made to serve policy: spend a little more inflation to buy a little less unemployment.

The critique of Friedman (1968) and Phelps ended that menu: the trade-off exists only **while expectations have not been fulfilled**. Expansionary policy cuts unemployment → workers notice their real wage has fallen → they demand a compensating nominal raise → unemployment returns to its natural rate, leaving only higher inflation. Writing expectations into the price adjustment equation:

$$\pi_t = \pi_t^e + h\,\frac{Y_t - Y_f}{Y_f}$$

In the long run $\pi_t^e$ catches up with actual inflation, and the unemployment rate is no longer related to any inflation level — the long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment. Stagflation in the 1970s (inflation and unemployment rising together) is explained cleanly by this frame: an upward shift in expected inflation stacked on an oil price shock moves the whole curve to the right. Today's New Keynesian Phillips curve (the forward-looking-expectations version with sticky prices) is still the standard central bank tool for analysing the dynamics of inflation.

## Stop five: growth models — from fluctuations to the long run

Once fluctuations are explained, another set of questions surfaces: why are some countries rich and some poor? Can growth last?

**Harrod–Domar** (1939/1946) is the first systematic growth model, and also a warning: under the assumption of a fixed capital–output ratio $v$, the growth rate is $G = \frac{s}{v}$. Stable growth at full employment requires the actual, warranted and natural growth rates to be equal ($G_A = G_W = G_N$) — a "knife-edge" condition that is almost impossible to satisfy, and the smallest deviation diverges. Growth on a knife edge invited the relaxation of the assumptions.

**Solow** (1956) turned the capital–output ratio into a variable: capital and labour are substitutable, with diminishing marginal returns. The per-capita capital accumulation equation

$$\dot{k} = sf(k) - (n+\delta)k$$

splits growth into "capital deepening" from savings and "capital widening" from population growth plus depreciation. Under the steady state $sf(k^*) = (n+\delta)k^*$, the only remaining source of long-run per-capita output growth is the rate of technological progress. Three classical conclusions follow: the savings rate changes only the steady-state level, not the steady-state growth rate; conditional convergence (poor countries grow faster when the other parameters are close); and the golden rule $f'(k) = n+\delta$, which characterises the steady state maximising per-capita consumption. Solow's soft spot is equally clear: technological progress is exogenous — the most important source of growth is put outside the model.

**Endogenous growth** (1980s, Romer and others) took that soft spot head-on: let technology be determined by decisions inside the economic system (R&D spending, the accumulation of knowledge). The simplest AK model, $Y = AK$, abolishes diminishing marginal returns to capital, so sustained growth no longer depends on exogenous technology; policy changes the long-run growth rate for the first time — investment in education, R&D subsidies and the protection of intellectual property are no longer only level effects, but growth effects.

## Stop six: the open economy — Mundell–Fleming and the impossible trinity

The last gap is the national border. Mundell (1963) and Fleming (1962) extended IS-LM to the open economy: in the product market net exports depend on the real exchange rate, $NX(\varepsilon)$; the interest rate is tied to capital flows and the exchange rate, and under full capital mobility the domestic rate is pinned to the world rate, $r = r^*$.

The conclusions condense into one table (small open economy, perfectly mobile capital):

| Exchange rate regime | Fiscal policy | Monetary policy |
|---------|---------|---------|
| Fixed | Highly effective | Ineffective |
| Floating | Ineffective | Highly effective |

The mechanism is the same in both cases: **capital flows force the monetary authority's hand**. Under a fixed rate, fiscal expansion raises the interest rate and attracts capital inflows, so the central bank must buy foreign exchange and release domestic currency, LM is pushed right passively, and the expansion is magnified; monetary expansion lowers the rate, capital flows out, and the central bank has no choice but to pull domestic currency back, cancelling the expansion itself. Under a floating rate it is the reverse: fiscal expansion raises the rate, the currency appreciates, net exports fall, and the crowding-out comes entirely from outside; monetary expansion magnifies net exports through depreciation, doubling the effect.

Draw exchange rate regimes, capital mobility and monetary independence as a triangle and you have the **Mundell impossible trinity**: at most two of the three can be held at once. Hold monetary independence and exchange rate stability and you must control capital (China's former choice); want free capital flows and monetary independence and you must let the rate float (the United States); want exchange rate stability and capital mobility and you must give up independent monetary policy (the peg of Hong Kong).

## Comparing the model systems

| Model | Coordinate plane | Endogenous variables | Key assumptions | Main uses |
|------|---------|---------|---------|---------|
| 45° line | (Y, AE) | Equilibrium income | Fixed price and interest rate | Intuition for the multiplier and demand management |
| IS-LM | (Y, r) | Income, interest rate | Fixed price | Short-run policy analysis (fiscal vs monetary) |
| AD-AS | (Y, P) | Income, price | Short-run wage rigidity | Fluctuations, stagflation, the short/long run split |
| Phillips curve | (π, u) | Inflation dynamics | How expectations are formed | The edge of the inflation–unemployment trade-off |
| Harrod–Domar | — | Growth rate | Fixed $v$ | The inherent instability of growth |
| Solow model | (k, growth) | Steady-state capital | Diminishing marginal returns | Convergence, the roles of savings and technology |
| Endogenous growth | — | Long-run growth rate | Knowledge / non-diminishing returns | Policy's effect on growth |
| Mundell–Fleming | (Y, e) | Income, exchange rate | Capital mobility | Policy mixes under different exchange rate regimes |

Looking along the time dimension: the ultra-short run (prices fully rigid) is IS-LM's territory, the medium run (prices adjustable) belongs to AD-AS and the Phillips curve, and the long run (full employment, prices fully flexible) is the world of growth models; along the openness dimension, the closed economy models are completed into the foreign sector by Mundell–Fleming.

## Limits and what follows

This line of evolution runs only to the 1980s; progress since has continued on the same logic:

- **Rational expectations and the Lucas critique** (1976): model parameters change with the rule of policy, so "evaluating a new policy with historical parameters" is not logically sound — DSGE rewrote all the models above at the level of method, but the standing of IS-LM and AD-AS as teaching tools and as fast policy intuition has never been shaken.
- **Real business cycles and the new new classical synthesis**: fluctuations no longer come only from demand, and the quarrel over technology shocks and price stickiness merges inside the DSGE frame.
- **The zero lower bound and the return of the liquidity trap**: after 2008 the liquidity trap stopped being a textbook footnote and became the reality of the major economies, and the Keynesian region of IS-LM regained explanatory power.
- **The global financial cycle** (Rey 2013): under free capital flows a small country's monetary independence is less than the impossible trinity predicted — the "trilemma" is really a dilemma.
- **Behavioural macro and heterogeneity**: the representative-agent and full-rationality assumptions are supplemented by behavioural corrections and heterogeneous-agent models (HANK), bringing the micro-foundations of consumption and inflation dynamics closer to the data.

There is no "best" model, only a "best-suited" model — only by seeing what each model assumed and what it let go can you choose the right tool for the question at hand. That is both this page's closing and the question one carries while reading the chapters.
