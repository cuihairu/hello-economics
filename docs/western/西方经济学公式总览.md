# A formula overview of western economics

This file gathers the core formulas of each chapter of western economics into one place, as a quick-reference tool alongside the main text: what problem each formula addresses, where it is derived from, and at which step it is most easily misused. The full chain of reasoning and the economic meaning are set out in the chapters of the main text; only the leanest skeleton is kept here.

## Chapter 1 · Demand and supply

### The basic representation of demand and supply

$$Q_d = Q_d(P)$$

The quantity demanded is a function of price.

$$Q_s = Q_s(P)$$

The quantity supplied is a function of price.

### Market equilibrium

$$Q_d(P^*) = Q_s(P^*)$$

The equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity are determined by demand equaling supply.

### The price elasticity of demand

$$E_d=-\frac{dQ}{dP}\cdot\frac{P}{Q}$$

Measures how much the quantity demanded changes when price changes by 1%.

### The cross-price elasticity

$$E_{XY}=\frac{\Delta Q_X/Q_X}{\Delta P_Y/P_Y}=\frac{\Delta Q_X}{\Delta P_Y}\cdot\frac{P_Y}{Q_X}$$

$$E_{XY}=\frac{dQ_X}{dP_Y}\cdot\frac{P_Y}{Q_X}$$

Measures the effect of a change in the price of good $Y$ on the demand for good $X$.

### Where the formulas come from

- The equilibrium formula comes from the "market-clearing condition", that is, quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
- The point-elasticity formula comes from "the ratio of the rate of change of quantity demanded to the rate of change of price".
- The cross-elasticity formula comes from "how sensitive the demand for one good is to a change in the price of another".

### Common misuses

- Do not mix point elasticity and arc elasticity.
- The price elasticity of demand is usually written with a negative sign because price and quantity demanded usually move in opposite directions.
- When analysing a change in total revenue, first judge whether demand is elastic or inelastic, and only then the consequences of raising or cutting price.

## Chapter 2 · Utility theory

### Marginal utility

$$MU=\frac{\Delta TU}{\Delta Q}$$

The addition to total utility from consuming one more unit.

### Consumer equilibrium under cardinal utility theory

$$
\begin{cases}
\frac{MU_1}{P_1}=\frac{MU_2}{P_2}=\cdots=\frac{MU_n}{P_n}=\lambda \\
P_1X_1+\cdots+P_nX_n=I
\end{cases}
$$

The marginal utility per unit of money spent is equal across goods, and the budget constraint holds.

### The budget constraint

$$P_1X_1+P_2X_2=I$$

### Indifference curves

$$U=f(X_1,X_2)=U_0$$

### The marginal rate of substitution

$$MRS_{1,2}=-\frac{\Delta X_2}{\Delta X_1}$$

$$MRS_{1,2}=\lim_{\Delta X_1 \to 0}-\frac{\Delta X_2}{\Delta X_1}=-\frac{dX_2}{dX_1}$$

### Where the formulas come from

- The cardinal equilibrium condition comes from "the marginal utility of the last unit of money must be equal everywhere".
- The ordinal optimum comes from "the tangency of the budget line with an indifference curve".
- The marginal rate of substitution comes from "the substitution relation between two goods at constant utility".

### Common misuses

- Do not confuse equality of $MU/P$ with equality of $MU$.
- The slope of an indifference curve is negative, and $MRS$ is normally taken in absolute value to express the strength of substitution.
- A Giffen good is not an ordinary inferior good but the special case where "the negative income effect outweighs the substitution effect".

## Chapter 3 · Production and cost

### The production function

$$Q=f(L,K)$$

### Marginal product

$$MP=\frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta L}$$

$$MP_L=\frac{dQ}{dL}$$

$$MP_K=\frac{dQ}{dK}$$

### The marginal rate of technical substitution

$$MRTS_{LK}=-\frac{\Delta K}{\Delta L}$$

$$MRTS_{LK}=-\frac{dK}{dL}$$

### The isocost equation

$$C=r_LL+r_KK$$

### Cost minimisation or output maximisation at given cost

$$
\begin{cases}
MRTS_{LK}=\frac{r_L}{r_K} \\
r_LL+r_KK=C
\end{cases}
$$

or written as

$$
\begin{cases}
\frac{MP_L}{r_L}=\frac{MP_K}{r_K} \\
r_LL+r_KK=C
\end{cases}
$$

### Cost minimisation at a given output

$$
\begin{cases}
MRTS_{LK}=\frac{r_L}{r_K} \\
f(L,K)=y
\end{cases}
$$

### Marginal cost

$$MC(Q)=\frac{\Delta TC(Q)}{\Delta Q}$$

$$MC(Q)=\lim_{\Delta Q \to 0}\frac{\Delta TC(Q)}{\Delta Q}=\frac{dTC}{dQ}$$

### The relation between marginal cost and marginal product

$$MC=\frac{dTC}{dQ}=W\cdot\frac{dL}{dQ}=\frac{W}{MP_L}=\frac{r_L}{MP_L}$$

### The firm's profit

$$\pi=TR-TC=PQ-TC$$

### The first-order condition for the firm's use of factors

$$P\cdot MP_L-r_L=0$$

$$P\cdot MP_K-r_K=0$$

which further gives

$$\frac{r_L}{MP_L}=\frac{r_K}{MP_K}$$

### Where the formulas come from

- Marginal product comes from differentiating the production function with respect to the factor input.
- The cost-minimisation condition comes from the tangency of an isoquant with an isocost line.
- Marginal cost comes from differentiating the total cost function with respect to output.
- $MC=\frac{r_L}{MP_L}$ comes from the chain rule, linking the change in cost to the change in output.

### Common misuses

- $MRTS$ is a technological substitution relation, not the price ratio itself; the two coincide only at the optimum.
- Do not identify returns to scale with economies of scale outright: the former is about output and factors changing in the same proportion, the latter about cost.
- The rational input range is normally Stage II of production, not the stage where marginal product is negative.

## Chapter 4 · Market theory

### Total revenue, average revenue, marginal revenue

$$TR=p(y)\cdot y$$

$$AR=\frac{TR}{y}=p(y)$$

$$MR=\frac{\Delta TR}{\Delta y}$$

### The firm's profit-maximisation principle

$$MR=MC$$

### A complete worked derivation for a monopolist

If

$$Q=50-3P$$

then the inverse demand function is

$$P=\frac{50}{3}-\frac{Q}{3}$$

the total revenue function is

$$TR=PQ=\frac{50Q}{3}-\frac{Q^2}{3}$$

and the marginal revenue function is

$$MR=\frac{dTR}{dQ}=\frac{50}{3}-\frac{2Q}{3}$$

If $MC=4$, the optimality condition is

$$\frac{50}{3}-\frac{2Q}{3}=4$$

### Where the formulas come from

- Total revenue is obtained from "price times quantity".
- Marginal revenue is obtained by differentiating the total revenue function with respect to output.
- The firm's optimum comes from the first-order condition of the profit function $\pi(Q)=TR(Q)-TC(Q)$.

### Common misuses

- Under monopoly one normally solves for output from $MR=MC$ first, then substitutes back into the demand function for the price.
- Under perfect competition $MR=P$, but under monopoly and monopolistic competition usually $MR<P$.
- The decision variable in the Cournot model is output, not price.

## Chapter 5 · The market for factors of production

### The value of the marginal product

$$VMP=P\cdot MP$$

### Marginal revenue product

$$MRP=MR\cdot MP$$

### The profit-maximisation principle for the use of factors

$$MRP=MFC$$

In perfectly competitive product and factor markets this can be written

$$VMP=P\times MP=r$$

### Euler's theorem

$$Q=L\cdot\frac{\partial Q}{\partial L}+K\cdot\frac{\partial Q}{\partial K}$$

or written as

$$Q=K\cdot MP_K+L\cdot MP_L$$

### The identity behind profit maximisation

$$\frac{d\pi}{dL}=\frac{dTR}{dL}-\frac{dTC}{dL}$$

$$\frac{dTR}{dL}=\frac{dTR}{dQ}\cdot\frac{dQ}{dL}=MR\cdot MPP=MRP$$

### Where the formulas come from

- Factor demand derives from product demand, which is why it is called a derived demand.
- $VMP=P\cdot MP$ comes from "marginal product times the price of the product".
- $MRP=MFC$ comes from the first-order condition of profit with respect to the factor input.

### Common misuses

- In a perfectly competitive product market $MR=P$, so $MRP=VMP$; under imperfect competition the two differ.
- In a monopsonistic factor market, $MFC$ is normally above the factor price.
- A backward-bending labour supply is a conclusion for the range of high wages, not a bend that occurs at every wage level.

## Chapter 6 · General equilibrium and welfare economics

### Walras's law

$$\sum_{i=1}^{k+r}p_i x_i=\sum_{i=1}^{k+r}p_i y_i$$

### The marginal rate of transformation

$$MRT=\lim_{\Delta X \to 0}\left|\frac{\Delta Y}{\Delta X}\right|=\left|\frac{dY}{dX}\right|$$

### General equilibrium in the product and factor markets

$$x_i=x_i(p_1,\cdots,p_k,p_{k+1},\cdots,p_{k+r})$$

$$y_i=y_i(p_1,\cdots,p_k,p_{k+1},\cdots,p_{k+r})$$

$$x_i=y_i \qquad (i=1,\cdots,k)$$

$$x_j=x_j(p_1,\cdots,p_k;p_{k+1},\cdots,p_{k+r})$$

$$y_j=y_j(p_1,\cdots,p_k;p_{k+1},\cdots,p_{k+r})$$

$$x_j=y_j \qquad (j=k+1,\cdots,k+r)$$

### Total endowments in the Edgeworth box

$$w_1=w_{11}+w_{21}$$

$$w_2=w_{12}+w_{22}$$

### The condition for Pareto optimality in pure exchange

$$MRS_{XY}^A=MRS_{XY}^B$$

### The condition for Pareto optimality in production

$$MRTS_{LK}^X=MRTS_{LK}^Y$$

### The condition for simultaneous optimality in exchange and production

$$MRS_{XY}=MRT_{XY}$$

### Where the formulas come from

- General equilibrium comes from "all markets clearing at once".
- The Pareto conditions come from "if the marginal rates of substitution are not equal, there is still room for improvement".
- Walras's law comes from the aggregate identity "total expenditure is identically equal to total income".

### Common misuses

- Walras's law says the number of independent equations is limited; it does not mean the full solution for prices falls out automatically.
- $MRS$, $MRTS$ and $MRT$ correspond respectively to consumption, production and product transformation — do not learn them mixed up.

## Chapter 7 · Market failure and microeconomic policy

### The efficiency loss of monopoly

Under monopoly $P>MC$, and the loss of allocative efficiency is measured by deadweight loss. With linear demand and constant marginal cost:

$$DWL=\frac{1}{2}(P_m-MC)\,(Q_c-Q_m)$$

where $P_m$ and $Q_m$ are the monopoly price and output, and $Q_c$ the competitive output.

### The social optimum in the presence of an externality

Marginal social cost equals marginal private cost plus marginal external cost:

$$MSC=MPC+MEC$$

A Pigouvian tax makes the private decision face the true social cost:

$$t=MEC\quad(\text{at the optimal output})$$

### The efficient provision of public goods

The Samuelson condition: the marginal willingness to pay of all consumers summed equals marginal cost:

$$\sum_{i} MRS_i = MC$$

The essential difference from the optimality condition for private goods (each $MRS=MRT$ on its own) lies in the non-rivalry of public goods — the same unit of the good enters everyone's utility at once.

### Where the formulas come from

- Deadweight loss comes from "between monopoly output and competitive output, the excess of willingness to pay over marginal cost that no one any longer captures".
- The Pigouvian tax comes from "internalising the external cost, so that the decision-maker faces the full social marginal cost".
- The summation condition for public goods comes from "non-rivalry makes marginal social benefit the vertical sum of individual marginal benefits".

### Common misuses

- Deadweight loss is a net loss to society as a whole, not a loss to the consumer or the producer alone.
- The level of a Pigouvian tax depends on the marginal external cost at the optimal output; it is not a fixed rate.
- The Coase theorem holds when property rights are clear and transaction costs are low enough; when transaction costs are high, the initial allocation of rights affects the outcome.

## Chapter 8 · Macroeconomic activity and macroeconomics

### The unemployment rate

$$\text{unemployment rate}=\frac{\text{number of unemployed}}{\text{total labour force}}\times 100\%$$

### Nominal and real national income

$$\text{real national income}=\text{nominal national income} \div \text{price deflator}$$

## Chapter 9 · The theory of simple national income determination

### The consumption function

$$C=C(Y)$$

or in linear form

$$C=\alpha+\beta Y_d$$

### The marginal propensity to consume

$$MPC=\frac{\Delta C}{\Delta Y}$$

### The saving function

$$S=Y-C$$

### The marginal propensity to save

$$MPS=\frac{\Delta S}{\Delta Y}$$

### Equilibrium in a two-sector economy

$$Y=C+I$$

$$Y=C+S$$

$$I=S$$

### The investment multiplier

$$\Delta Y=\frac{\Delta I}{1-\beta}$$

### Equilibrium in a three-sector economy

$$C=\alpha+\beta(Y-T_0)$$

$$C+I+G=\alpha+\beta(Y-T_0)+I_0+G_0=Y$$

$$Y=\frac{\alpha-\beta T_0+I_0+G_0}{1-\beta}$$

### The balanced-budget multiplier

$$\Delta Y=\frac{\Delta G}{1-\beta}-\frac{\beta\Delta T}{1-\beta}=\Delta G \; (= \Delta T)$$

### Where the formulas come from

- Two-sector equilibrium comes from $AE=Y$, rewritten as $C+I=C+S$, which gives $I=S$.
- The multiplier formula comes from substituting the consumption function into the equilibrium condition and finding the effect of a change in exogenous spending on income.
- The three-sector formula comes from adding government purchases and taxes to aggregate expenditure and to disposable income.

### Common misuses

- $MPC+MPS=1$ holds when the increment to income is split between consumption and saving.
- The tax multiplier normally carries a negative sign.
- A balanced-budget multiplier equal to 1 does not mean fiscal policy has no effect; it means the increment to income equals the increment to government spending.

## Chapter 10 · General equilibrium of the product and money markets

### The marginal efficiency of capital

$$R_0=\frac{R_1}{1+r_c}+\frac{R_2}{(1+r_c)^2}+\cdots+\frac{R_n}{(1+r_c)^n}$$

### The investment function

$$I=I(r)$$

### The money demand function

$$L=L_1(Y)+L_2(r)$$

### Money market equilibrium

$$L_1(Y)+L_2(r)=m$$

### The IS curve

$$S(Y)=I(r)$$

### The LM curve

$$L_1(Y)+L_2(r)=m$$

### Example: from the consumption function to the saving function

$$S=Y-C=-100+0.4Y$$

### Example: deriving the IS curve

$$-100+0.4Y=520-r$$

$$Y=1550-2.5r$$

### Example: deriving the LM curve

$$0.2Y-4r=120$$

$$Y=600+20r$$

### Solving IS-LM jointly

$$
\begin{cases}
Y=1550-2.5r \\
Y=600+20r
\end{cases}
$$

### Where the formulas come from

- The IS curve comes from product market equilibrium, $S(Y)=I(r)$.
- The LM curve comes from money market equilibrium, $L(Y,r)=m$.
- Overall equilibrium comes from the product and money markets clearing at the same time.

### Common misuses

- First derive the saving function from the consumption function, then write IS — do not jump ahead.
- If the money supply in LM is written as the real money supply, check whether it is $M/P$.
- The simple multiplier model ignores the feedback through interest rates; IS-LM builds in the money market constraint.

## Chapter 11 · Macroeconomic policy

### Deposit creation and total deposits

$$\Delta M=\frac{\Delta D}{r_d}$$

where $\Delta D$ is the initial increase in original deposits, $r_d$ is the required reserve ratio, and $\Delta M$ is total deposits inclusive of the original deposit (a different notion from the money multiplier $m=\frac{1+c}{r_d+e+c}$, which belongs to the money supply chapter of money and banking).

### The expansion of derived deposits

$$100+100(1-10\%)+100(1-10\%)^2+\cdots =1000$$

The series sum 1000 is total deposits inclusive of the original 100 (reserve ratio 10%), of which derived deposits are 900.

### The derived-deposit multiplier

$$\text{derived deposits}=\text{original deposits}\times\left(\frac{1}{\text{deposit reserve ratio}}-1\right)$$

## Chapter 12 · Aggregate demand and aggregate supply analysis

### Deriving aggregate demand from IS-LM

$$
\begin{cases}
I(r)=S(Y) \\
L_1(Y)+L_2(r)=\frac{M}{P}
\end{cases}
$$

### Example: deriving the aggregate demand function

$$
\begin{cases}
2200-100r=Y-800-0.8Y \\
0.5Y-250r=\frac{600}{P}
\end{cases}
$$

$$2Y-15000=\frac{1200}{P}$$

$$Y=7500+\frac{600}{P}$$

### Where the formulas come from

- The AD curve comes from a change in the price level changing the real money supply, which then works through IS-LM onto equilibrium income.
- Short-run AS comes from money-wage rigidity: price changes move the real wage and employment.
- Long-run AS comes from fully flexible wages and the economy returning to full employment.

### Common misuses

- When deriving AD, do not mix the nominal money supply $M$ with the real money supply $M/P$.
- A vertical long-run aggregate supply does not mean output never changes; it means long-run equilibrium output is determined by potential output.

## Chapter 13 · Economic growth

### The Harrod-Domar model

$$G=\frac{s}{v}$$

### The warranted growth rate

$$G_w=\frac{S_d}{v_r}$$

### The neoclassical growth model

$$sf(k)=\Delta k+nk$$

Savings per worker split into capital deepening ($\Delta k$) and capital widening ($nk$).

### The steady-state condition

$$sf(k)=nk$$

### The golden-rule condition

$$f'(k)=n$$

### Harrod-Domar's stable growth condition

$$G_A=G_W=G_N$$

### Where the formulas come from

- The Harrod-Domar growth rate comes from "savings turning into investment, and investment driving output growth".
- The Solow steady state comes from "savings per worker exactly covering capital widening".
- The golden rule comes from the condition on the optimal capital stock that "maximises consumption per worker".

### Common misuses

- Do not mix the Harrod-Domar and Solow models together; one stresses knife-edge growth, the other convergence to a steady state.
- The golden-rule condition is an optimal steady state, not just any steady state.

## Chapter 14 · Inflation theory

### The consumer price index

$$CPI=\frac{\text{the value of a fixed basket of goods at current prices}}{\text{the value of the same fixed basket at base-period prices}}\times 100\%$$

### The price adjustment equation

$$\pi_t=\pi_t^e+h\frac{(Y_t-Y_f)}{Y_f}$$

### A worked recursion of inflation

$$\pi_2=18\%+0.4\frac{90-100}{100}=14\%$$

$$\pi_3=14\%+0.4\frac{90-100}{100}=10\%$$

$$\pi_4=10\%+0.4\frac{90-100}{100}=6\%$$

$$\pi_5=6\%+0.4\frac{90-100}{100}=2\%$$

### Where the formulas come from

- The CPI comes from comparing "the price of a fixed basket" with its base-period price.
- The price adjustment equation comes from "expected inflation + demand pressure" jointly determining current inflation.

### Common misuses

- If expectations are formed from last period's inflation (adaptive expectations), replace $\pi_t^e$ with $\pi_{t-1}$ before recursing.
- When the output gap $\frac{Y_t-Y_f}{Y_f}$ is negative, it pushes the inflation rate down.

## Chapter 15 · Disagreements in macroeconomics

### The equation of exchange (the quantity theory of money)

$$MV=PT$$

With the velocity of money $V$ and the volume of transactions $T$ stable, changes in the money supply are reflected proportionally in prices — the starting point of monetarism.

### The Lucas supply curve (the surprise supply function)

$$Y=Y^*+\alpha\,(P-P^e)\qquad(\alpha>0)$$

Output departs from potential only when the price level turns out unexpectedly — once expectations are fulfilled, only prices rise and output is unchanged. This puts "expectations" into aggregate supply.

### The policy implication under rational expectations

If expectations are rational ($P^e=P$), systematic demand management cannot change output:

$$Y=Y^*$$

Anticipated policy is ineffective; only unanticipated policy works in the short run — which is why policy rules and policy credibility became central objects of macroeconomic analysis.

### Where the formulas come from

- The equation of exchange comes from the identity "money is the medium of exchange, so its quantity times its velocity equals the total value of transactions".
- The Lucas supply curve comes from "workers and firms decide on expectations, and only expectation errors change actual supply".
- The rational-expectations conclusion comes from "substituting rational expectations into the supply function, whereupon the expectation-error term vanishes".

### Common misuses

- $MV=PT$ is an identity; the quantity theory holds only after adding the assumption that $V$ and $T$ are stable.
- "Anticipated policy is ineffective" depends on rational expectations and instantly flexible wages and prices; in the New Keynesian framework, price stickiness still leaves systematic policy a short-run role.

## Derivation points by module

### The elasticity module

**Core formula**

$$E_d=-\frac{dQ}{dP}\cdot\frac{P}{Q}$$

**Derivation order**

1. Rearrange the demand function as $Q=f(P)$.
2. Differentiate with respect to price to get $dQ/dP$.
3. Substitute the $P$ and $Q$ of the point under examination.
4. Compare $E_d$ with 1, then analyse the change in total revenue.

**Common misuses**

- Do not drop the negative sign.
- When substituting, use the quantity demanded at that price for $Q$.

### The consumer optimum module

**Core formula**

$$
\begin{cases}
\frac{MU_1}{P_1}=\frac{MU_2}{P_2}=\lambda \\
P_1X_1+P_2X_2=I
\end{cases}
$$

or

$$MRS_{12}=\frac{P_1}{P_2}$$

**Derivation order**

1. Write the budget constraint.
2. Write the marginal-utility or marginal-rate-of-substitution condition.
3. Solve the two jointly for the optimal consumption bundle.

**Common misuses**

- The optimum must satisfy not only the slope condition but also the budget constraint.

### The firm optimum module

**Core formula**

$$MR=MC$$

**Derivation order**

1. Given the demand function, first derive the inverse demand function.
2. Then write the total revenue function $TR(Q)$.
3. Differentiate $TR$ to get $MR$.
4. Set $MR=MC$ and solve for output first.
5. Then return to the demand function for the price.

**Common misuses**

- Do not take the quantity solved from $MR=MC$ directly as the price.

### The IS-LM module

**Core formula**

$$S(Y)=I(r)$$

$$L(Y,r)=m$$

**Derivation order**

1. First derive the saving function from the consumption function.
2. Write IS.
3. Write LM.
4. Solve jointly for $Y$ and $r$.

**Common misuses**

- IS comes from the product market and LM from the money market — do not write their origins the wrong way round.

### The AD-AS module

**Core formula**

$$
\begin{cases}
I(r)=S(Y) \\
L(Y,r)=\frac{M}{P}
\end{cases}
$$

**Derivation order**

1. Put the price level $P$ into the real money supply.
2. Solve IS-LM jointly.
3. Eliminate $r$ to get $Y(P)$.

**Common misuses**

- This is the aggregate demand function, not the aggregate supply function.

### The growth module

**Core formula**

$$G=\frac{s}{v}$$

$$sf(k)=nk$$

**Derivation order**

1. The analytical focus of Harrod-Domar is the savings rate and the capital-output ratio.
2. The analytical focus of the Solow model is the savings-per-worker curve and the capital-widening line.

**Common misuses**

- Do not fold the growth-rate formula and the steady-state condition into one model.

## Common derivation chains

### Chain 1: from the demand function to the firm's optimal price

$$Q=f(P)$$

$\downarrow$ first invert into a demand function in terms of price

$$P=P(Q)$$

$\downarrow$

$$TR=P(Q)\cdot Q$$

$\downarrow$

$$MR=\frac{dTR}{dQ}$$

$\downarrow$

$$MR=MC$$

$\downarrow$ solve for $Q^*$ first

$$P^*=P(Q^*)$$

**Applies to**

- A monopolist's optimal output and optimal price
- Optimal decisions of imperfectly competitive firms

### Chain 2: from the utility function to the optimal consumption bundle

$$U=U(X_1,X_2)$$

$\downarrow$ take marginal utilities

$$MU_1,\ MU_2$$

$\downarrow$

$$\frac{MU_1}{P_1}=\frac{MU_2}{P_2}$$

$\downarrow$ then solve jointly with

$$P_1X_1+P_2X_2=I$$

$\downarrow$

$$X_1^*,X_2^*$$

**Applies to**

- Consumer equilibrium
- The tangency condition between an indifference curve and the budget line

### Chain 3: from the production function to the optimal factor inputs

$$Q=f(L,K)$$

$\downarrow$

$$MP_L,\ MP_K$$

$\downarrow$

$$\frac{MP_L}{r_L}=\frac{MP_K}{r_K}$$

$\downarrow$

solve jointly with the cost constraint or the output constraint

$\downarrow$

$$L^*,K^*$$

**Applies to**

- Cost minimisation
- The optimal factor combination

### Chain 4: from the consumption function to simple Keynesian equilibrium

$$C=\alpha+\beta Y$$

$\downarrow$

$$Y=C+I$$

$\downarrow$

$$Y=\alpha+\beta Y+I$$

$\downarrow$

solve for $Y^*$

$\downarrow$

$$S=Y-C$$

**Applies to**

- The simple model of national income determination
- Multiplier analysis

### Chain 5: from the consumption function to the IS curve

$$C=\alpha+\beta Y$$

$\downarrow$

$$S=Y-C$$

$\downarrow$

$$S(Y)=I(r)$$

$\downarrow$

rearrange to get

$$Y=a-br$$

**Applies to**

- Deriving the IS curve
- Analysing the effects of fiscal policy

### Chain 6: from money demand to the LM curve

$$L=L(Y,r)$$

$\downarrow$

$$L(Y,r)=m$$

$\downarrow$

rearrange to get

$$Y=c+dr$$

**Applies to**

- Deriving the LM curve
- Analysing the effects of monetary policy

### Chain 7: from IS-LM to the aggregate demand curve

$$
\begin{cases}
S(Y)=I(r) \\
L(Y,r)=\frac{M}{P}
\end{cases}
$$

$\downarrow$

eliminate $r$

$\downarrow$

to get

$$Y=Y(P)$$

**Applies to**

- Deriving the aggregate demand curve
- Analysing the effect of a change in the price level on output

### Chain 8: from the output gap to the path of inflation

$$\pi_t=\pi_t^e+h\frac{(Y_t-Y_f)}{Y_f}$$

$\downarrow$ under adaptive expectations

$$\pi_t^e=\pi_{t-1}$$

$\downarrow$

recurse period by period

$\downarrow$

$$\pi_2,\pi_3,\pi_4,\dots$$

**Applies to**

- Analysing the cost of fighting inflation by engineering a recession
- The dynamic path of inflation
