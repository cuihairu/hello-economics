# The historical thread of western economics

**Note on approach:** this page organises reading notes around the changes in economic thought and in the questions being asked; the assumptions, mechanisms and proper applications of the macro models are treated on the page [the evolution of macroeconomic models](/western/宏观经济模型演进). The two pages keep their own perspectives.

This is not a table of contents, nor a plain list of "who proposed what." What really needs to be caught is a chain of theoretical advance: whom the next theory drew on, which inadequacy in the preceding theory it set out to repair, at which step it went beyond its predecessors, and what new problems it left behind.

## The question tree and the progress towards answers

> How to read this: each step is recorded as "advance the question -> the gist -> the matching chapters"; if one step touches several places in the text, all are listed.

### Great question 1: why can markets form order, and why can prices allocate resources

- Sub-question 1.1: how do the price and quantity of a single commodity market take shape.
  Matching chapter: [Chapter 1 · Demand and supply](/western/需求与供给)
- Sub-question 1.2: why does the consumer choose as they do.
  Matching chapter: [Chapter 2 · Utility theory](/western/效用论)
- Sub-question 1.3: why does the firm produce as it does, and why do costs change.
  Matching chapter: [Chapter 3 · Production and cost](/western/生产和成本论)
- Sub-question 1.4: why do price and output differ across market structures.
  Matching chapter: [Chapter 4 · Market theory](/western/市场理论)
- Sub-question 1.5: how do factor prices such as wages, interest and rent take shape.
  Matching chapter: [Chapter 5 · The market for factors of production](/western/生产要素市场)
- The progress towards answers:
  1. Smith answered first: why the market is not naturally chaotic.
     Gist: market order can be coordinated spontaneously by the division of labour, exchange, prices and competition, without depending on unified central command.
     Matching chapters: [Chapter 1 · Demand and supply](/western/需求与供给) · [Chapter 4 · Market theory](/western/市场理论) · [Chapter 6 · General equilibrium and welfare economics](/western/一般均衡论和福利经济学)
  2. Ricardo advanced to: once the market runs, how is income distributed.
     Gist: the market determines not only exchange but also how the shares of income — wages, profits and rent — are distributed.
     Matching chapter: [Chapter 5 · The market for factors of production](/western/生产要素市场)
  3. The marginal revolution revised it: price cannot be seen from production alone; it must also be seen from subjective utility.
     Gist: price is shaped by production and cost, and also depends on the consumer's subjective appraisal of the last unit of a good.
     Matching chapters: [Chapter 1 · Demand and supply](/western/需求与供给) · [Chapter 2 · Utility theory](/western/效用论)
  4. Marshall assembled it into: a complete micro framework of demand, supply, cost and market structure.
     Gist: demand, supply, cost and market structure can be placed inside one framework of partial-equilibrium analysis.
     Matching chapters: [Chapter 1 · Demand and supply](/western/需求与供给) · [Chapter 3 · Production and cost](/western/生产和成本论) · [Chapter 4 · Market theory](/western/市场理论)
  5. The boundary of this round: it explained how markets run, but not whether market outcomes are efficient as a whole.
     Gist: what was explained here is how a single market runs; it has not been shown that the whole economy is simultaneously in equilibrium and efficient.
     Matching chapter: [Chapter 6 · General equilibrium and welfare economics](/western/一般均衡论和福利经济学)

### Great question 2: is market allocation naturally efficient, and when does it fail

- Sub-question 2.1: once each single market is in equilibrium, can the whole economic system be in equilibrium at the same time.
  Matching chapter: [Chapter 6 · General equilibrium and welfare economics](/western/一般均衡论和福利经济学)
- Sub-question 2.2: what does it mean for an allocation of resources to be efficient.
  Matching chapter: [Chapter 6 · General equilibrium and welfare economics](/western/一般均衡论和福利经济学)
- Sub-question 2.3: if markets are not always efficient, why must government step in.
  Matching chapter: [Chapter 7 · Market failure and microeconomic policy](/western/市场失灵和微观经济政策)
- The progress towards answers:
  1. Marshall solved only: how a single market reaches equilibrium.
     Gist: partial equilibrium explains a single market, but does not by itself imply that all markets reach equilibrium at once.
     Matching chapters: [Chapter 1 · Demand and supply](/western/需求与供给) · [Chapter 4 · Market theory](/western/市场理论)
  2. Walras advanced to: can the entire economy be in equilibrium at once.
     Gist: only when the prices of the several markets coordinate with one another and all markets clear simultaneously is there equilibrium of the whole.
     Matching chapter: [Chapter 6 · General equilibrium and welfare economics](/western/一般均衡论和福利经济学)
  3. Pareto pressed on to ask: is equilibrium the same as efficiency.
     Gist: efficient means that no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off.
     Matching chapter: [Chapter 6 · General equilibrium and welfare economics](/western/一般均衡论和福利经济学)
  4. Pigou and market failure theory revised it: in reality externalities, public goods and imperfect competition destroy the optimum.
     Gist: externalities, public goods and imperfect competition push market prices away from the socially optimal outcome.
     Matching chapters: [Chapter 6 · General equilibrium and welfare economics](/western/一般均衡论和福利经济学) · [Chapter 7 · Market failure and microeconomic policy](/western/市场失灵和微观经济政策)
  5. The conclusion of this round: markets are efficient but conditionally, so government enters the analytical frame.
     Gist: market efficiency requires strict conditions; when they fail, government intervention must be brought into the analysis.
     Matching chapter: [Chapter 7 · Market failure and microeconomic policy](/western/市场失灵和微观经济政策)

### Great question 3: why do mass unemployment, crisis and aggregate fluctuations arise

- Sub-question 3.1: why must the object of study shift from single markets to the economy in aggregate.
  Matching chapter: [Chapter 8 · Macroeconomic activity and macroeconomics](/western/宏观经济活动与宏观经济学)
- Sub-question 3.2: how does aggregate demand determine total income and employment.
  Matching chapter: [Chapter 9 · The theory of simple national income determination](/western/简单国民收入决定理论)
- Sub-question 3.3: how do the product and money markets jointly determine macro equilibrium.
  Matching chapter: [Chapter 10 · General equilibrium of the product and money markets](/western/产品市场和货币市场的一般均衡)
- Sub-question 3.4: how does government regulate the economy with fiscal and monetary policy.
  Matching chapter: [Chapter 11 · Macroeconomic policy](/western/宏观经济政策)
- The progress towards answers:
  1. The classical and neoclassical original answer: prices and wages automatically restore full employment.
     Gist: they believed that so long as wages and prices were flexible enough, the economy would return to full employment on its own.
     Matching chapters: [Chapter 8 · Macroeconomic activity and macroeconomics](/western/宏观经济活动与宏观经济学) · [Chapter 15 · Disagreements in macroeconomics](/western/宏观经济学的意见分歧)
  2. The Great Depression proved: this answer is not enough.
     Gist: reality showed that even with prices cut, unemployment and depression could persist.
     Matching chapters: [Chapter 8 · Macroeconomic activity and macroeconomics](/western/宏观经济活动与宏观经济学) · [Chapter 9 · The theory of simple national income determination](/western/简单国民收入决定理论)
  3. Keynes rewrote the question as: why does deficient effective demand produce long-run unemployment.
     Gist: deficient aggregate demand depresses output and employment, and the market does not restore full employment quickly on its own.
     Matching chapter: [Chapter 9 · The theory of simple national income determination](/western/简单国民收入决定理论)
  4. Hicks advanced to IS-LM, joining the product and money markets in one system.
     Gist: IS-LM puts the product and money markets into one model and explains income and the interest rate together.
     Matching chapter: [Chapter 10 · General equilibrium of the product and money markets](/western/产品市场和货币市场的一般均衡)
  5. A step further: fiscal and monetary policy enter the standard framework of analysis.
     Gist: fiscal and monetary policy can regulate output, employment and the interest rate by working on aggregate demand.
     Matching chapter: [Chapter 11 · Macroeconomic policy](/western/宏观经济政策)
  6. The boundary of this round: aggregate imbalance was explained, but growth, inflation and expectations were not yet fully explained.
     Gist: short-run imbalance was accounted for, but long-run growth and the mechanisms of inflation and expectations still need separate treatment.
     Matching chapters: [Chapter 12 · Aggregate demand and aggregate supply analysis](/western/总需求和总供给分析) · [Chapter 13 · Economic growth](/western/经济增长) · [Chapter 14 · Inflation theory](/western/通货膨胀理论) · [Chapter 15 · Disagreements in macroeconomics](/western/宏观经济学的意见分歧)

## I. Question 1: why can markets form order, and why can prices allocate resources

### 1776, Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations*

- Principal work:
  *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations*.
- Whom it drew on:
  It inherited the physiocrats' and classical liberalism's attention to market exchange and the division of labour, but pressed it forward into a more systematic explanation of market society.
- What it proposed:
  The theory of the division of labour and the idea of the "invisible hand."
- What it was explaining:
  Why a society without unified central command can still form economic order.
- Core view:
  Self-interested individuals, a deepening division of labour and market exchange can coordinate resources spontaneously.
- The advance over predecessors:
  It stopped discussing commerce and wealth in fragments and made market order itself an object that can be explained.
- What it left unsolved:
  It lacked rigorous instruments for price formation, consumer choice and the firm's optimisation.
- Matching chapters:
  [Demand and supply](/western/需求与供给) · [Market theory](/western/市场理论)

### 1817, David Ricardo, *On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation*

- Principal work:
  *On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation*.
- Whom it drew on:
  It stood explicitly on the classical political economy tradition of Smith and carried it forward.
- What it proposed:
  A more systematic theory of distribution and the tradition of comparative-static analysis.
- What it was explaining:
  How the shares of income — wages, profits, rent — change, and how resource allocation ties into the distribution of income.
- Core view:
  Prices are bound up with the structure of distribution; analysing the market cannot look only at exchange but must look also at factor rewards and distribution among classes.
- The advance over Smith:
  It pushed "market order" further to "distributional structure," so that economics explains not only exchange but how income is distributed.
- What it left unsolved:
  It still lacked the later marginalists' fine theory of consumer and firm decision-making.
- Matching chapter:
  [The market for factors of production](/western/生产要素市场)

### The 1870s, Jevons, Menger and Walras, the marginal revolution

- Principal works:
  Jevons's *The Theory of Political Economy*, Menger's *Principles of Economics*, Walras's *Elements of Pure Economics*.
- Whom they drew on:
  On one side they inherited the classical school's concern with value and distribution; on the other they were plainly dissatisfied with old explanations such as the labour theory of value.
- What they proposed:
  Marginal utility analysis and the method of marginal decision-making.
- What they were explaining:
  Why the value of commodities appears as price, and why consumers are willing to buy a given commodity.
- Core view:
  Value cannot be understood from labour or cost alone; it must also be understood from subjective utility and marginal change.
- The advance over the classical school:
  The centre of explanation moved from "value decided on the production side" to "demand decided by the consumer's marginal appraisal," laying the ground for modern demand theory.
- Whom they directly revised:
  Mainly the classical school, and especially the labour theory of value's over-reliance on production-side explanation.
- What they left unsolved:
  The consumer side grew stronger, but the firm, cost, market structure and general equilibrium were not yet fully integrated.
- Matching chapter:
  [Utility theory](/western/效用论)

### 1890, Marshall, *Principles of Economics*

- Principal work:
  *Principles of Economics*.
- Whom it drew on:
  It combined the classical school's cost analysis with the marginal revolution's utility analysis.
- What it proposed:
  The systematic framework of supply and demand determining price, partial equilibrium and elasticity analysis.
- What it was explaining:
  How price and output take shape in a single market, and how consumers and firms interact within it.
- Core view:
  Price is the joint result of demand and supply, and market equilibrium can be analysed within a partial framework.
- The advance over the marginal revolution:
  It no longer spoke of subjective utility alone, but unified demand, supply, cost and elasticity into a micro framework that can be taught and derived.
- Whom it directly revised:
  In effect it reconciled the two halves — classical school and marginal revolution — that each told half the story in isolation.
- What it left unsolved:
  It excelled at single markets, but could not fully say how all markets reach equilibrium together, or whether that equilibrium is efficient.
- Matching chapters:
  [Demand and supply](/western/需求与供给) · [Production and cost](/western/生产和成本论) · [Market theory](/western/市场理论)

### The logic of this stage

- Smith answered first "why the market has order."
- Ricardo moved attention on to "distributional structure."
- The marginal revolution revised the classical understanding of the source of value.
- Marshall assembled the scattered ideas into the basic framework of modern microeconomics.
- But at this point only "the single market" could be explained clearly; "whether the whole economic system is efficient" remained open.

## II. Question 2: is market allocation naturally efficient, and when does it fail

### From 1874, Walras, general equilibrium theory

- Principal work:
  *Elements of Pure Economics*.
- Whom it drew on:
  It built directly on the questions posed by the marginal revolution and Marshallian price theory, but extended the object of analysis from the single market to the whole system.
- What it proposed:
  General equilibrium theory.
- What it was explaining:
  If the several markets are linked to one another, can the entire economy reach equilibrium simultaneously at one set of prices.
- Core view:
  The equilibrium of one market depends on the others; genuine equilibrium must be the simultaneous equilibrium of all markets.
- The advance over Marshall:
  Marshall solved partial equilibrium; Walras raised the question to equilibrium of the whole.
- What it left unsolved:
  Simultaneous equilibrium does not mean efficiency, still less the best social welfare.
- Matching chapter:
  [General equilibrium and welfare economics](/western/一般均衡论和福利经济学)

### Late nineteenth to early twentieth century, Pareto, the foundations of welfare economics

- Principal work:
  *Lectures on Political Economy* and the later writings on welfare analysis.
- Whom it drew on:
  It inherited the general equilibrium tradition but was not content to ask only "can it be in equilibrium," pressing on to "is the equilibrium any good."
- What it proposed:
  The Pareto optimality criterion.
- What it was explaining:
  What kind of allocation of resources deserves to be called efficient.
- Core view:
  If no one can be made better off without making someone worse off, the allocation of resources is efficient.
- The advance over Walras:
  Walras answered "does equilibrium exist"; Pareto answered "is the equilibrium efficient."
- What it left unsolved:
  The Pareto criterion discusses efficiency but not fairness; and it depends on strict conditions.
- Matching chapter:
  [General equilibrium and welfare economics](/western/一般均衡论和福利经济学)

### 1920, Pigou, *The Economics of Welfare*

- Principal work:
  *The Economics of Welfare*.
- Whom it drew on:
  It stood on Pareto's welfare analysis, but began to face the real-world question "why are market outcomes not optimal."
- What it proposed:
  The classic statement of the theory of externalities.
- What it was explaining:
  Why the outcome of private decisions may depart from the social optimum.
- Core view:
  Private cost and social cost may diverge, and market prices need not contain all social effects.
- The advance over Pareto:
  Pareto gave the efficiency standard; Pigou began to explain why reality falls short of it.
- Whom it directly revised:
  It revised the over-strong optimism that "meetable equilibrium conditions alone guarantee good outcomes."
- What it left unsolved:
  Externalities are only one class of market failure; public goods, monopoly and information problems still awaited extension.
- Matching chapter:
  [Market failure and microeconomic policy](/western/市场失灵和微观经济政策)

### From the mid-twentieth century on, market failure theory and micro policy analysis

- Whom it drew on:
  It continued the welfare economics tradition from Pigou and absorbed the criticisms of public choice and information economics.
- What it proposed:
  Systematic analyses of public goods, externalities, imperfect competition and asymmetric information.
- What it was explaining:
  Under what conditions markets cannot allocate resources efficiently, and why government enters the frame of economic analysis.
- Core view:
  Market efficiency is conditional, and government intervention is not costless either.
- The advance over Pigou:
  From "one class of failure" it extended to "many classes of failure," and it institutionalised the analysis of policy instruments.
- What it left unsolved:
  Government itself can fail, so the question is no longer "market or government" but "which failure costs less."
- Matching chapter:
  [Market failure and microeconomic policy](/western/市场失灵和微观经济政策)

### The logic of this stage

- Marshall first explained how a single market reaches equilibrium.
- Walras advanced to equilibrium of the whole.
- Pareto pressed further with the efficiency criterion.
- Pigou and the later market failure theory began to explain why reality falls short of the optimum.
- Thus economics moved from "explaining how the market runs" to "judging the bounds of the market."

## III. Question 3: why do mass unemployment, crisis and aggregate fluctuations arise

### 1936, Keynes, *The General Theory*

- Principal work:
  *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*.
- Whom it drew on:
  It stood on the classical and neoclassical tradition, but challenged head-on the old belief that "prices and wages automatically restore full employment."
- What it proposed:
  The theory of effective demand.
- What it was explaining:
  Why the economy stays for long periods in unemployment and depression instead of restoring equilibrium on its own.
- Core view:
  Deficient aggregate demand leads to prolonged low output and high unemployment; spontaneous market adjustment is not reliable.
- The advance over the classical and neoclassical theories:
  It moved the centre of analysis from the prices of individual markets to aggregate demand, total output and total employment, founding macroeconomics.
- Whom it directly criticised:
  The classical theory of employment and the belief that "wages and prices flexible enough will automatically restore full employment."
- What it left unsolved:
  The direction of the theory was clear, but the modelling was thin; how the product and money markets interact still awaited formalisation.
- Matching chapters:
  [Macroeconomic activity and macroeconomics](/western/宏观经济活动与宏观经济学) · [The theory of simple national income determination](/western/简单国民收入决定理论)

### 1937, Hicks, the IS-LM model

- Principal work:
  *Mr Keynes and the "Classics": A Suggested Interpretation*.
- Whom it drew on:
  It was, directly, a formalising arrangement built on Keynes's theory.
- What it proposed:
  The IS-LM model.
- What it was explaining:
  How the interest rate, investment, the money supply and total income jointly determine macro equilibrium.
- Core view:
  Product market equilibrium and money market equilibrium jointly determine income and the interest rate.
- The advance over Keynes:
  It carried Keynes's ideas from polemical statement to a model that can be derived, drawn and used for policy comparison.
- What it left unsolved:
  It explained mainly the short-run closed economy, and said too little about the supply side and long-run growth.
- Matching chapter:
  [General equilibrium of the product and money markets](/western/产品市场和货币市场的一般均衡)

### The 1950s, Hansen and others, the neoclassical synthesis

- Bridging figures:
  Hansen, Samuelson and others pressed this line to standard form in the textbook system.
- Whom it drew on:
  It tried to reconcile Keynes with traditional neoclassical theory.
- What it proposed:
  The neoclassical synthesis.
- What it was explaining:
  How to place short-run demand management and long-run equilibrium analysis in one system.
- Core view:
  In the short run Keynes is valid; in the long run one returns to the traditional supply and price mechanisms.
- The advance over Keynes:
  It carried Keynesianism into the mainstream textbook system and made it the postwar policy consensus.
- What it left unsolved:
  Once stagflation appeared, the stability of this synthesis would be challenged.
- Matching chapter:
  [Macroeconomic policy](/western/宏观经济政策)

### The logic of this stage

- Classical theory believed the market would automatically restore full employment.
- Keynes denied this outright and explained long-run unemployment.
- Hicks turned Keynes into a model.
- The neoclassical synthesis stitched Keynes to traditional theory for a time.
- But this still left unanswered the harder questions of long-run growth, sustained inflation and changing expectations.

## IV. Question 4: how can growth, inflation, expectations and the bounds of policy be understood together

### 1956, Solow, growth theory

- Principal work:
  *A Contribution to the Theory of Economic Growth*.
- Whom it drew on:
  It inherited neoclassical production theory, but moved the question from short-run fluctuation to long-run growth.
- What it proposed:
  The neoclassical growth model.
- What it was explaining:
  What mainly determines the growth of output in the long run.
- Core view:
  Long-run growth cannot be explained by demand stimulus alone; one must look to capital accumulation, population growth and technological progress.
- The advance over the Keynesian framework:
  Keynes excelled at short-run fluctuation; Solow added long-run growth.
- What it left unsolved:
  It did not directly address inflation, unemployment or policy expectations.
- Matching chapter:
  [Economic growth](/western/经济增长)

### 1958, Phillips, the Phillips curve

- Principal work:
  *The Relation between Unemployment and the Rate of Change of Money Wage Rates in the United Kingdom, 1861-1957*.
- Whom it drew on:
  It came out of postwar empirical research and the Keynesian policy background.
- What it proposed:
  An empirical relation between unemployment and wage change, later extended to unemployment and inflation.
- What it was explaining:
  Why in the short run inflation and unemployment seem to trade off against each other.
- Core view:
  Policymakers appear able to make a trade between inflation and unemployment.
- The advance over the previous stage:
  It carried macro policy from "can one intervene" to "what does intervention cost."
- What it left unsolved:
  Whether this relation was stable in the long run was not truly explained at the time.
- Matching chapter:
  [Inflation theory](/western/通货膨胀理论)

### The 1960s, Friedman and Phelps, the natural rate hypothesis

- Principal works:
  Friedman's *The Role of Monetary Policy*, and Phelps's papers on the Phillips curve and the theory of optimal unemployment.
- Whom they drew on:
  They set out to revise directly the stable Phillips curve and Keynesian policy optimism.
- What they proposed:
  The natural rate of unemployment and the expectations-augmented Phillips curve.
- What they were explaining:
  Why demand expansion cannot hold unemployment below the natural rate in the long run.
- Core view:
  A short-run trade-off may exist, but sustained stimulus in the long run brings only higher inflation.
- The advance over the Phillips curve:
  They carried a static empirical relation into dynamic expectations analysis.
- What they left unsolved:
  The logic of rational expectations and policy rules had not yet been driven to its conclusion.
- Matching chapters:
  [Inflation theory](/western/通货膨胀理论) · [Disagreements in macroeconomics](/western/宏观经济学的意见分歧)

### The 1950s, Friedman's monetarism

- Principal work:
  Friedman's *A Monetary History of the United States* and the related monetarist papers.
- Whom it drew on:
  It stood on the quantity theory of money and the natural rate hypothesis, and also criticised Keynesian discretion.
- What it proposed:
  Monetarism, the single monetary rule.
- What it was explaining:
  Why inflation persists, and why discretionary policy can backfire.
- Core view:
  Inflation is at bottom a monetary phenomenon; a monetary policy rule is more reliable than ad hoc intervention.
- The advance over Keynesianism:
  It pulled the focus of macro analysis back from fiscal demand management to the money supply and policy rules.
- What it left unsolved:
  How agents' expectations form, and why policy fails systematically, remained imperfectly explained.
- Matching chapter:
  [Disagreements in macroeconomics](/western/宏观经济学的意见分歧)

### From the 1970s on, Lucas, Sargent and Wallace, new classical macroeconomics

- Principal work:
  Lucas's *Econometric Policy Evaluation: A Critique* and the papers on rational expectations.
- Whom they drew on:
  They inherited monetarism and the natural rate hypothesis but brought rational expectations in on top.
- What they proposed:
  Rational expectations, the policy-ineffectiveness proposition, the Lucas critique.
- What they were explaining:
  Why the old policy models fail in a new environment, and why government cannot systematically manipulate output and employment over the long run.
- Core view:
  Agents adjust their expectations to policy, so empirical models with fixed parameters cannot be used directly to evaluate policy.
- The advance over monetarism:
  Monetarism stressed policy rules; the new classicals went further and explained "why rules matter," attacking Keynesianism from the micro logic of expectations.
- What they left unsolved:
  They depend on the assumption that prices and wages are flexible enough, and so struggle to explain real-world stickiness and persistent unemployment.
- Matching chapter:
  [Disagreements in macroeconomics](/western/宏观经济学的意见分歧)

### From the 1980s on, New Keynesianism

- Bridging figures:
  Mankiw, Romer, Stiglitz, Akerlof and others supplied, from different angles, the foundations of price stickiness, wage stickiness and imperfect competition.
- Whom it drew on:
  It accepted the methodological demands of the rational expectations revolution, but turned to criticise new classical macro for assuming too strongly that "markets always clear quickly."
- What it proposed:
  Explanations such as sticky prices, sticky wages, imperfect competition, menu costs and efficiency wages.
- What it was explaining:
  Why prices in reality do not adjust in an instant, and why short-run unemployment and demand fluctuations remain real.
- Core view:
  Agents can be rational, yet markets still adjust slowly because of frictions and stickiness.
- The advance over new classical macro:
  It did not simply return to old Keynesianism; after accepting the demands of expectations and microfoundations, it rebuilt a realistic basis for the effectiveness of policy.
- What it left unsolved:
  The bounds of policy, financial shocks and stabilisation in open economies continue to be argued over.
- Matching chapter:
  [Disagreements in macroeconomics](/western/宏观经济学的意见分歧)

### The logic of this stage

- Solow added long-run growth.
- Phillips turned the inflation-unemployment relation into a policy question.
- Friedman and Phelps used the natural rate hypothesis to revise Keynesian optimism.
- Monetarism moved the focus to monetary rules.
- Lucas and others used rational expectations to attack the old policy models.
- New Keynesianism, having accepted the expectations revolution, brought real-world stickiness and the working of policy back in.

## V. Finally, the whole book compressed into one sentence

- The order of advance in western economics is not "chapters side by side," but:
  first explain how the market runs, then judge whether the market is efficient, then explain why aggregates fall out of balance, and at last argue whether government can intervene and how it should.
