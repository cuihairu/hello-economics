# Chapter 5 · Factor markets

> **The core question of this chapter**: how are the prices of factors of production (wages, interest, rent, profit) determined? How does the firm's demand for factors differ from consumers' demand for products?

## The arc of this chapter

The previous chapters answered "how much does the product sell for?"; this chapter returns to the question classical economists cared about first: **how is what is produced shared between labour, capital and land?** Say distilled it into the "three-factor theory" — labour gets wages, capital gets interest, land gets rent — but this correspondence is merely asserted; it does not say on what grounds each factor "deserves" its share.

After the marginal revolution, Clark supplied the mechanism in *The Distribution of Wealth* (1899): under perfect competition, the return to each factor is exactly its **marginal product** — not because it "should" get that much, but because as long as the gain from employing one more unit of a factor exceeds its price, the firm keeps employing it, until the two are equal. Distribution thereby turns from an ethical proposition into a supply-and-demand proposition: factor prices are determined by supply and demand in factor markets, and the firm's demand for factors is itself **derived** from consumers' demand for products (derived demand).

Two extensions complete the framework of this chapter. First, Wicksteed (1894) used Euler's theorem to show that, with constant returns to scale, paying each factor its marginal product exactly exhausts the total product — the theory of distribution is arithmetically self-consistent. Second, chapter 4's market structures are carried over into factor markets: a seller monopoly in the product market pushes marginal revenue down ($MRP<VMP$), while a buyer monopoly in the factor market pushes marginal factor cost above the factor price ($MFC>$ factor price) — so a worker may face markets that are imperfect on both sides. There is one famous exception on the labour-supply side: in 1871 Jevons had already derived the **backward-bending labour supply curve** from the utility trade-off between income and leisure.

## Core concepts

### Derived demand

Derived demand, also called "induced demand", refers to the firm's demand for a factor of production arising out of consumers' demand for a product. Derived demand originates in the firm; it is the firm's demand for factors of production, and differs from consumers' demand for goods.

In the product market, demand comes from consumers, who buy products for their own consumption and obtain satisfaction from it; a single act of purchasing ends there. By contrast, in the factor market demand comes from firms, which buy factors of production for production and earn profit from it. A firm's purchase of a factor of production is not the end of an economic act. Whether a firm can make a profit does not depend on itself alone, but on consumers' demand for the product it produces, and on whether consumers are willing to pay a sufficient price for its product. If there is no consumer demand for the product, then the firm cannot obtain revenue from producing and selling it, and will not buy means of production or produce the product at all. In other words, consumers' direct demand for a product induces and derives the firm's demand for factors of production. The shape of the demand curve for a given factor of production is derived from the demand curve for the final product that uses it — no one demands bread, and there will be no bakery's demand for ovens.

### Value of marginal product

The value of marginal product is the value of the additional output produced by employing one more unit of a factor of production; it equals the product of marginal product and the product price, i.e. $VMP=P\cdot{MP_L}$ . Under perfect competition, the value of the factor's marginal product reflects the marginal revenue of the factor, and is the firm's demand curve for that factor.

Because marginal product is subject to the law of diminishing returns, the demand curve for a factor facing a perfectly competitive firm slopes downward to the right.

### Marginal revenue product

The marginal revenue product is the revenue brought about by employing one more unit of a given factor of production when the inputs of the other factors are held fixed. It equals the product of marginal product ( $MP$ ) and marginal revenue ( $MR$ ), i.e.:

$$MRP=MR \cdot MP$$

In a seller-monopolized product market, the price the firm can charge for a given output depends on the firm's output and typically moves in the opposite direction, so the revenue that a factor's marginal product adds for the firm is not the original price times marginal product, but marginal product times marginal revenue. The division of labour between $VMP$ and $MRP$ is thereby clear: they are equal when the product market is perfectly competitive; when the product market is a seller monopoly, $MR<P$, and the factor's revenue-creating power is pushed below its value-creating power — the gap in between is the monopoly markup.

### Average factor cost

Average factor cost is the cost a firm pays on average per unit of a factor of production.

Under perfect competition the price of the factor of production is given, so the firm's average factor cost equals the market price of that factor. Under a buyer monopoly in the factor market, the average factor cost curve is the supply curve for the factor that the firm faces. The marginal cost of employing the factor exceeds the factor price. This is because the firm then faces a supply curve for the factor that slopes upward to the right. The quantity of the factor the firm employs therefore affects the price of the factor. In this case the average factor cost curve rises as the quantity of the factor employed rises — a monopsonist who wants to hire more must raise the wage for everyone, including those already on the payroll.

### Marginal factor cost

Marginal factor cost is the increase in cost a firm incurs by employing one more unit of a factor of production. It is denoted $MFC$ .

The firm's profit-maximizing rule for employing a factor is:

$$MRP=MFC$$

Under perfect competition the price of the factor of production is unchanged, so the cost of employing one more unit of the factor equals the price of that factor. In a monopsony of the factor, the firm's marginal factor cost of employing the factor exceeds the price of the factor.

### The backward-bending labour supply curve

Following the worker's optimization, for a given wage rate the worker determines the optimal supply of labour at the utility-maximizing point, thereby yielding the supply curve for labour. When the wage level is low, the substitution effect of a rise in the wage rate on labour outweighs the income effect, so people are willing to supply more labour and less leisure; but once the wage level has risen far enough, the substitution effect falls below the income effect, so people increase leisure time and cut back labour time. The supply curve for labour therefore bends back (to the left).

## Problems and analysis

### How is the demand curve for a factor obtained by a perfectly competitive firm? Why does it slope downward to the right?

**[Notation]**
| Symbol | English full name | Meaning |
|------|----------|----------|
| $VMP$ | Value of Marginal Product | Value of marginal product |
| $P$ | Price | Product price |
| $MP$ / $MP_L$ | Marginal Product | Marginal product / marginal product of labour |
| $r$ | Rental Rate | Price of the factor |

- (1) As in the product market, a perfectly competitive factor market has many firms and many suppliers of the factor, and all of them choose their optimal employment of and supply of the factor at the given factor price. In the product market the firm is also a perfect competitor, i.e. the market price of the product is the basis for the firm's decisions. For the firm, the principle for choosing how much of a factor to employ is profit maximization, i.e. the factor's "marginal revenue" and "marginal cost" must be equal.

- (2) Under perfect competition, the firm's "marginal revenue" from employing a factor is the value of the factor's marginal product, i.e. the product of marginal product and price. This is because the additional output from one more unit of the factor equals the factor's marginal product, and this output is sold at an unchanged product price, so the additional revenue to the firm from one more unit of the factor equals the product price times marginal product, i.e. $VMP= P\times MP$ .

	Looking at the factor's "marginal cost", the price the firm faces for the factor is determined by market supply and demand and does not change with the quantity of the factor the firm employs, so the increase in cost from one more unit of the factor equals the price of that factor. The firm's profit-maximizing rule for employing a factor therefore takes the form:

	$$VMP=P \times MP =r$$

	where $r$ is the price of the factor.

- (3) For the factor price $r$ determined by the market, the firm chooses the profit-maximizing quantity of the factor employed, so the value of the factor's marginal product constitutes the firm's demand curve for that factor. Since the factor's marginal product $MP$ is subject to the law of diminishing returns while the product price under perfect competition remains unchanged, the value of the factor's marginal product $VMP$ also declines as the quantity of the factor employed rises; that is, the demand curve for the factor facing a perfectly competitive firm slopes downward to the right, as shown in Figure 5-1.

### Why does the labour supply curve bend backward? Explain using the income and substitution effects.

- (1) A backward-bending labour supply curve means that, as the price of labour — the wage rate — rises, the quantity of labour supplied initially increases gradually, but once the price of labour has risen far enough, the quantity of labour supplied actually falls. If each worker's supply depends only on the wage, the backward-bending labour supply curve can be shown in Figure 5-2.

![](../img/exercises/micro/5-2.png)

- (2) The backward bend of the labour supply curve can be analysed from the following three angles:

- ① The backward bend of the supply curve for labour is the combined result of the substitution and income effects of the wage rate of labour. The quantity of labour a worker is willing to supply at a given wage rate depends on how the worker weighs the utility brought by wage income against that brought by leisure. The consumer's total utility is provided by income and by leisure. Income brings satisfaction to the consumer through the purchase of goods: the more income, the higher the level of consumption, the greater the utility. Likewise, leisure is a special kind of consumption; the more leisure time, the higher the level of utility. However, the time available to a worker is fixed, so a worker's labour-supply behaviour can be stated as: given a fixed time constraint, arrange time between labour and leisure so as to realize maximum utility.

- ② In general, the higher the wage rate, the greater the compensation for forgoing leisure, and the more labour the worker is willing to supply in place of leisure. Put differently, a higher wage rate raises the opportunity cost of leisure accordingly, so the worker's leisure time grows shorter. The substitution effect of a rising wage rate therefore increases the quantity of labour. At the same time, a higher wage rate raises the worker's income level, and then the worker needs more leisure time. That is, once the wage rate has risen, the worker can raise their standard of living without supplying more labour. The income effect of a higher wage rate therefore reduces the quantity of labour.

- ③ The substitution and income effects are the two sides of the impact of a rising wage rate. If the substitution effect outweighs the income effect, the quantity of leisure demanded falls as its price rises; conversely, if the income effect outweighs the substitution effect, the quantity of leisure demanded rises as its price rises. This is what the backward bend of the labour supply curve means. If the original wage, i.e. the price of leisure, is low, the quantity of labour supplied is small at that point, and the income effect of a wage rise cannot offset the substitution effect; but if the wage rate is already at a high level, and the quantity of labour supplied is correspondingly large, the whole increment in labour income from a wage rise is large, and the income effect then outweighs the substitution effect. In other words, once a rise in wages has made people reasonably comfortable, people value leisure more highly. Therefore, once the wage has reached a certain height and continues to rise, people's labour supply does not increase but rather falls — the labour supply curve passes its turning point and begins to bend backward.

### What does Euler's theorem imply for the theory of factor income distribution?

**[Notation]**
| Symbol | English full name | Meaning |
|------|----------|----------|
| $Q$ | Output / Total Product | Total output |
| $L$ | Labor | Factor of labour |
| $K$ | Capital | Factor of capital |
| $MP_L$ / $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial L}$ | Marginal Product of Labor | Marginal product of labour |
| $MP_K$ / $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial K}$ | Marginal Product of Capital | Marginal product of capital |

**（1）Euler's theorem and its distributional meaning**

Euler's theorem is also called the **theorem of exact factor payments**, and is a mathematical theorem concerning homogeneous functions.

**Core meaning**: under perfect competition and constant returns to scale, the whole product is exactly enough to distribute among the factors, no more and no less.

Suppose there are two factors of production, labour $L$ and capital $K$, and the production function is $Q=Q(L,K)$. If returns to scale are constant, then:

$$Q=L\cdot\frac{\partial Q}{\partial L}+K\cdot\frac{\partial Q}{\partial K}$$

This is Euler's theorem, where:
- $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial L} = MP_L$ (the marginal product of labour)
- $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial K} = MP_K$ (the marginal product of capital)

**Economic interpretation**: the whole product $Q$ is exactly equal to the income accruing to labour plus the income accruing to capital.

**（2）The arithmetic of income distribution**

| Factor | Quantity employed | Marginal product | Income received |
|------|--------|----------|------------|
| Labour | $L$ | $MP_L$ | $L \times MP_L$ |
| Capital | $K$ | $MP_K$ | $K \times MP_K$ |
| **Total** | — | — | $= Q$ (total output) |

Under perfect competition:
- factor price = value of marginal product ($w = P \times MP_L$, $r = P \times MP_K$)
- each factor receives income according to its marginal contribution
- the total is exactly distributed, with no remainder

Example: suppose a factory employs 100 workers and 10 machines and produces 1000 units. The marginal product of a worker is 5 units per person, so labour income is $=100\times5=500$ units; the marginal product of a machine is 50 units per machine, so capital income is $=10\times50=500$ units; the total is $500+500=1000$ units, exactly distributed.

**（3）Why it matters**

Wicksteed introduced this theorem into the theory of distribution in 1894: so long as returns to scale are constant, "paying each factor its marginal product" exhausts output in aggregate — neither a shortfall nor a surplus — and the marginal-productivity theory of distribution is arithmetically self-consistent. But the boundary lies here: with **increasing** returns to scale, distribution by marginal product falls short (the gap must be made up by profit or a surplus); with **decreasing** returns, it overshoots. The theorem therefore marks "constant returns to scale" as the range of application of this theory of distribution — not that the distribution is "fair": the marginal product of a factor itself depends on how endowments of factors are distributed, and who owns what remains a question outside the theory.

### How is the price of labour determined when the product market is a seller monopoly and the factor market a buyer monopoly?

- (1) The price of a factor of production is determined by the supply of and demand for that factor. The labour market consists of the supply of and the demand for labour. The supply of labour comes from workers, while the demand for labour comes from producers. The price of labour, this factor, is a market equilibrium price determined by supply and demand in the labour market, but it is constrained by market conditions.

- (2) In factor markets, the general principle by which a firm decides how much of a factor such as labour to employ is that the marginal revenue product equals the marginal factor cost.

	To maximize profit, the cost the firm incurs for each unit of labour employed must equal the revenue that unit of labour brings in: if the former exceeds the latter, the firm reduces its use of that factor; if the former is less than the latter, the firm increases its use. By definition, the cost the firm incurs for the last unit of labour employed is the marginal factor cost of that unit, and the revenue obtained by selling the product produced by using that unit of labour is the marginal revenue product of labour, i.e. marginal product times marginal revenue.

	Under a buyer monopoly in the factor market, the firm's rule for hiring labour is still that the marginal revenue product of labour equals the marginal factor cost. A buyer monopoly in the labour market means that only one firm in the market buys labour as a factor of production. The supply curve for labour that this firm faces is therefore the market supply curve, sloping upward to the right, and the firm's marginal factor cost of employing labour is no longer a constant: it rises faster than the average factor cost of labour, i.e. the marginal factor cost of labour lies above the supply curve for labour.

- (3) Under a seller monopoly in the product market, the market demand curve the firm faces when selling the product it produces with labour slopes downward. This makes the marginal revenue of the product less than the market price, and thereby makes the marginal revenue product of labour lower than the value of its marginal product.

- (4) The firm decides the quantity of labour employed on the basis of **the marginal revenue product equal to the marginal factor cost** ($MRP=MFC$), and then fixes the price of labour at the point on the supply curve for labour corresponding to that quantity — a monopsonist pays the "lowest price it can get away with" on the supply curve, rather than the marginal value the factor creates. As shown in Figure 5-4, under perfect competition the equilibrium price of labour is $r_e$; with a seller monopoly in the product market and a buyer monopoly in the factor market, the price of labour is $r_3$.

![](../img/exercises/micro/5-4.png)

This analysis shows that, compared with a perfectly competitive firm, the monopsonist's marginal factor cost of employing a factor lies above the supply curve for the factor while the value of the factor's marginal product is the same, so it employs less of the factor and pays a lower price.

**[Product seller monopoly + factor buyer monopoly: summary]**

**Basic concepts:**

| Concept | Meaning | Mathematical form |
|------|------|----------|
| Marginal revenue product (MRP) | The increase in revenue from one more unit of the factor | $MRP = MP_L \times MR$ |
| Marginal factor cost (MFC) | The increase in cost from one more unit of the factor | $MFC = \frac{\Delta TC}{\Delta L}$ |
| Value of marginal product (VMP) | The market value of the marginal product | $VMP = MP_L \times P$ |

**The effects of double monopoly:**

| Market | Firm's position | Curve faced | Relation |
|------|----------|------------|------|
| Product market | Seller monopoly | Downward-sloping demand curve | $MR < P$ → $MRP < VMP$ |
| Factor market | Buyer monopoly | Upward-sloping supply curve | $MFC > \text{factor price}$ |

**The decision principle:**

$$MRP = MFC$$

i.e. the marginal revenue product = the marginal factor cost

**Comparing market structures:**

| Market type | Product market | Factor market | Equilibrium condition | Factor price | Quantity of factor employed |
|----------|----------|----------|----------|----------|------------|
| Perfect competition | Perfect competition | Perfect competition | $VMP = \text{factor price}$ | Highest | Largest |
| Product seller monopoly | Seller monopoly | Perfect competition | $MRP = \text{factor price}$ | Intermediate | Intermediate |
| Factor buyer monopoly | Perfect competition | Buyer monopoly | $VMP = MFC$ | Intermediate | Intermediate |
| Double monopoly | Seller monopoly | Buyer monopoly | $MRP = MFC$ | Lowest | Smallest |

**A graphical sketch of double monopoly:**

```
工资(W)
  ↑
  |           S (供给曲线)
  |          /
  |         / MFC (边际要素成本) > S
  |        /
  |       /   ← MFC在S上方
  |      /
  |     ·  ← 均衡点：MRP = MFC
  |    /|
  |   / |
  |  /  |

  |/    | ← 实际工资在供给曲线上
  +----------------→ 劳动量(L)

```

**Conclusion**: double monopoly produces the lowest factor price and the smallest employment, and is the market structure least favourable to workers.

## Derivation and worked examples

### What is the firm's profit-maximizing principle in factor markets? Prove your conclusion.

**[Notation]**
| Symbol | English full name | Meaning |
|------|----------|----------|
| $\pi$ | Profit | Profit |
| $TR$ | Total Revenue | Total revenue |
| $TC$ | Total Cost | Total cost |
| $MRP$ | Marginal Revenue Product | Marginal revenue product |
| $VMP$ | Value of Marginal Product | Value of marginal product |
| $MFC$ | Marginal Factor Cost | Marginal factor cost |
| $MR$ | Marginal Revenue | Marginal revenue |
| $P$ | Price | Product price |
| $MPP$ / $MP$ | Marginal Physical Product | Marginal physical product / marginal product |
| $L$ | Labor | Quantity of labour employed |
| $w$ | Wage | Wage rate (factor price) |

**[Core concepts: a distinction]**

Before beginning the proof, two easily confused concepts must be distinguished:

| Concept | English | Definition | Formula | Applies when |
|------|------|------|------|----------|
| Value of marginal product | VMP | Market value of the marginal product | $VMP = P \times MP_L$ | The product market is perfectly competitive |
| Marginal revenue product | MRP | The increment of revenue from one more unit of the factor | $MRP = MR \times MP_L$ | Any product market |

**The key relations**:

- Perfectly competitive product market: $P = MR$, hence $VMP = MRP$
- Seller-monopolized product market: $MR < P$, hence $MRP < VMP$

**[Conclusion]**

The general profit-maximizing principle in factor markets is:

$$MRP = MFC$$

i.e. the marginal revenue product = the marginal factor cost.

**[Proof]**

Step 1: set up the profit function. Assume that all factors other than labour $L$ are unchanged. The firm's profit is:

$$\pi = TR - TC$$

Step 2: profit maximization requires that the first derivative with respect to $L$ be zero:

$$\frac{d\pi}{dL} = \frac{dTR}{dL} - \frac{dTC}{dL} = 0$$

i.e.:

$$\frac{dTR}{dL} = \frac{dTC}{dL}$$

Step 3: analyse the left-hand side. The increase in total revenue brought by one more unit of labour can be split into "extra output × marginal revenue per unit of output":

$$\frac{dTR}{dL} = \frac{dTR}{dQ} \times \frac{dQ}{dL} = MR \times MP_L = MRP$$

This is the **marginal revenue product (MRP)**.

Step 4: analyse the right-hand side. The increase in total cost brought by one more unit of labour:

$$\frac{dTC}{dL} = MFC$$

This is the **marginal factor cost (MFC)**.

Step 5: conclude:

$$MRP = MFC$$

**[Economic meaning]**

| Case | Condition | Firm's decision |
|------|------|----------|
| $MRP > MFC$ | Revenue from the extra factor > cost | Increase the factor input |
| $MRP < MFC$ | Revenue from the extra factor < cost | Reduce the factor input |
| $MRP = MFC$ | Revenue from the extra factor = cost | Optimum reached, no further adjustment |

**[Specific forms under different market structures]**

| Product market | Factor market | Equilibrium condition | Note |
|----------|----------|----------|------|
| Perfect competition | Perfect competition | $VMP = w$ | $MRP = VMP$, $MFC = w$ |
| Seller monopoly | Perfect competition | $MRP = w$ | $MRP < VMP$, $MFC = w$ |
| Perfect competition | Buyer monopoly | $VMP = MFC$ | $MRP = VMP$, $MFC > w$ |
| Seller monopoly | Buyer monopoly | $MRP = MFC$ | $MRP < VMP$, $MFC > w$ |

where $w$ is the wage rate (the price of labour).

The factor-market rule $MRP = MFC$ is perfectly symmetric to the product-market rule $MR = MC$: the former asks "is one more unit of the factor worth employing?", the latter asks "is one more unit of the product worth producing?" — the same marginal analysis, with only the decision variable changed.

## Limits and what follows

- **Is marginal productivity a "law of nature"?** Clark offered it as a proof that distribution is just: each factor receives the share it creates. The criticism followed at once — the marginal product itself depends on how endowments of factors are distributed (who owns capital, who can obtain education); the theory explains "how to divide", but does not answer "on what grounds to divide this way". The aggregation problem for capital at the end of the previous chapter (the Cambridge capital debate) applies here as well: the marginal product of capital can only be computed once capital has first been summed into a single number.
- **A testable implication of monopsony: a minimum wage need not reduce employment.** The competitive model predicts that a minimum wage lowers employment; the monopsony model predicts instead that setting the wage between the monopolistic low wage and the competitive wage raises employment. Card and Krueger (1994) studied fast-food restaurants in New Jersey and Pennsylvania and found that employment did not fall after the minimum wage was raised, shaking the field — the finding cannot establish that monopsony is widespread, but it shows that the default assumption "labour markets are perfectly competitive" is not empirically safe. Monopsony later became one of the main frameworks, alongside monopoly competition, for explaining "wages below productivity" (search frictions and efficiency wages are the other two).
- **Has evidence been found for the backward-bending supply curve?** In short-run and individual labour-supply data, the substitution effect usually dominates, and the supply curve slopes upward to the right with very little elasticity; but stretched out over a century, the picture reverses: real wages in industrialized countries have risen several-fold, while the work week fell from more than 60 hours in the 19th century to around 40 — the income effect does take over the direction in the long run, once income is high enough. The backward-bending curve in textbooks is an image of "long-run historical trend" and "short-run individual response" superimposed.
