# Chapter 8. Macroeconomic activity and macroeconomics

> **The core question of this chapter**: how do you measure an economy as a whole (GDP, GNP)? And how are macroeconomics' main problems (unemployment, inflation, economic fluctuations) defined and measured?

## The shape of this chapter

To talk about "the whole economy" you first have to be able to *count* the whole economy. That seemingly dull question stayed open until the 1930s: during the Great Depression the US government wanted to know how badly the economy had turned, and commissioned Simon Kuznets to compile national income data — the report he submitted to Congress in 1934 is GDP's birth certificate. Without that accounting framework Keynes's *General Theory* could only have been a thought experiment; with it, "is aggregate demand deficient or excessive" becomes a measurable, arguable proposition. Kuznets later won the 1971 Nobel Prize, awarded for precisely this work.

This chapter is the macro part's set of measures: it first defines GDP and GNP (how much was produced), nominal versus real (price rise or output rise), and personal disposable income (who ends up holding it), then defines the unemployment rate (how many people have no work). These concepts recur in every later chapter — like the supply-and-demand tables of the micro part, they are the shared language of all macro argument.

## Core concepts

### Gross domestic product

Gross domestic product (GDP) is the total market value of all final goods and services produced in an economic society (a country or region) within a given period, using its factors of production.
GDP is commonly used to gauge a country's level of economic output and its degree of economic development. GDP is usually accounted for by two methods, the expenditure method and the income method. GDP calculated by the expenditure method equals consumption plus investment plus government spending plus net exports; GDP calculated by the income method equals wages plus interest plus rent plus profit, added to indirect taxes, corporate transfer payments and depreciation.

That the two methods must give the same answer is no coincidence: any expenditure is simultaneously somebody's income — the ten yuan paid for a loaf of bread ends up as the baker's wage, rent and profit. That identity is the seed of the aggregate supply-and-demand analysis that follows.

### Gross national product

Gross national product (GNP) is the total market value of the final products produced by the factors of production owned by a country's nationals within a given period — that is, the total market value of final output produced by a country's residents.
Whether labour and the other factors of production sit at home or abroad, the value of the goods and services produced by the country's nationals is counted in GNP. This aggregate indicator does not deduct the depreciation of capital consumed during production, which is why it is called a "gross" total.

The difference between GDP and GNP lies only in "where production takes place" versus "who owns the factors": GDP is counted by territory (a foreign-owned factory in China counts toward China's GDP), GNP is counted by nationality (the profit it remits home counts in GNP's deduction). The gap between the two is net factor income from abroad.

### Personal disposable income

Personal disposable income is the part of a household's total cash income that can be used to arrange the household's daily life — that is, the household's total cash income minus personal income tax, minus bookkeeping subsidies, minus the costs of the household's side-production activities.
Not all personal income is available to the individual, because personal income tax still has to be paid; after-tax personal income is therefore personal disposable income, that is, the income people may freely spend or save.

It is the real independent variable of the consumption function (Chapter 9): households deciding how much to consume look at the money in their hands, not the national income printed in a statistical yearbook.

### The unemployment rate

The unemployment rate is the ratio of unemployed persons to the total labour force (the sum of employed and unemployed persons) within a given period. There are two kinds of unemployment rate, the registered rate and the surveyed rate. The unemployment rate in formula form is:

$$\text{Unemployment rate}=\frac{\text{Number of unemployed}}{\text{Total labour force}}\times 100\%$$

Note that the denominator is "the total labour force" and not the total population: people with no work who have no wish to work are not in the labour force at all. So a falling unemployment rate is not necessarily good news — discouraged job seekers may simply have left the labour force (the discouraged worker), shrinking the denominator.

### Nominal and real national income

(1) Nominal national income is the national income computed at current-year prices for goods and services, and it does not take inflation into account. Because prices may move sharply for reasons including inflation, the concept of real national income was introduced to make comparison possible.

(2) Real national income is the national income computed at base-year prices, using some earlier year as the base, or in other words the national income deflated by a price index.

The relation between nominal and real national income is:

$$\text{Real national income}=\text{Nominal national income} \div \text{Price deflator}$$

## Problems and analysis

### What problems must be watched when computing national income by the income and expenditure methods?

Accounting for national income by the income and expenditure methods requires attention to the following:

- (1) Gross national product is conditional on a given period, so spending on products or income from other periods occurring in the current period should not be counted in this period's GDP, to avoid double counting across periods.
- (2) Gross domestic product is valued at the market value of final goods and services, so intermediate inputs must not be counted, to avoid double counting.
- (3) The GDP that is computed is expressed in monetary terms, hence the distinction between nominal and real GDP.
- (4) In theoretical analysis the purpose of accounting is to explain the size of output in the economy, that is the real value of goods and services. GDP measured at current prices therefore has to be deflated by a price index: $\text{Real GDP}=\text{Nominal GDP} / \text{GDP deflator}$. The general price level is normally used as the GDP deflator.

### What is the relation between national income accounting and aggregate demand-and-supply analysis?

- (1) National income accounting is a settlement of how a country's economy performed over a given period. It provides a kind of hint for macroeconomic equilibrium analysis (aggregate demand-and-supply equilibrium analysis).

- ① National income accounting can be carried out by the expenditure method and the income method. From an ex post standpoint, ignoring statistical error, national income computed by the two methods is necessarily identical. Ex post, the larger the national income for which expenditure equals income, the better the economy has performed. From another angle, to explain the outcome of economic performance one may examine expenditure and income outside the operation of the economy. National income accounting identities thus reflect the equilibrium condition of the initial interaction between expenditure and income.

- ② If the economy is at equilibrium (aggregate demand equals aggregate supply), the outcome of economic performance will be the accounting result. So to analyse the determinants of national income, place expenditure and income in the economy separately as ex ante items, and the identity becomes exactly the equilibrium condition for the operation of those factors.

- (2) For any given general price level, a country's aggregate demand is made up of consumption demand, investment demand, government demand and demand from abroad; the supply of consumer goods and investment goods that forms aggregate supply shows up as the income obtained by the owners of those goods and services (consumption and savings). In other words, the two forces that make up the equilibrium condition, examined as ex ante plans, become aggregate demand and aggregate supply in analysis.

One line to carry it away: accounting is an **ex post** ledger (expenditure does equal income), while analysis is **ex ante** intention (planned spending and planned supply may not be equal) — macroeconomic theory studies exactly how that "may not be equal" gets adjusted.

## Limits and what follows

- **GDP is not welfare.** Kuznets wrote as much in his 1934 report: a country's welfare can hardly be inferred from a measure of national income. GDP counts neither housework nor voluntary labour, charges no cost for environmental damage and resource depletion, ignores distribution, and books disaster-recovery spending as growth. The Stiglitz-Sen-Fitoussi Commission (2009), prompted by the financial crisis, revisited these limits, and several countries began developing "green GDP" and welfare-dimension indicators — GDP remains the best single gauge, but nobody treats it as the whole dashboard anymore.
- **The unemployment rate misses people.** The discouraged, who have quit looking, do not count as unemployed; part-time workers count as employed; the underground economy is simply invisible — different definitions can tell very different stories. That is why statistical offices publish several measures at once, and why researchers often cross-check with the labour force participation rate.
- **The relation between income and well-being has an edge.** Cross-country comparisons show per capita GDP and residents' life satisfaction are roughly positively correlated; but Easterlin (1974) found that once income passes the subsistence line, within a single country over time, growth has not brought a matching rise in happiness — "more money, more happiness" holds between countries but holds far weaker inside a country's own history. The edge of the measure is, in the end, the edge of the theory.
