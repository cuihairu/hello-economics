# Chapter 9 Simple income determination theory

> **Core question of this chapter**: What determines a country's national income? How do consumption, saving and investment interact to form an equilibrium? Why is there a multiplier effect?

## Chapter roadmap

Classical economics answered with Say's law: supply creates its own demand, saved money automatically turns into investment through the interest rate, and the economy therefore always runs near full employment. In 1936 Keynes overturned the whole logic: investment is not determined by the "supply" of saving, but by firms' expectations about the future; saving is merely a residual of income — if income falls, saving falls on its own. The burden of adjustment moves from "the interest rate" to "income": when investment falls below saving, it is not a fall in the interest rate that corrects it, but a contraction of income until saving comes down to meet it. Depressions thereby get an explanation: deficient investment → contraction of income → saving passively falling to equality with investment — equilibrium is attained, but it is an equilibrium of unemployment.

This chapter's model subtracts deliberately: only the goods market, no money market (that is the task of the IS-LM model in Chapter 10); prices and the interest rate are both assumed fixed, and output adjusts alone. On this simplest canvas Keynes draws two psychological laws (a declining propensity to consume, unstable investment expectations) and one amplifier — the multiplier (the employment multiplier Kahn proposed in 1931 to defend public works, absorbed by Keynes as an income multiplier). The policy implication is direct and startling: one dose of government spending can, through a chain reaction, create income several times its own size.

## Core concepts

### The consumption function

The consumption function, also called the propensity to consume, is the function describing the relation between consumption and the level of income.
With the general price level unchanged, the consumption expenditure of the household sector depends chiefly on the level of income, so the consumption function can be written as:

$$C = C(Y)$$

**[Notation]**
| Symbol | English full name | Meaning |
|------|----------|----------|
| $C$ | Consumption | Consumption |
| $Y$ | Income / National Income | Income / national income |

Normally, as income rises, consumption expenditure rises too, but not as fast as income — that is, the marginal propensity to consume diminishes.

### Marginal propensity to consume

The marginal propensity to consume is the ratio of the part of an additional unit of income spent on additional consumption, given by:

$$MPC = \frac{\Delta C}{\Delta Y}$$

**[Notation]**
| Symbol | English full name | Meaning |
|------|----------|----------|
| $MPC$ | Marginal Propensity to Consume | Marginal propensity to consume |
| $\Delta C$ | Change in Consumption | Increase in consumption |
| $\Delta Y$ | Change in Income / National Income | Increase in income / national income |

In Keynes's view there is a psychological law between income and consumption: as income rises, consumption rises too, but the increase in consumption is smaller than the increase in income. Hence in general the marginal propensity to consume lies between 0 and 1.

This "psychological law" is one of the three laws of the *General Theory* and the foundation of the whole income determination theory: because MPC is less than 1, an increase in income always leaks a share out of the stream of consumption (into saving), so an expansion of autonomous spending decays and converges rather than amplifying without limit — and the denominator of the multiplier $1/(1-\beta)$ is exactly it.

### The saving function

Saving is the part of income not consumed, and so it depends on income. The saving function is the relation expressing how the quantity of saving changes as the quantity of income changes. Its general form is:

$$S = Y - C$$

**[Notation]**
| Symbol | English full name | Meaning |
|------|----------|----------|
| $S$ | Savings | Saving |
| $Y$ | Income / National Income | Income / national income |
| $C$ | Consumption | Consumption |

### Marginal propensity to save

The marginal propensity to save is the corresponding change in saving that arises when people's income changes (rises or falls) by one unit; it can also be read as the share of additional income that is saved.

In formula form:

$$MPS = \frac{\Delta S}{\Delta Y}$$

**[Notation]**
| Symbol | English full name | Meaning |
|------|----------|----------|
| $MPS$ | Marginal Propensity to Save | Marginal propensity to save |
| $\Delta S$ | Change in Savings | Increase in saving |
| $\Delta Y$ | Change in Income / National Income | Increase in income / national income |

The marginal propensity to save is always greater than 0 and less than 1.

### Investment multiplier

The investment multiplier is the product of Kahn's (1931) employment multiplier and Keynes's income determination theory: an increase in autonomous spending is not "one-off" — it is passed down the chain of "expenditure → income → re-consumption" layer by layer and ultimately adds up to a geometric series. With other things unchanged, suppose autonomous investment rises by one unit; then equilibrium national income rises by a factor of $\frac{1}{1-\beta}$. That is, if investment rises by $\Delta I$, the increase in equilibrium national income is:

$$\Delta Y = \frac{\Delta I}{1 - \beta}$$

**[Notation]**
| Symbol | English full name | Meaning |
|------|----------|----------|
| $\Delta Y$ | Change in National Income | Change in national income |
| $\Delta I$ | Change in Investment | Change in investment |
| $\beta$ | Marginal Propensity to Consume | Marginal propensity to consume |

This result is called the investment multiplier theorem.

### Tax multiplier

The tax multiplier is negative: taxation does not directly create demand — it first squeezes disposable income and then works indirectly through consumption, so it is already discounted by the MPC in the first round. With other things unchanged, suppose a lump-sum tax rises by one unit; equilibrium national income **falls** by $\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}$. If taxation rises by $\Delta T$, the change in equilibrium national income is:

$$\Delta Y = -\frac{\beta \Delta T}{1-\beta}$$

**[Notation]**
| Symbol | English full name | Meaning |
|------|----------|----------|
| $\Delta Y$ | Change in National Income | Change in national income |
| $\Delta T$ | Change in Taxes | Change in taxes |
| $\beta$ | Marginal Propensity to Consume | Marginal propensity to consume |

This result is called the tax multiplier theorem.

### Balanced-budget multiplier

The balanced-budget multiplier is the ratio of the change in national income to the change in spending when government revenue and expenditure rise or fall by equal amounts while the government's fiscal budget is kept balanced.

If government purchases and taxes both rise by one unit:
- Because of the rise in spending, national income rises by $\frac{1}{1-\beta}$
- Because of the rise in taxes, national income rises by $-\frac{\beta}{1-\beta}$

Therefore, when government spending and taxes both change by $\Delta G(=\Delta T)$, the change in equilibrium national income is:

$$\Delta Y = \frac{\Delta G}{1-\beta} - \frac{\beta \Delta T}{1-\beta} = \Delta G(=\Delta T)$$

**[Notation]**
| Symbol | English full name | Meaning |
|------|----------|----------|
| $\Delta Y$ | Change in National Income | Change in national income |
| $\Delta G$ | Change in Government Spending | Change in government spending |
| $\Delta T$ | Change in Taxes | Change in taxes |
| $\beta$ | Marginal Propensity to Consume | Marginal propensity to consume |

**Conclusion**: the balanced-budget multiplier equals 1, that is, when government spending and taxes both rise by the same amount, national income also rises by that amount.

## Questions and analysis

### Why is investment equals saving (I = S) the basic equilibrium condition of simple income determination? What is the mechanism by which national income changes?

**[Notation]**
| Symbol | English full name | Meaning |
|------|----------|----------|
| $AE$ | Aggregate Expenditure | Aggregate expenditure |
| $Y$ | Income / National Income | Income / national income |
| $C$ | Consumption | Consumption |
| $I$ | Investment | Investment |
| $S$ | Saving | Saving |

- (1) In general, the equilibrium national income of an economy is determined where the society's aggregate expenditure and aggregate income are equal. With the society's aggregate income given, a change in aggregate demand brings about a change in output. The output or national income of the economic society is determined by aggregate expenditure or aggregate demand, and the output determined by aggregate demand is equilibrium output. When the level of output equals the level of aggregate demand, firms' production settles down.

- (2) Simple income determination theory includes only an analysis of the goods market. It assumes that the economic society has only two sectors, households and firms. In a two-sector economy aggregate expenditure (or aggregate demand) consists of consumption and investment; if $AE$ denotes aggregate expenditure, $Y$ income, and $C$ and $I$ consumption and investment respectively, then, measuring national income by the expenditure method, equilibrium output can be written $AE=C+I$. In a two-sector economy aggregate income likewise consists of consumption and saving; measuring national income by the income method gives $Y= C+S$. Hence the equilibrium condition of income equal to expenditure can be written $AE= Y$, that is $I = S$. It follows that investment equals saving is the basic form of the equilibrium condition.

- (3) The mechanism by which national income changes works through the adjustment of firms' output in the economy:

- ①If the society's income exceeds demand, firms' output piles up, so inventories rise. To adjust to the market, firms cut production.
- ②If the society's income is below aggregate demand, firms' inventories fall, so firms raise production. Firms therefore organize production according to aggregate social demand, which finally brings the society to an equilibrium state where aggregate income equals aggregate expenditure.

### What is the equilibrium condition of the goods market? What criticisms has it received?

**[Notation]**
| Symbol | English full name | Meaning |
|------|----------|----------|
| $AE$ | Aggregate Expenditure | Aggregate expenditure |
| $Y$ | Income | Income |
| $C$ | Consumption | Consumption |
| $I$ | Investment | Investment |
| $S$ | Saving | Saving |

- (1) Of national income, aggregate expenditure consists of consumption expenditure and investment expenditure. If $AE$ denotes aggregate expenditure, then $AE=C+I$. Aggregate income $Y$ has two uses, consumption $C$ and saving $S$, so $Y= C+S$. The equilibrium condition of the goods market is that aggregate expenditure equals aggregate income. Hence $C+I= C+S$, so that investment equals saving, i.e. $I =S$. So long as this equilibrium condition is satisfied the problem of realizing the goods market is solved, and the circulation of the social total product proceeds smoothly.

- (2) Keynes's account of the equilibrium condition of the goods market has the following shortcomings:

	- ①The equilibrium condition of the goods market confounds saving with capital accumulation. Capital accumulation is the capitalization of surplus value; it occurs inside the firm, not in the household sector. The credit system certainly breaks the limit set by the firm's stock of monetary capital, yet even under modern capitalism with fairly developed credit relations, investment still draws chiefly on capital accumulation. Household saving is at most a supplement.

	- ②Investment and saving in the equilibrium condition of the goods market are the results of agents deciding according to their own psychological laws, and are therefore both capricious, making it hard to discuss either of them properly.

### How is equilibrium national income determined once government enters the model?

**[Notation]**
| Symbol | English full name | Meaning |
|------|----------|----------|
| $G$ / $G_0$ | Government Purchase / Government Spending | Government purchases / government purchase expenditure |
| $T_0$ | Lump-sum Tax | Lump-sum tax levied by government |
| $Y_d$ | Disposable Income | Disposable personal income |
| $C$ | Consumption | Consumption |
| $Y$ | National Income | National income |
| $\alpha$ | Autonomous Consumption | Autonomous consumption (constant) |
| $\beta$ | Marginal Propensity to Consume | Marginal propensity to consume |
| $I_0$ | Autonomous Investment | Autonomous investment |
| $I$ | Investment | Investment |

- (1) Macroeconomics calls an economy comprising the household sector, the firm sector and the government sector a three-sector economy. The three-sector economy is obtained by adding the government sector to the original two sectors (households and firms).

- (2) Once the government sector is added, the composition of aggregate expenditure and aggregate income both change. From the standpoint of aggregate expenditure, it consists of consumption, investment and government purchases.
	- First, assume government purchases are a government-controlled quantity that can be treated as a constant in determining national income, i.e. $G=G_0$.
	- Next, government taxation affects households' disposable income and so affects private consumption. Taxation can be a proportional tax or a lump-sum tax; the former is levied according to the size of the tax base, the latter is independent of it.

	To simplify the analysis, suppose government levies only a lump-sum tax on income. Let the lump-sum tax be $T_0$; then consumers' disposable income is $Y_d=Y-T_0$. Hence the consumption function is:
	$$C=\alpha + \beta Y_d= \alpha + \beta(Y -T_0)$$

	Accordingly, the national income determined by the equilibrium of planned expenditure and planned income is given by:
	$$C+I+G=\alpha + \beta(Y-T_0)+I_0+G_0=Y$$

That is:

$$Y=(\alpha - \beta T_0 +I_0 +G_0)/(1-\beta)$$

The equilibrium result shows that increases in investment and government purchases raise equilibrium income, while an increase in taxation lowers it.

### Why can a multiplier turn one expenditure into a multiple of income? What conditions does it require?

**[Notation]**
| Symbol | English full name | Meaning |
|------|----------|----------|
| $\Delta Y$ | Change in National Income | Change in national income |
| $\Delta I$ | Change in Investment | Change in investment |
| $\beta$ | Marginal Propensity to Consume | Marginal propensity to consume |
| $\frac{1}{1-\beta}$ | Investment Multiplier | Investment multiplier |

- (1) The multiplier principle is the effect whereby a change in various forms of spending produces a multiple change in national income.
Here the change in spending includes changes in private investment and government purchases, as well as changes in taxes and government transfers. If it is investment in spending that increases, the multiplier is the investment multiplier; if it is government spending, the multiplier is the government spending multiplier. Keynes's multiplier theory explains how changes in the various forms of spending in the economy affect changes in national income.

- (2) This is illustrated here by the investment multiplier theorem.
The investment multiplier is the ratio between the change in income and the change in investment spending that brings about that change, given by:

$${\Delta Y}=\frac{\Delta I}{1-\beta}$$

where $\beta$ is the household sector's marginal propensity to consume, and $\frac{1}{1-\beta}$ is the investment multiplier. It shows that the change in national income is directly proportional to the change in the investment (government purchases) that causes it, and inversely proportional to the marginal propensity to save. The investment multiplier shows that with other things unchanged, once investment rises in the economy the increase in aggregate demand raises output, and this increase can set off a chain reaction: the rise in income again raises the quantity consumers spend, further increasing aggregate demand, until in the end aggregate income rises several times over. In short, the multiplier theory reveals a feature of the modern economy: because of the close links among its sectors, an increase in one sector's spending (demand) not only raises that sector's production and income accordingly, but also raises production, income and spending in other sectors, so that the increase in national income is a multiple of the initial increase in spending.

- (3) Conditions under which the multiplier principle operates
	- ①Idle resources must exist in the society, i.e. excess productive capacity must exist. Without excess capacity and idle resources, an increase in investment and the resulting increase in consumption expenditure would not raise production but would only push up the price level.
	- ②The mutual independence of the determinants of investment and saving. They must be assumed independent; otherwise the multiplier effect would be much smaller, because the increase in the demand for money funds caused by increased investment would raise the interest rate, and a higher interest rate encourages saving and weakens consumption, partly offsetting the tendency for increased investment to raise income and thereby consumption.
	- ③Whether the increase in the money supply can meet the need created by increased spending. If the money supply is constrained, then when investment and consumption expenditure rise the accompanying rise in money demand gets no support from a corresponding rise in money supply; the interest rate rises, which not only suppresses consumption but also investment, lowering aggregate demand.
	- ④The additional income must not be spent on imports; otherwise the rise in income will be limited.

## Derivations and worked examples

### Given the consumption function C = 100 + 0.6Y and autonomous investment, $I = 60$, find:

**[Notation]**
| Symbol | English full name | Meaning |
|------|----------|----------|
| $C$ | Consumption | Consumption |
| $Y$ | National Income | National income |
| $I$ | Investment | Investment |
| $S$ | Saving | Saving |
| $Y_f$ | Full-employment National Income | Full-employment level of national income |
| $k$ | Investment Multiplier | Investment multiplier |
| $\Delta Y$ | Change in National Income | Change in national income |
| $\Delta I$ | Change in Investment | Change in investment |

- (1) What is equilibrium national income (Y)?
- (2) What is equilibrium saving (S)?
- (3) If the full-employment level of national income is $Y_f = 1000$, how must investment change for the economy to reach full-employment equilibrium?
- (4) What is the investment multiplier (k) in this problem?

- (1) From the composition of national income:

$$Y=C+I=100+0.6Y+60$$

Solving gives equilibrium national income: $Y = 400$.

- (2) When $S=I$ the goods market is in equilibrium. Hence equilibrium saving is $S=I=60$.
- (3) With the full-employment level of national income at $Y_f = 1000$, consumption when the economy attains full-employment equilibrium is:

$$C=100+0.6\times 1000=700$$

Then saving is:

$$S=Y_f -C = 1000 - 700 = 300$$

Therefore, in full-employment equilibrium investment is:

$$I=S=300$$

That is, investment must rise from 60 to 300 for the economy to move from the unemployment equilibrium at $Y=400$ to the full-employment equilibrium at $Y_f=1000$.

- (4) The investment multiplier is:

$$k=\frac{\Delta Y}{\Delta I}=\frac{1000-400}{300-60}=2.5$$

exactly equal to $\frac{1}{1-\beta}=\frac{1}{0.4}=2.5$: the extra 240 units of investment is amplified by the income–consumption chain reaction into 600 units of additional income.

## Limits and what comes next

- **The consumption-function puzzle: whose propensity declines?** Keynes said the marginal propensity to consume falls with income, and short-run data support him; but Kuznets (1946) found that over decades of American data the share of total income going to consumption stayed almost constant. The propensity in the short run, the ratio in the long run — Modigliani's life-cycle hypothesis and Friedman's permanent income hypothesis (1950s) each unraveled the puzzle: people arrange consumption according to "lifetime income" rather than "current income", short-run fluctuations are smoothed out by saving, and in the long run consumption grows in proportion. Consumption theory thereby passed from a psychological law to a problem of intertemporal choice.
- **The paradox of thrift and the fallacy of composition.** In this model, if society as a whole saves more, income contracts and aggregate saving does not necessarily rise — thrift that is a virtue for an individual household becomes, in the aggregate, a cause of depression. This "fallacy of composition" is the very essence of Keynes's macro view: what holds at the micro level need not hold in the aggregate. But it depends on the assumption that "investment does not expand with saving" — return to the classical world (where the interest rate turns saving into investment) and the paradox vanishes. Which world is nearer reality is the dispute running through the chapters that follow.
- **The multiplier has taken some blows from evidence.** The textbook multiplier $1/(1-\beta)$ assumes prices, the interest rate and the money supply all stay put, and that income buys only domestic goods. In reality taxes and imports leak automatically with income, the interest rate rises and crowds out private investment, and the central bank reacts — econometric estimates of the multiplier usually fall between 1 and 1.5, so the "amplifier" of expansionary fiscal policy is far weaker than the 45-degree-line diagrams suggest. The claim that the balanced-budget multiplier equals 1, made in 1945, has on the other hand stood the test of time.
