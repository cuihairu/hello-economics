# The knowledge backbone of this site

I organize the site by subject: four courses — western economics, money and banking, public finance and international economics — plus a group on mathematical foundations. This page pulls together five things: which course is framed on which standard textbook, the time coordinates of the classics and the theories, official documents and data sources, application scenarios and reading clues, and the site's conventions. The working draft behind it is the [bibliography](/reference/参考书目), the [books](/books) shelf, and live fetches from eight official sites (2026-10-10). Where the sources fall short I say so on the spot rather than invent a claim.

## 1. Subject frameworks and textbooks

1. **Western economics is built on the fifteen chapters of Gao Hongye.** Gao Hongye's *Western Economics* (8th edition, separate micro and macro volumes, China Renmin University Press) is the main source of the chapter framework — from supply and demand through the disagreements between macro schools, fifteen chapters, which is exactly this system. Mankiw's *Principles of Economics* is another telling of the same system, with richer cases; Liang Xiaomin's *Introduction to Western Economics* (4th edition) fills the entry-level view. See the western economics entries in the [bibliography](/reference/参考书目).

2. **Money and banking follows the framework of Cao Longqi's *Finance*.** Sixth edition: the chapters on money supply and demand, interest rates, the financial system and policy all run on it. Mishkin's *The Economics of Money, Banking, and Financial Markets* adds the empirical view of money demand and monetary transmission.

3. **Public finance takes its structure from Chen Gong's *Public Finance*.** Tenth edition; the organization of the chapters on fiscal functions, expenditure, taxation, government debt, and budget and deficit is carried over from this book.

4. **The mainstream framework of international economics comes from Krugman.** Krugman and Obstfeld's *International Economics* covers trade theory and the open macroeconomy; the domestic-textbook wording of the balance of payments and exchange rate chapters is filled in from the macro part of Gao Hongye's *Western Economics*.

5. **Socialist economic theory has two books behind it.** Wei Xinghua and Zhang Yu's *Socialist Economic Theory* (Higher Education Press) is the standard framework from the introduction through government regulation; Wu Jinglian's *China's Economic Reform in the Contemporary Era* gives the empirical narrative of institutional change and reform history.

6. **Mathematical foundations follow the chapter split of the standard domestic textbooks.** Tongji University's *Advanced Mathematics* is the standard treatment of limits, differentiation and integration; the linear algebra and probability and statistics parts correspond to similar standard texts (Tongji's *Linear Algebra*, Sheng Zhou's *Probability Theory and Mathematical Statistics*). Specific editions follow whatever the chapters actually cite on the site.

7. **There are two lines of reference for the historical record of economic thought.** Henry William Spiegel's *Growth of Economic Thought* supplies the facts for the timeline and the economist entries; Mark Blaug's *Economic Theory in Retrospect* supplies the theoretical lineage and the critical commentary. Works of the primary-source level (Adam Smith's *The Wealth of Nations*, 1776; Marshall's *Principles of Economics*, 1890; Keynes's *General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*, 1936; Friedman and Schwartz's *A Monetary History of the United States*, 1963; Hayek's *The Use of Knowledge in Society*, 1945; Coase's *The Problem of Social Cost*, 1960) are cited where the chapters touch them.

## 2. Coordinates of the classics

8. **Thirteen representative works, laid out by year of publication — each marks a shift in how questions are asked.** From *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) drawing the boundary of the discipline, to the *General Theory* (1936) reversing the direction of policy in the middle of the Great Depression, to *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2013) putting inequality back at the centre of the agenda. The full list with covers is on the [books shelf](/books); author names link through to the [economist entries](/people).

## 3. Official documents and data sources

9. **FRED is the time-series base of data journalism.** Run by the St. Louis Fed, it has carried global time series since 1991; charts, an Excel add-in, an API and classroom material are all available, and ALFRED holds point-in-time (vintage) data. Most of the CPI, unemployment and interest rate charts you see in news come from here. Source: [FRED home](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/) (fetched live on 2026-10-10 via WebFetch; local curl failed at the connection layer — both channels used).

10. **NBER working papers are the entry point for tracking the frontier.** A non-profit research organisation with 19 project groups; working papers come with popular summaries (*The Digest*), and the Business Cycle Dating Committee sets the official chronology of US recessions — the peak month is the last month of expansion and the trough month the last month of recession, judged on three monthly indicators of depth, diffusion and duration, and deliberately revised backwards, announced only once the data have settled. Source: [NBER home](https://www.nber.org/), [Business Cycle Dating](https://www.nber.org/research/business-cycle-dating) (fetched live 2026-10-10).

11. **BEA is the official publisher of US GDP.** A statistical agency under the Department of Commerce, it releases GDP, personal income and spending, international trade and regional data by topic, region and account; the interactive data tables (iTable) and the Learning Center are friendly to self-study. Source: [BEA home](https://www.bea.gov/) (fetched live 2026-10-10).

12. **World Bank Open Data is the cross-country comparison tool.** Free and open, it holds global development data; WDI browses by economy or by indicator, DataBank visualizes, and maps are shaded by income group. From 2026 the data are folded into the Data360 platform. Source: [World Bank Open Data](https://data.worldbank.org/) (fetched live 2026-10-10).

13. **The National Bureau of Statistics is the official outlet for China's macro data.** Monthly, quarterly, annual, census, international and departmental data plus the *China Statistical Yearbook* are online; PMI, producer prices and the like are posted on the front page. Source: [National Bureau of Statistics](https://www.stats.gov.cn/) (fetched live 2026-10-10; first pass, local curl failed at the connection layer and the text came via WebFetch, and a same-day retest got curl 200).

14. **The People's Bank of China is the first-hand source for China's monetary policy.** Its site is organized around monetary policy, survey and statistics, financial stability and similar columns; monetary policy committee quarterly meeting communiqués, notices on adjusting policy tools and various reports appear there first, and there is also a glossary and a financial literacy section. When reading the [monetary policy](/monetary/货币政策) chapter, every expansion of the toolkit can be checked against the original wording here. Source: [People's Bank of China](https://www.pbc.gov.cn/) (fetched locally with curl on 2026-10-10; first pass 302, retest 200).

15. **The European Central Bank is the first-hand source for euro-area monetary policy.** Its site is organized around monetary policy and markets, the 2% price stability target, open market operations, asset purchase programmes and similar columns; the latest monetary policy press conference (2026-09-10) and various reports appear there first. When reading the [monetary policy](/monetary/货币政策) chapter, every step of the toolkit and of transmission can be checked against the original wording here. Source: [European Central Bank](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/) (fetched locally with curl on 2026-10-10: front page 302 to the English page, 200; earlier readings were intermittent, this round of retests reached it).

16. **The Nobel Prize site in Economic Sciences is the yearly cross-check for the economist entries.** Established by Sveriges Riksbank in 1968, winners are announced each October; the site carries winner stories and popular-science essays, which can be checked against the entries and years on the [economist page](/people). Source: [Nobel Prize, Economic Sciences](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/) (fetched locally with curl on 2026-10-10; WebFetch got 403 anti-crawler — both channels used).

## 4. Application scenarios and reading clues

17. **Reading data journalism, find the publisher first.** CPI comes from BLS or the NBS, GDP from BEA or the NBS, interest rates from FRED and the PBoC; before reading a bulletin, separate year-on-year from month-on-year and get the base period and the definition straight — those three terms are on the [glossary](/glossary) — and only then look at the chart. This site's [inflation and deflation](/monetary/通货膨胀与通货紧缩) chapter and the [macroeconomic activity and macroeconomics](/western/宏观经济活动与宏观经济学) chapter are organized around that reading order. Source: this round of live fetches, see items 9, 11, 13 and 14 of section 3.

18. **Tracking the frontier runs on two lines: NBER and the Nobel Prize.** Working papers plus the "New This Week" mail digest; *The Digest* popular summaries are free to read. The Nobel announcement each October is the yearly measure of theoretical influence. Source: items 10 and 16 of section 3 (fetched live 2026-10-10).

19. **For primary sources and historical records, go to FRASER.** The St. Louis Fed's digitized archive of economic history: scanned Federal Reserve bulletins, Economic Reports of the President and other historical documents; the origins of the thirteen works on this site's [books shelf](/books) can be read alongside them there. Source: the FRED front page's description of FRASER (fetched live 2026-10-10); FRASER itself was unreachable on both channels, so the details of its contents are not verified.

20. **My usual order of reading: timeline → subject → classic → person.** First look at the [theory timeline](/timeline) to see how a question got answered, then check the body of the subject (each Readme carries a "how the theory was born and how it was tested" section), then turn the primary sources by the [books shelf](/books) in year order, and finally look at the network of influence on the [economist entries](/people). Source: this site's own structure (walked through on 2026-10-10).

## 5. Conventions and traps

21. **Timeline years use the "year of appearance", not the "year of acceptance".** Coase's *The Problem of Social Cost* (1960) was cited by almost nobody that year; it only became the foundation of law and economics in the 1970s, through Stigler's naming and promotion. Rational expectations was proposed by Muth in 1961 and only really rewrote macro models after the Lucas critique in 1976. The moment a consensus forms usually lags by fifteen to twenty years — that is a different line to draw. This convention is noted on the [theory timeline](/timeline) page.

22. **Mankiw and Gao Hongye are two tellings of one system, not two opposing theories.** The prose here is organized on the Gao Hongye framework, with Mankiw supplying cases; when the two are compared, the difference is written as a difference in *telling*, not as a disagreement in *theory*.

23. **This site does not pile up memorization points; it answers three questions.** Why did it appear (what problem had it to solve at the time), how did it develop (who corrected it, what new problems did it leave), and how was it tested (which side do real data and later work stand on)? The "how the theory was born and how it was tested" section of each chapter is written to that spec, so every formula goes back to the situation that produced it.

24. **Economic data get revised, so cite with a sense of time.** BEA's first GDP reading is revised as the data improve, and the NBER deliberately waits for the data to settle before declaring a peak or a trough; FRED's ALFRED exists precisely to hold point-in-time data. When citing a number, note the date and the vintage you took it at. Source: items 9, 10 and 11 of section 3 (fetched live 2026-10-10).

25. **Official sites block crawling differently, so reachability has to be judged on two channels — and the channels themselves fail intermittently.** First round of measurement: BLS and IMF returned 403 to curl, FRED and FRASER failed at the local connection layer but WebFetch could reach them, and the Nobel site was the reverse (curl 200, WebFetch 403). Same-day retest: FRED and FRASER still 000, ECB 302 (jump to the English page, 200), IMF and OECD 403 (anti-crawler), BLS went from 403 to 000; in this round WebFetch reported "cannot verify the security of the domain" for every domain, which is a network limit on the tool side rather than the sites being unreachable. The same site changes result at different times (the NBS went from failure to 200, the PBoC from 302 to 200) — a failure on a single channel does not count. Source: this round of measurement (two rounds, 2026-10-10).

26. **Things with no evidence, and things not done.** The official-documents and application-scenarios sections were added on 2026-10-10 (sections 3 and 4). The details of FRASER's contents, and the bodies of IMF, OECD and BLS, were never fetched, so they get no entries. The RePEc archives are researcher-governed rather than an official publisher, so they do not go in "official documents" (decided 2026-10-10). The ECB was originally excluded as "intermittent"; the same-day retest reached it (302 → 200), so the reason no longer holds, and it is now item 15 of section 3 (revised 2026-10-10). Every other entry has a source.
