# Money and banking

This group of subjects is not about reciting three piles of terms on money, banks and exchange rates; it answers one overarching question:

**How money and credit are created, how the financial system runs, and how the central bank keeps it stable in an open economy.**

Compressed into a chain of questions, the whole course falls into roughly three layers:

1. Money and interest rates: why money exists, and how the interest rate is formed.
2. Banks and the financial system: how credit expands, and why it cannot do without markets and regulation.
3. Open macro-finance: how inflation, monetary policy, the exchange rate and internal–external equilibrium connect.

## 1. The questions this group keeps coming back to

- Why people hold money, and why the interest rate matters.
- Why commercial banks are the centre of modern credit creation.
- Why the financial system cannot rely on market discipline alone.
- Why inflation and deflation both become macro problems.
- Why the exchange rate, the international monetary system and internal–external equilibrium sit in one and the same course.

## 2. How the theories were born and how they were tested

- **A century of testing the equation of exchange**: Fisher wrote the quantity theory of money as $MV=PT$ in 1911. Friedman and Schwartz used nearly a century of data in *A Monetary History of the United States* (1963) to verify its most forceful corollary: the Great Depression was not a spontaneous market collapse but the result of a one-third contraction of the money supply in 1929–1933 — the "Great Contraction" became a live experiment for monetary theory.
- **Debt–deflation that fulfils itself**: Fisher proposed in 1933 that over-indebtedness triggers a spiral of "sell assets → prices fall → real debt grows heavier → sell more assets"; he lived through the mechanism himself in the 1929 bankruptcy. After 2008 the theory returned to the centre of macro policy debate.
- **The quarrel over the liquidity trap**: Keynes imagined that at very low interest rates monetary expansion stops working. Japan's zero-rate experience after the 1990s and its quantitative easing experiments turned this hypothesis from a textbook fancy into a testing ground that has run for thirty years.
- **The empirical fate of Tobin's q**: the co-movement of share prices and investment exists in the data but is weak, which bred corrections such as "financing constraints" and "uncertainty" — a specimen of how a theory evolves after being partly falsified by data.
