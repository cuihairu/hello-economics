# Chapter 8. Exchange rate theory

> **The core question of this chapter**: how is the exchange rate determined? Which stretch of reality can each doctrine - purchasing power parity, the international lending theory and the rest - explain? And how should the exchange rate regime be chosen?

## The arc of this chapter

The order in which exchange rate theories arose followed exactly the order in which monetary systems collapsed. The gold standard era needed no explanation of the exchange rate - the ratio of gold contents (mint parity) was the answer, and all that remained was the small fluctuation of foreign exchange supply and demand around the gold points, hence the international lending theory. After the First World War countries' paper money ceased to be convertible into gold and prices in Austria and Germany soared, so "how much foreign currency is this note worth" suddenly became an anchorless question; in his 1918 article and his 1922 book Cassel pointed the answer at the domestic value of money, that is purchasing power parity, while in the same period Keynes in *A Tract on Monetary Reform* (1923) had already used interest differentials to explain the effect of capital flows on the exchange rate. In the German inflation of the 1920s the exchange rate broke loose both from prices and from the balance of payments, so the French economist Aftalion proceeded from subjective valuation and proposed the psychological theory of exchange rates. After Bretton Woods collapsed and floating rates arrived, commodity arbitrage could not explain exchange rates changing by the minute, so monetary analysis tied the exchange rate to money supply and demand, and the globalisation of capital in the 1970s gave rise to the financial asset approach, which treats foreign exchange as one item in a portfolio (Dornbusch's 1976 overshooting model is its most famous branch). The five doctrines of this chapter are not five parallel opinions but the answers of five separate eras. The part on regime choice comes from another question: after 1973 fixed rates were no longer given from outside, and "which arrangement to choose" itself became a policy question, to which Heller's economic analysis and the later "bipolarity thesis" are the answers.

## Core concepts

### Purchasing power parity

Purchasing power parity was proposed by the Swedish economist Cassel at the beginning of the twentieth century as the basis for explaining long-run exchange rate determination.
He held that the purchasing power of the two countries' currencies (or the two countries' price levels) can determine the exchange rate between them, which in effect analyses exchange rate determination at the level of the value that money represents. He also held that changes in the exchange rate depend on changes in the two currencies' purchasing power. Purchasing power parity has two forms: the absolute form and the relative form.
The absolute form explains the determination of the exchange rate at a point in time;
the relative form explains the movement of the exchange rate between two points in time. Of the two, relative purchasing power parity has more practical significance.

The relative form was put forward first because the absolute form requires the two countries' general price indices to be comparable and the base-period exchange rate to be reasonable, conditions hard to meet in practice; the relative form cares only about "rates of change", which cancels out part of the weighting differences between the two baskets of goods, and is therefore more practical. This also reveals purchasing power parity's real use: it is not a short-run trading tool but a long-run reference frame for judging whether a currency is overvalued or undervalued.

### The exchange rate regime

The exchange rate regime refers to the systematic rules laid down by a country, an economy, an economic region or the international community on the principles, bases, methods and institutions for setting, maintaining, adjusting and managing the exchange rate. The exchange rate regime is an important component of the monetary system.

It is worth noting that all exchange rate arrangements differ in only one core respect: when an external shock arrives, who bears the cost of adjustment. Under a pegged rate government bears it (foreign exchange reserves and domestic contraction), under a floating rate the private sector bears it (exchange rate fluctuation), and intermediate arrangements share it between the two. The four items of an exchange rate regime are all, in essence, answers to "who bears it and how":
- (1) The principles and bases for setting the exchange rate. For example whether a country's exchange rate is set officially or by the market, and on what basis the value of its currency itself rests.
- (2) The methods of maintaining and adjusting the exchange rate. For example what adjustment methods a country adopts to revalue or devalue its currency, and whether it fixes the exchange rate, lets it float freely or manages its float.
- (3) The laws, systems and policies for managing the exchange rate. For example whether a country manages the exchange rate strictly, loosely or by non-intervention, and the scope of application and the objects of its laws and policies.
- (4) The institutions that set, maintain and manage the exchange rate. For example whether a country assigns the responsibility for managing the exchange rate to the central treasury, the monetary authority or a specialised agency.

### The currency board arrangement

The currency board arrangement refers to an explicit legal commitment that the currency of a country or region will be exchanged at a fixed rate for a particular foreign currency, together with limits on official money issue, so as to ensure the statutory obligation is honoured, as in Argentina and China's Hong Kong Special Administrative Region.

The currency board is the most extreme of the exchange rate arrangements: it voluntarily gives up the lender-of-last-resort function, and issue of the domestic currency requires full foreign exchange backing. Why tie one's own hands? Because the credibility of a peg is itself a scarce good - a verbal promise can be broken at any time, whereas the legal rule "no foreign exchange, no issue" makes breaking it technically impossible. Hong Kong's linked exchange rate system (from 1983) relies on just this credibility, at the cost that interest rates must follow the dollar and the banking system must rely on other arrangements (such as the liquidity support facility) to fill the gap left by having no lender of last resort. Argentina's currency board was set up in 1991 and collapsed in the 2002 crisis, showing that rules cannot substitute for fiscal discipline.

## Problems and analysis

### What problem does purchasing power parity solve? Where do its contributions lie?

- (1) Purchasing power parity was proposed by the Swedish economist Cassel at the beginning of the twentieth century as the basis for explaining long-run exchange rate determination. He held that the purchasing power of the two countries' currencies (or the two countries' price levels) can determine the exchange rate between them, which in effect analyses exchange rate determination at the level of the value that money represents. He also held that changes in the exchange rate depend on changes in the two currencies' purchasing power.

- (2) In the more than eighty years since it appeared, purchasing power parity has had a wide international influence both in theory and in practice, which has made it one of the most important exchange rate theories of the twentieth century. Its contributions show themselves mainly in the following respects:

① Cassel held that the purchasing power of the two countries' currencies (or the two countries' price levels) can determine the exchange rate between them, which in effect analyses exchange rate determination at the level of the value that money represents, and this grasps the main aspect of exchange rate determination. Its direction is therefore correct.

② As a conversion tool, purchasing power parity can convert a country's main national economic indicators, such as GDP and GNP, from their expression in domestic currency into another country's currency for international economic comparison, and can avoid the distortion that arises in a country's indicators expressed in foreign currency when the prevailing exchange rate is too high or too low. It is therefore generally held that conversion at purchasing power parity is more scientific and reliable than conversion at the prevailing actual exchange rate.

③ Purchasing power parity determines the long-run trend of the exchange rate. Ignoring the various short-term factors that affect exchange rate fluctuation over short periods, in the long run the course of the exchange rate and the trend of purchasing power parity are broadly consistent.

④ Purchasing power parity links price indices and the exchange rate level, which is in effect to discuss money transactions from the standpoint of goods transactions, and this is of no small reference value for discussing the relation between a country's exchange rate policy and the development of its import and export trade.

### How does the international lending theory explain exchange rate formation under the gold standard?

The international lending theory, also called the balance of payments theory, is a theory popular in the heyday of the gold standard that explains the formation of foreign exchange supply and demand and of the exchange rate.

Because mint parity existed, the theory concentrates on foreign exchange supply and demand. Its main idea is that the state of foreign exchange supply and demand depends on the creditor and debtor relations arising from international merchandise imports and exports and capital flows - the balance of payments. When a country's liquid claims, that is foreign exchange receivables, exceed its liquid liabilities, that is foreign exchange payables, the supply of foreign exchange exceeds demand, so the exchange rate of foreign currency falls; and conversely it rises.

## Synthetic essay

### How did exchange rate theory evolve step by step?

Western economists have long studied the determination of the exchange rate in depth and put forward a number of doctrines expounding exchange rate determination and fluctuation from different angles.

- (1) The international lending theory

The international lending theory, also called the balance of payments theory, is a theory popular in the heyday of the gold standard that explains the formation of foreign exchange supply and demand and of the exchange rate. Because mint parity existed, the theory concentrates on foreign exchange supply and demand. The state of foreign exchange supply and demand depends on the creditor and debtor relations arising from international merchandise imports and exports and capital flows - the balance of payments. When a country's liquid claims, that is foreign exchange receivables, exceed its liquid liabilities, that is foreign exchange payables, the supply of foreign exchange exceeds demand, so the exchange rate of foreign currency falls; and conversely it rises.

- (2) The purchasing power parity doctrine

Purchasing power parity is an exchange rate theory with a very long history and far-reaching influence. Its central idea is that people want foreign currency for no other reason than that it has the ability to buy goods abroad. And the purchasing power of money is in fact the reciprocal of the price level, so the exchange rate is in reality determined by the ratio of the two countries' price levels. This ratio is called purchasing power parity and can be expressed in the following two formulas. Absolute purchasing power parity:

$$r=\frac{P_A}{P_B}$$

Here $P_A$  is the general price index of country A; $P_B$  is the general price index of country B; the exchange rate r is the ratio of the two countries' general price indices. Relative purchasing power parity:

$$r_1=r_0\frac{\frac{P_{A1}}{P_{B1}}}{\frac{P_{A0}}{P_{B0}}}$$

Here $r_0$  is the exchange rate in the base period; $r_1$  is the exchange rate in some period; $P_{A0}$、$P_{B0}$  and $P_{A1}$ 、 $P_{B1}$  are the price indices of countries A and B in the base period and in some period respectively. Here the exchange rate  $r_1$  reflects the ratio of the changes in the two countries' relative price indices. This doctrine coexisted with mint parity. The reason it could exist at that time is that coins of equal gold content do not necessarily have equal "purchasing power". As for the modern fiat credit money system, the value a country's currency represents is shown precisely by the purchasing power it represents. So after mint parity withdrew, purchasing power parity remained. The core contradiction of this doctrine is that its premises are subject to the limiting condition that the two countries' production structures, consumption structures and price systems be broadly similar. If these conditions hold, the comparability of the two countries' money purchasing power is fairly full; otherwise it is small.

- (3) The psychological theory of exchange rates

The psychological theory analyses the exchange rate from the angle of people's subjective psychology. The theory holds that in conditions of economic disorder the movement of the exchange rate is inconsistent both with foreign exchange receipts and payments and with movements of purchasing power parity; at such times the most important factor determining the exchange rate is people's psychological judgement and forecast. Psychological expectations do have a certain influence, but to overemphasise subjective psychological factors is one-sided.

- (4) Monetary analysis

Monetary analysis developed on the foundation of purchasing power parity theory. It holds that the exchange rate is constrained by the two countries' money supplies, thereby linking the exchange rate to monetary policy. The doctrine stresses the role of the supply and demand of money in determining money's purchasing power, and is therefore of some significance, though it errs in relying too heavily on the quantity theory of money.

- (5) The financial asset approach

The financial asset approach arose in the 1970s. In that period international capital moved in volume, financial markets developed internationally and financial assets grew ever more diverse. In particular after floating rates were adopted, with changes in interest rates, the balance of payments, inflation and other factors, the exchange rates of the various currencies changed constantly. Choosing which foreign-currency financial assets to hold (including money and securities) thereby became a vital question for investors.
Investors frequently adjust the proportions of their foreign-currency assets according to economic conditions and expectations, which often causes large international flows of money capital and has a great influence on exchange rates.
The common limitation of monetary analysis and the financial asset approach is that they treat the exchange rate mainly as the price in asset markets and underestimate the constraint that the trade balance and goods markets still exert on the exchange rate's long-run trend - the capital account may decide today's exchange rate, but it is the current account that decides where the exchange rate should sit ten years from now.

### What does a country's choice between fixed and floating rates depend on?

The exchange rate regime is an important component of the monetary system, and refers to the systematic rules laid down by a country, an economy, an economic region or the international community on the principles, methods, manners and institutions for setting, maintaining, adjusting and managing the exchange rate.

- (1) The kinds of exchange rate regime

The International Monetary Fund divides exchange rate regimes into three broad classes: first, fixed exchange rates; second, managed floating rates; third, freely floating rates. A fixed exchange rate is one in which the monetary authority keeps the rate of the domestic currency against other currencies basically fixed and confines the band of fluctuation within narrow limits. A managed float is one in which the monetary authority intervenes in the market through various measures and instruments so that the rate floats in a direction favourable to the country or is maintained at a level favourable to it. A free float means the monetary authority rarely intervenes in the foreign exchange market and the rate is determined spontaneously by supply and demand in that market.

The repeated eruption of financial crises in emerging market countries, above all the Asian financial crisis of 1997, drew the international community's close attention to the question of exchange rate arrangement. From 1999 the International Monetary Fund further subdivided the three broad regime types into the following eight categories:
- ① Exchange rate arrangements with no separate legal tender;
- ② Currency board arrangements;
- ③ Conventional pegged arrangements;
- ④ Horizontal peg arrangements;
- ⑤ Crawling peg arrangements;
- ⑥ Crawling band arrangements;
- ⑦ Managed floating with no predetermined path;
- ⑧ Independently floating arrangements.

- (2) The merits and drawbacks of the various arrangements

- ① A floating exchange rate regime can let the exchange rate lever adjust the balance of payments automatically, reducing the influence of changes in international economic conditions and of foreign countries' economic policies, and lowering the risk of shocks from international hot money. Its feature is to let "the market correct the market" and make market participants bear their own risks. But the uncertainty brought by frequent and violent exchange rate fluctuations also harms the domestic economy. Moreover, in today's world a completely freely floating exchange rate regime does not in fact exist.

- ② A fixed exchange rate regime avoids frequent violent exchange rate fluctuation, gives the market a clear price signal, stabilises expectations, and favours foreign trade settlement and the normal flow of capital. Its feature is to let government lead the market, with government bearing the risk of market change. But market participants lose their risk awareness and their capacity to resist risk, and it easily induces large short-term capital inflows. With large capital inflows the monetary authority is often forced to revalue or devalue the currency, triggering financial turbulence.

- ③ Between fixed and floating rates lies the managed float, which combines the merits of both: the rate is generated by market supply and demand, which largely overcomes the defects of a fixed regime and lets the country keep a relatively independent macroeconomic policy. At the same time, when the rate strays far from economic fundamentals and brings uncertainty and rising transaction costs, the regime preserves the government's right to correct "market defects" in time, avoiding damage to the real economy from excessive exchange rate volatility. But a regime that must be both fixed and floating is fairly difficult to operate.

- (3) The economic analysis of regime choice

The "economic analysis" of exchange rate regime choice was put forward mainly by Robert Heller, international economic adviser to former US president Kennedy, who held that a country's choice of exchange rate regime is decided mainly by economic factors.
These factors are:
- ① The degree of economic openness;
- ② The size of the economy;
- ③ The commodity structure and geographic distribution of import and export trade;
- ④ The development of domestic financial markets and their degree of integration with international financial markets;
- ⑤ The relative inflation rate.

The concrete relation of these factors to the choice of regime is:
- A high degree of openness, a small economy, or imports and exports concentrated in a few commodities or a few countries generally inclines a country toward a fixed or pegged exchange rate regime.
- A low degree of openness, diversified import and export commodities or a dispersed geographic distribution, close ties with international financial markets, considerable and frequent capital inflows and outflows, or domestic inflation inconsistent with other major countries, inclines a country toward a floating or flexible exchange rate regime.
- According to Heller's comparison of some countries' exchange rate policies, a floating regime is clearly associated with a low ratio of imports and exports to GNP (that is low openness), a high diversification of the commodity structure and geographic distribution of import and export trade, a relatively high inflation rate, and a high degree of financial internationalisation.

## Limits and what follows

- **Purchasing power parity is both confirmed and refuted by the data.** In the long run it does work: Rogoff's (1996) survey found the "half-life" of the real exchange rate's return to its mean to be roughly three to five years, tests over short windows almost all reject PPP, but with samples of two centuries or more (Lothian and others' work) convergence can be seen. That is, as a yardstick of "undervalued or overvalued" it is reliable, while as a trading signal it is too slow - the "Big Mac index" the Economist has published since 1986 uses it exactly as a popular long-run reference, not as a short-run forecast.
- **Asset market theories won the short-run explanation but did not win prediction.** Frankel's monetary approach and Dornbusch's (1976) overshooting model explain the exchange rate as the price of portfolio equilibrium and successfully show why exchange rates overshoot in response to monetary shocks; but Meese and Rogoff's (1983) tests showed that these fundamentals models performed out of sample no better than a random walk. The exchange rate thereby became the field of macroeconomics hardest to converge: the five doctrines of this chapter coexist to this day rather than merging into one standard model the way IS-LM did.
- **The "economic analysis" of regime choice has been revised by crisis experience.** Heller's list answers "which countries tend to peg"; what the years after 1990 exposed was that "intermediate regimes cannot stand": the 1992 pound, the 1994 peso and the 1997 baht were all pegs attacked by speculation once capital accounts were opened. Calvo and Reinhart's (2002) "fear of floating" found that countries nominally floating still managed their rates in practice, and Fischer (2001) and others' "bipolarity thesis" predicted intermediate arrangements would disappear. The IMF's statistical categories also adjusted to reality: in 1999 the original three broad classes were subdivided into eight, in 2009 the classification switched to five "de facto arrangements" divided by actual behaviour, and in 2016 it was consolidated again into groups such as hard pegs, soft pegs, other managed arrangements and free floating - the classification itself reads ever more like a compromise with reality. China's arrangement took another path: after the July 2005 exchange rate reform it became "a managed floating exchange rate regime based on market supply and demand and adjusted with reference to a basket of currencies", with greater flexibility and the central parity mechanism layered in step by step - precisely the real-world version of this chapter's line that a regime "must be both fixed and floating, and is fairly difficult to operate".
