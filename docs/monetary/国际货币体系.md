# Chapter 9. The international monetary system

> **The core question of this chapter**: how has the international monetary system evolved? Why did the Bretton Woods system collapse? What are the features of today's international monetary system?

## The arc of this chapter

The international monetary system has to answer three questions at once: what serves as the international means of payment (liquidity), how the exchange rate is set (parity), and who adjusts when balances get out of line (adjustment). The gold standard of 1880-1914 was the first spontaneous answer - gold supply, mint parity and Hume's price-specie flow mechanism solved the three questions in one stroke, but it pressed all the adjustment burden onto domestic prices and employment, and the outbreak of war in 1914 broke it off. Between the wars the attempts of individual currencies to restore the gold standard one by one (the 1922 Genoa conference, countries returning to pre-war parity) failed one after another, and the beggar-thy-neighbour competitive devaluations spread the Great Depression across the world - precisely the outcome the 44 countries meeting at Bretton Woods in July 1944 wanted to avoid: after the haggling between the Keynes plan and the White plan, the "double linkage" tied gold to the dollar, and adjustable pegs plus IMF lending and supervision replaced spontaneous market discipline. Triffin (1960) saw the contradiction built into the system at exactly this point; Nixon closed the gold window on 15 August 1971, and the Smithsonian Agreement, which raised parities and widened the bands, only bought time; after the major currencies turned floating in 1973, the Jamaica Agreement reached at the Kingston meeting of January 1976 (effective April 1978) ratified the accomplished facts: the legalisation of floating, the demonetisation of gold, and an expansion of SDRs and quotas. So the "system without a system" of this chapter's last section has been the reality since 1976, and the 1997-98 crisis, the 2008 crisis and the renminbi's entry into the SDR basket in 2016 are all attempts to seek order anew within that systemless system.

## Core concepts

### The international monetary system

The international monetary system refers to the monetary arrangements among nations.
Specifically, the international monetary system includes the following three aspects of content:
- The international exchange rate system
- The balance of payments and international reserve assets
- The coordination of national economic policies with international economic policies

An ideal international financial system should be able to safeguard the development of international trade and the stability and prosperity of the world economy. Since its formation the international monetary system has passed in succession through the international gold standard system, the Bretton Woods system and the Jamaica system.

These three items correspond in fact to the three links of an international transaction: at what relative price to exchange (the exchange rate), what to pay with (reserve assets), and what to do when the balance does not square afterwards (policy coordination). Every change of system in history came about because at least one of these three links gave way first: the gold standard was destroyed by reserves (gold could not keep up with the expansion of trade) and by coordination (wartime freezes on each side), Bretton Woods by the exchange rate (rigid parities) and reserves (an excess of dollars), and the Jamaica system loosened all three at once, leaving capital flows and crises to fill the gap.

### The Triffin dilemma

The Triffin dilemma refers to the contradiction, under the Bretton Woods system, between the two responsibilities the dollar bore - guaranteeing the convertibility of dollars into gold at the official price and maintaining other countries' confidence in the dollar, on the one hand, and providing sufficient international liquidity (that is, dollars) on the other.
It was pointed out by the American economist Triffin in 1960, and is therefore called the "Triffin dilemma". Under the Bretton Woods system, to meet the needs of world economic growth international reserves had to grow correspondingly, and this could happen only through balance of payments deficits of the reserve currency supplier - the United States. But the more dollars other countries held, the less confidence they had in the dollar-gold convertibility relation, and the more they wanted to convert dollars into gold; this contradiction, known as the "Triffin dilemma", finally made the Bretton Woods system impossible to sustain.

It must be stressed that this is a dilemma and not an oversight: the more world trade expands, the more the US needs to export dollars (otherwise global liquidity falls short); the more dollars are exported, the thinner America's gold grows relative to them, and the less credible the conversion promise. The source of confidence (dollar scarcity) and the source of liquidity (dollar abundance) point in opposite directions, and the running of the system itself undermines the system's foundation. When Triffin raised the question in 1960, US gold reserves no longer covered the dollars held by foreign official holders; Nixon's closing of the gold window in 1971 was the first answer to this question - not persuaded, but run upon. The same structure replayed itself in the "dollar squeezes" of 2008 and March 2020: the world relies on the dollar for collateral and settlement, while the supply of dollars is decided by America's own domestic policies.

### The Bretton Woods system

The Bretton Woods system refers to the international monetary system after the Second World War whose basic feature was the fixed exchange rate system.
The main content of the Bretton Woods system was:
- Establishing a dollar-centred exchange rate parity system (the double linkage mechanism), that is the dollar pegged to gold at 35 dollars per ounce, and other currencies pegged to the dollar;
- The dollar occupying the central position in international reserves and official settlement, with foreign monetary authorities able to convert dollars into gold at the official price at the official level;
- Establishing a permanent international financial institution, the International Monetary Fund;
- Adjusting balance of payments disequilibrium through multiple channels.
Because of the "Triffin dilemma", the rigidity of the exchange rate system and the IMF's limited capacity to coordinate the resolution of balance of payments disequilibrium, on 15 August 1971 the US president Nixon announced the suspension of the official convertibility of the dollar into gold, terminating the official conversion relation of 35 dollars per ounce. With that, the fixed exchange rate system of Bretton Woods was declared bankrupt.

Why the arrangement grew into the shape of a "double linkage" deserves a further word. The post-war consensus was that exchange rates had to be stable, but there was not enough gold: in 1945 the United States held roughly two-thirds of the capitalist world's gold reserves, and having each country restore the gold standard on its own was neither realistic (it would reproduce pre-war deflationary pressure) nor necessary. So the designers put gold in the background and let the dollar circulate in front as "a certificate for gold" - this solved liquidity, but it also shifted the credit of the system onto the promise and policy of the United States alone. Keynes had proposed in 1941-44 to avoid this single point of dependence with a supernational unit of account, the "bancor", and a clearing union, but Britain's inability to service its debt left his plan beaten by the White plan at Bretton Woods; nearly every argument about the international monetary system in the sixty years since has been a continuation of that defeat.

## Problems and analysis

### What makes the international gold standard run automatically?

The gold standard is a monetary system that takes gold of a certain fineness and weight as the standard money, with gold as the foundation of the monetary system. Under the international gold standard, gold fully exercised the function of world money. It is generally held that the 35 years from 1880 to 1914 were the golden age of the international gold standard system. The main features of the international gold standard were:

- (1) Gold served as the international currency

Under the gold standard, gold coins could be freely minted and freely converted, and gold moved across borders freely. Because gold coins could be freely minted, the face value of a coin could stay in line with its gold content and the quantity of coins could spontaneously meet the needs of circulation; because gold coins could be freely converted, the various metal subsidiary coins and banknotes could circulate stably as representing a definite quantity of gold, keeping the value of money stable; and because gold could move across borders freely, the exchange rate of the domestic currency could stay stable.

The international gold standard system nominally required gold to serve as the international currency, but since transporting gold was inconvenient and risky, gold bore no interest and custody fees had to be paid, and Britain at that time occupied an absolutely dominant position in international finance and trade, people usually substituted sterling for gold, with sterling playing the role of the international currency.

- (2) A strict fixed exchange rate system

Under the gold standard, the exchange rate between the currencies of different countries was decided by the ratio of their respective gold contents - gold parity. The exchange rate was of course not exactly equal to mint parity; influenced by supply and demand, it fluctuated narrowly around mint parity within a band no wider than the gold points between the two countries, otherwise gold would replace money in moving between them. In fact the exchange rate parities of the main countries - Britain, the United States, France and Germany - did not move at all from 1880 to 1914, never appreciating or depreciating.

- (3) The automatic adjustment mechanism of the balance of payments

Its mechanism is the "price-specie flow mechanism" proposed by the British philosopher and economist David Hume in *Of the Balance of Trade* (1752):

$$\text{A balance of payments deficit}→\text{gold flows out}→\text{the money supply shrinks}→\text{prices and costs fall}→\text{export competitiveness strengthens}→\text{exports expand and imports fall}→\text{the balance of payments turns to surplus}→\text{gold flows in}$$

The reverse direction is:

$$\text{A balance of payments surplus}→\text{gold flows in}→\text{the money supply grows}→\text{prices and costs rise}→\text{export competitiveness weakens}→\text{imports rise and exports fall}→\text{the balance of payments turns to deficit}→\text{gold flows out}$$

The subtlety of this mechanism is that it requires no act of government: gold flows automatically change the domestic money stock, the money stock automatically changes prices, prices automatically change competitiveness, and so a deficit corrects itself. And for the same reason its cost likewise needs no government approval - the whole adjustment is absorbed by domestic prices and employment. For the automatic adjustment mechanism above to work, every country had to observe three rules strictly:
- ① The domestic currency was fixed to a definite quantity of gold and could be converted into gold at any time;
- ② Gold could be imported and exported freely, and the financial authorities of each country had to buy and sell gold and foreign exchange without limit at the official parity at all times;
- ③ Money issue had to be backed by corresponding gold reserves.

But in actual operation these three conditions were not executed by the various countries exactly as written, and so the gold standard's automatic adjustment mechanism never did solve the countries' balance of payments disequilibria.

### How is the balance of payments adjusted under the Jamaica system?

The Jamaica system refers to the new international monetary system formed after the collapse of the Bretton Woods system on the basis of the Jamaica Agreement reached by the IMF's "Interim Committee on the International Monetary System" in 1976. The basic content of the Jamaica system includes:
- The legalisation of floating exchange rates
- The demonetisation of gold
- Strengthening the role of SDRs
- Increasing IMF liquidity
- Expanding financing

Under the Jamaica system the main channels for adjusting the balance of payments are the following five:

- (1) The use of domestic economic policy.
	The balance of payments is one of the goals of macroeconomic management and a main content of governments' macroeconomic management. Governments can, through the coordinated use of macro policies such as fiscal and monetary policy, adjust the economic variables that influence the balance of payments and so eliminate disequilibrium. In applying domestic economic policy attention should be paid to internal-external equilibrium: when the domestic economic goal conflicts with external equilibrium, internal policy and exchange rate policy should be used in coordination.

- (2) Exchange rate policy.
	Through the policy of devaluing or revaluing the domestic currency, together with the choice of exchange rate regime, one influences the receipts and payments of the current and capital accounts and so moves the balance of payments toward equilibrium. But in actual application exchange rate policy is often constrained by the Robinson-Lerner condition, so it must be supplemented by administrative means such as exchange control or trade control.

- (3) Balancing the balance of payments through international financing.
	Short-run balance of payments disequilibrium can be dealt with by obtaining financing from international and regional monetary organisations and by raising funds in international financial markets.

- (4) Strengthening international coordination.
	Countries can, through channels such as the International Monetary Fund, the summits of the G7 and various worldwide and regional political and economic organisations and regular coordination meetings, through intergovernmental consultation and communication, jointly adopt effective measures to intervene in and influence international markets and the economic policies of the various countries, so as to remove the adverse effects of balance of payments disequilibrium and promote equilibrium in all countries' balances of payments.

- (5) Adjustment through the increase or reduction of foreign exchange reserves.
	A country's surpluses and deficits of foreign exchange reserves affect the structure of its money supply and can lead to balance of payments disequilibrium. Countries can therefore, through increasing or reducing foreign exchange reserves and adopting sterilisation policies, change the structure of the money supply correspondingly without changing its volume, so as to adjust the balance of payments.

## Synthetic essay

### The gold standard and Bretton Woods both ran fixed exchange rates: where are they the same and where do they differ?

- (1) The mechanism fixing the exchange rate under the two systems
- ① Under the gold standard, since gold coins could be freely minted and converted and gold could be carried across borders freely, the exchange rate between the currencies of different countries was decided by the ratio of their gold contents - gold parity; the rate was therefore a fixed one, fluctuating narrowly around gold parity within a band no wider than the gold points between the two countries, while the balance of payments adjusted automatically.
- ② Under the Bretton Woods system what was established was a parity system centred on the dollar under the double linkage mechanism. The dollar's link to gold (an ounce of gold = 35 dollars) and other currencies' link to the dollar were the two pillars of the Bretton Woods system. Foreign monetary authorities could convert dollars into gold at the official price at the official level. Once the legal parity of a country's currency against the dollar was confirmed by the International Monetary Fund (IMF) it could not be changed, and its band of fluctuation could not exceed ±1% of parity. Only when a member's fundamental balance of payments was out of equilibrium could the parity be changed with IMF approval.

- (2) The similarities between the two fixed exchange rate systems, under Bretton Woods and under the gold standard
- ① Every country set a gold parity for its own currency, and central rates were determined on the basis of gold parity.
- ② Market exchange rates were relatively stable, fluctuating around the central rate within very small limits.

- (3) The differences between the two fixed exchange rate systems
	The fixed rate under the gold standard was the typical fixed rate, while the fixed rate under Bretton Woods, strictly speaking, can only be called an adjustable pegged rate system. Specifically:
- ① The fixed rate under the gold standard formed and adjusted spontaneously; the fixed rate under Bretton Woods was established artificially - the authorities set the central rate by stipulating a nominal gold parity and kept the rate fluctuating within an artificially prescribed narrow band through policy measures such as foreign exchange intervention and controls.
- ② Under the gold standard the gold parities of the currencies never changed, so exchange rates between countries could stay genuinely stable; under Bretton Woods the gold parities were adjustable - when a country's balance of payments showed fundamental disequilibrium, the parity could be changed with the approval of the International Monetary Fund.

### Where is today's international financial system fragile, and in which directions does reform concentrate?

The existing international financial system dates from the Smithsonian Agreement of December 1971, whose core is an international monetary arrangement based mainly on floating exchange rates. To remove excessive economic fluctuation the International Monetary Fund continues to act as coordinator of the economic policies of the various countries and as "lender of last resort" in time of crisis. Under this "system without a system" exchange rates fluctuate violently, capital flows grow ever more frequent and financial crises recur, exposing the fragility of the existing international financial system and the necessity of reform.

- (1) The fragility of the international financial system concentrates in the following respects:

- ① The fragility of exchange rate regimes
	After the Jamaica system was established, on the surface most countries replaced fixed with floating rates. In fact only a small number of developed countries truly float; most developing countries, because of their lagging and dependent economic development, usually peg their currencies to international currencies such as the dollar. When major currency rates stay basically stable this hybrid international exchange rate arrangement can run fairly smoothly, but when major currency rates swing sharply a series of problems follows, directly causing competitive adjustment and economic imbalance in some developing countries.
		- a. A pegged exchange rate regime reduces the capacity of developing countries to manage their economies. In theory the policy instruments available to a government are fiscal policy, monetary policy and exchange rate policy, but under a pegged regime the exchange rate, which should be an instrument, becomes instead the target of policy; and since developing countries commonly suffer from inherent problems such as imperfect domestic financial markets and market participants lacking market awareness, instruments such as interest rates and money supply and demand work poorly, and it is hard to achieve internal and external equilibrium at the same time. Very often developing countries must sacrifice internal equilibrium to some degree in exchange for external equilibrium, and may even adopt policies that run directly counter to the requirements of their own economic development.
		- b. A relatively stable exchange rate level leads developing countries to accumulate large risks in the course of using foreign capital. Under a pegged regime the government has the obligation to maintain the fixed rate, which in effect amounts to the government guaranteeing the exchange rate risk of domestic and foreign investors. High growth from low-cost labour, together with the high interest rates that had to be maintained to defend an overvalued rate, attracted large volumes of international capital. Lacking risk constraints, domestic investors used foreign capital blindly and inefficiently. Mismatches of maturity and currency planted hidden risks for developing countries.
		- c. In theory a pegged exchange rate regime increases the likelihood that developing countries suffer speculative attack. Under a pegged regime the level of the rate cannot reflect the real purchasing power of the domestic currency, and the tendency of the rate to float is artificially suppressed. Once a speculative attack happens, the central bank defending the rate faces severe information asymmetry in its contest with international speculative capital: in market trading the central bank maintains a relatively fixed public target, while international speculative forces can strike at any possible opportunity.

- ② The instability of a diversified currency reserve system
- a. The adjustment of reserve structures by the various monetary authorities aggravates the instability of international financial markets. International financial markets change by the minute, and the risks and returns of reserve currencies shift constantly, forcing the monetary authorities of the various countries to adjust the composition of their reserves with them. But these conversions among reserve currencies themselves cause and aggravate violent fluctuations of exchange rates in the foreign exchange market. Not to mention the huge hot money of trillions of dollars a day, itself seeking higher interest and speculative gains in foreign exchange, which also flows frequently among reserve currencies and fans the flames of exchange market volatility.
- b. The diversification of the reserve system aggravates developing countries' dependence on developed countries. Each reserve currency issuer sets monetary policy according to its own domestic economic conditions, which necessarily affects the value and stability of developing countries' foreign exchange reserves. Developing countries can only passively accept the monetary policies of developed countries. Those that peg to a particular reserve currency suffer most of all.

- ③ The International Monetary Fund lacks independence and authority

Under the present monetary system the highest body for the coordination and management of global economic policy is still the International Monetary Fund. The Fund's lack of independence means it cannot defend global financial security.
- a. The IMF cannot discover hidden dangers of crisis through policy surveillance. Today, with globalisation advancing further, international capital markets swelling and financial derivatives developing rapidly into high-risk investment instruments, the IMF's traditional surveillance mechanism is clearly unequal to its task: far from foreseeing the latent possibility of crisis, it often causes misdirection.
- b. The IMF cannot reduce the destructive impact of crises through the exercise of its lender-of-last-resort function. As the supervisor of the global multilateral system of free payments, the IMF has the duty to provide financial assistance to members when they lose liquidity and solvency, and in fact it acts as the international lender of last resort. Yet the IMF's performance in the Asian financial crisis proved that this lender-of-last-resort function failed to work effectively.

- (2) Since the Asian financial crisis the various defects of the existing dollar-centred international financial system have gradually been exposed, and from this the effort to reform it has unfolded. The reform concentrates on two levels, international and regional:

- ① Reform of the International Monetary Fund

- a. Expanding financial resources. The IMF's funds are already seriously insufficient. During the Asian currency crisis the Fund showed itself stretched thin, its strength very limited. At present the main countries have reached agreement, and the plan to add 90 billion US dollars of new fund quotas has been settled.
- b. Strengthening the exchange and publication of information and pressing members to disclose information. The IMF has proposed further extending the Special Data Dissemination Standard and building an early warning system for balance of payments crises, with the aim of strengthening members' disclosure of economic information. Since 1998 the IMF has also put forward reports on the *Code of Good Practices on Fiscal Transparency* and the *Code of Good Practices on Transparency in Monetary and Financial Policies*, to carry out the work of getting members to open information and improve policy transparency, and it has set members stricter requirements of supervision and disclosure.
- c. Reforming the conditions attached to financial assistance. The IMF has relaxed the scope of policy commitments required of developing countries: it no longer stresses contractionary fiscal and monetary policies when aiding them, and policy guidance has turned to structural adjustment and the reorganisation of financial institutions.

- ② Building regional cooperation mechanisms

Regional cooperation mechanisms supplement the International Monetary Fund; their purpose is to safeguard the economic and financial security of the countries of a region and ensure stable economic development.
Through the following aspects regional cooperation can accomplish a great deal in crisis management:

- a. Cooperation in exchange rate regimes.
	Whether by jointly pegging to a basket of currencies or by creating a common currency, one can greatly increase the region's immunity. By weakening the link between the region's currencies and the dollar or any other single international currency, the frequency and amplitude of exchange rate fluctuation fall, the stability of exchange rates strengthens, the channels by which developed countries spread recession or inflation to developing countries through money narrow, the domestic economy is stabilised, and the strength to resist external attack grows.

- b. Regional surveillance mechanisms.
	By tracking and supervising members' economic development, economic structures, financial markets and economic policies over the long run, and conducting investigations and studies, hidden dangers of crisis can be found in good time. On this basis a regional consultation mechanism can be established to exchange information on macroeconomies, economic policies and financial markets regularly and irregularly. By applying pressure on members it can stop them from adopting policies likely to lead to crisis and overcome economic and financial fragility.

- c. A deterrent mechanism. It can strengthen the region's overall image, raise ordinary investors' confidence, keep capital markets stable and avoid the recurrence of crisis.

- d. Mechanisms of crisis prevention and mutual assistance. By providing the country in crisis with necessary international liquidity, it both wins that country time to take the measures needed to correct policy errors and cuts off the channel through which it might spread the crisis to other countries.

## Limits and what follows

- **The Triffin dilemma did not vanish with gold's departure; it changed form.** After the Jamaica system removed the obligation of convertibility, international liquidity still depended on the dollar: the dollar has long made up more than half of the IMF's official reserve currency composition. The "dollar squeezes" of 2008 and March 2020 replayed the same structure - the world uses dollars for collateral and settlement while the dollar supply is decided by US domestic policy. The renminbi's entry into the SDR basket in October 2016 (initial weight 10.92%) was a substantive step in the diversification of reserves, but it did not change this basic pattern.
- **Two of the Jamaica Agreement's hopes proved empty.** One was "strengthening the role of SDRs": SDRs are still only a few percent of global official reserves and have never come close to the status of a main reserve asset. The other was "demonetisation of gold": after the 1978 agreement removed gold's official pricing function, the central banks' annual gold purchases in 2022 and 2023 each exceeded 1000 tonnes, the highest level since the late 1960s. A third expectation failed likewise - floating rates did not automatically correct imbalances: through the 2000s US current account deficits coexisted for years with the reserve accumulation of surplus countries, and adjustment did not happen the way the textbook says.
- **The "golden age" of the gold standard needs a footnote in reverse, and the reform section needs updating in time.** Eichengreen's *Golden Fetters* (1992) showed with cross-country data that the severity of the Great Depression of the 1930s can almost be explained by how early or late a country left the gold standard - the earlier a country escaped the gold constraint the earlier it recovered, whereas the real cost of the automatic adjustment mechanism this chapter describes is to turn the whole adjustment burden into deflation and unemployment. As for the reform section, the state of play when this chapter was written has since been overtaken: the 2010 IMF quota reform did not take effect until March 2016, China's quota and voting power rose above 6% into the front rank, but the 85% supermajority threshold leaves the US holding a de facto veto over major decisions; after 2008 the coordination platform was upgraded from the G7 to the G20, and the Financial Stability Board (FSB, 2009) and the Chiang Mai Initiative Multilateralisation (launched 2010, doubled 2012) of 240 billion U.S. dollars filled in, respectively, the two jigsaw pieces of global surveillance and regional mutual assistance. The "regional cooperation mechanisms" this chapter speaks of are now institutions, not ideas.
