# Chapter 5. Financial markets

> **The core question of this chapter**: What types and functions do financial markets have? How do the money market and the capital market differ? How do financial instruments innovate? How are risk and return priced?

## The arc of this chapter

The markets and instruments of this chapter were built in batches, and almost every batch corresponds to a concrete trouble. The prototype of the exchange securities market was the concentrated trading in Amsterdam of the shares of the Dutch East India Company (VOC), founded in 1602 — the problem was not that firms lacked money, but that merchants who had subscribed to voyages of exploration could not get out, so there was a need for a place where illiquid equity could become transferable certificates; London's origin was cruder (in 1792 twenty-four brokers signed a pact under a buttonwood tree on Wall Street, because the official Philadelphia market refused to let them trade government bonds), but it too was born to solve the problem of "no counterparty" for government bonds and bank shares. The futures and options branch comes from agricultural products: the seasonality of grain production and price volatility made budgeting hard on both the trader's and the processor's side, Japan's eighteenth-century Dojima rice market already had officially certified futures contracts and "long and short" trading, the Chicago Board of Trade (CBOT) was established in 1848, and in 1865 standardised contracts were introduced with a matching margin and settlement system — it is these two institutional inventions, "standardisation + central counterparty," that constitute the real difference between futures and forwards. After the collapse of the Bretton Woods system and the take-off of inflation in the 1970s the remaining instruments appeared in a cluster: in 1972 the International Monetary Market of the Chicago Mercantile Exchange launched currency futures (exchange rates were no longer fixed, and firms needed to buy insurance systematically for the first time), interest rate futures were listed 1974–1977 (interest rate volatility being analogous), in April 1973 the Chicago Board Options Exchange began trading transferable options and that same year Black and Scholes gave the pricing formula, and in 1981 Salomon Brothers arranged the first widely known currency swap between the World Bank and IBM (both sides wanted to get round the market regulation they were each subject to and to cash in comparative advantage) — the four derivatives among this chapter's core concepts, arranged chronologically, run exactly "exchange rate volatility → interest rate volatility → non-linear risk → cashing in comparative advantage". Asset pricing theory was built up in the same period: Markowitz (1952) wrote risk as variance, Modigliani and Miller (1958) gave the no-arbitrage method, Sharpe (1964), Lintner (1965) and Mossin (1966) produced the CAPM, and Fama (1970) assembled the efficient market hypothesis — these five things explain the whole structure of this chapter's four questions in "Problems and analysis". China's path is a checklist of alternating market building and regulation: treasury bonds resumed in 1981, the Shanghai and Shenzhen stock exchanges opened in turn in 1990, concentrated supervision was established in 1992–1993, the national interbank lending market in 1996 and the interbank bond market in June 1997 (commercial banks were required to withdraw from exchange repos) were founded, the futures exchanges were cleaned up and merged into three in 1999–2000, the shareholding structure reform came in 2005, the Shibor quotation in 2007, ChiNext in 2009, CSI 300 stock index futures in 2010, the STAR Market and the registration-system pilot in 2019, the Beijing Stock Exchange in 2021, and full share registration in February 2023. Read the two lines together and the point of the chapter is not to memorise what the markets are called, but to see the conditions under which each kind of instrument can stand: a transferable price, a mechanism for handling default, and a counterparty willing to bear risk — miss one of the three and the instrument is only a concept on paper.

## Core concepts

### Financial instruments

A financial instrument, also called a financial asset, is a written document that certifies the amount, maturity and price of a financial transaction, and is legally binding on the rights and obligations of both creditor and debtor.
A financial instrument generally has the basic features of liquidity, risk and return.
Liquidity is the ability of a financial instrument to be converted into cash quickly without incurring a loss;
risk is the possibility that, under the influence of credit risk and market risk, the purchaser of the financial instrument suffers an economic loss;
and return is the ratio of the income obtained from holding the financial instrument to the principal.

The phrase "written document" needs updating today: the great majority of financial instruments long ago ceased to exist on paper, and their substance is a **transferable contractual right with legal enforcement**. This decides the three entry points of financial instrument analysis. First, whether something can be transferred is determined by law and by the registration and settlement system, not by technology — whether the same receivable can be split and sold depends on whether there is a genuine sale with bankruptcy remoteness (see asset securitisation in this chapter, chapter 4). Second, liquidity and risk are not mutually independent attributes: what is meant by "converting to cash quickly without loss" presupposes that the market continuously has two-way quotes, and it is precisely the quotes that disappear first in a panic. On 16 September 2008 the Reserve Primary Fund, holding Lehman Brothers commercial paper, saw its net value per share fall to 0.97 dollars (breaking below the 1 dollar par value from 16 September 2008), and the American commercial paper market froze that week — a textbook case of an "almost cash" asset suddenly losing its liquidity; the same logic appears in China as the interbank and non-standard assets of the June 2013 "liquidity crunch" and the redemption wave in wealth management products during the November 2022 bond adjustment. Third, a return is informative only when stated together with risk — this is the whole reason for the fourth section of this chapter (risk and return) and for the CAPM. As for the two terms "financial instrument" and "financial asset": the former emphasises instrumentality and contract structure (the issuer's perspective), the latter is the accounting and regulatory classification of a held asset (the holder's perspective); textbooks use them interchangeably, so switch by context as you read.

### The money market

The money market is the market for short-term financial accommodation of one year and less, and includes sub-markets such as the interbank lending market, the interbank bond market, the negotiable certificate of deposit market, the commercial paper market and the treasury bill market. The instruments of this market have very short maturities and approximate money, which is why it is called the money market. The money market is an important venue where financial institutions adjust liquidity, and the foundation of the central bank's monetary policy operations.

These four words "approximating money" are the key to understanding where this chapter joins the earlier ones: what participants in the money market borrow is not funds but **the surpluses and shortfalls of reserves and cash**, so the interest rate of this market is the one the central bank can manipulate directly (chapter 2's benchmark rate and chapter 7's open market operations both land here). For exactly this reason it is extremely sensitive to credit quality: treasury bills and interbank certificates of deposit usually have a very small spread, but on 16–17 September 2019 American repo rates jumped overnight from 2% to about 10% intraday (tax deadlines, government bond settlements and banks cutting back on repos under new regulation together draining reserves), which shows that this "almost money" channel has to be kept open by ample reserves; the across-the-board spike in dollar money market rates in March 2020, when the Federal Reserve had to intervene with unlimited repos and the Money Market Mutual Fund Liquidity Facility (MMLF), is the extreme version of the same thing. China's interbank lending and repo markets have formed the practical carrier of the policy rate since 1996 (the lending centre) and 1997 (the interbank bond market), and DR007 (the weighted seven-day repo rate pledged by depository institutions against interest-rate bonds) has become the first-choice indicator for watching funding conditions, which is exactly the landing of this "foundation of central bank operations" positioning. What needs qualification is the classification of the "interbank bond market": this market deals in both seven-day repos and thirty-year government bonds, so by maturity it straddles the two concepts of this chapter at once, and including it wholesale in the money market is by reference to its function as a "liquidity management venue".

### The capital market

The capital market generally refers to the market in which the transaction maturity is over one year, comprising mainly the stock market and the bond market, and meeting the medium- and long-term investment needs of industrial and commercial enterprises and the needs of government fiscal deficits.
Because the transaction maturity is long and the funds are used mainly to form real capital, it is called the capital market.

The emphasis of this definition lies in the second half: what the capital market deals with is **promises reaching across an uncertain future**, so its price is not a "price of funds" but a "judgement about the future". This brings two features that must be distinguished from the money market. First, the amplitude of price fluctuation is large and cannot be eliminated by maturity — a share has no maturity date, and any change in its discount rate or in growth expectations goes straight into the price, which is why chapter 8's exchange rate theory and this chapter's asset pricing share the logic of "expectations dominating"; second, the market's survival depends on two-way flow of issuance and exit, and if there is financing without return and clearance, valuations detach from cash flows. The reform history of China's capital market has revolved around this point: when the exchanges were set up in the early 1990s they were positioned as a financing channel to relieve state-owned enterprises, the 2005 shareholding structure reform solved the structural defect of "different rights for the same share, with non-tradable shares in the majority," margin trading and off-exchange financing amplified trading in 2013–2015, the STAR Market in 2019 and full registration in 2023 moved pricing power from examination to disclosure and market-based book-building, while the long-weak delisting system remains the unclosed link. Another contemporary change is that the "over one year" boundary is being blurred by policy operations themselves: from 2024 the People's Bank of China has brought government bond trading in the open market under its liquidity adjustment tools, so that medium- and long-term bonds now also carry the role of a money market instrument — one more instance that the line between the two concepts of this chapter can move.

### Forwards

A forward is an agreement to buy or sell a definite quantity of some asset at a definite price at some definite time in the future.
In a forward contract, the asset the parties agree to trade is called the underlying asset, the agreed price the contract price; the party agreeing to sell the underlying asset at the agreed price is called the short, and the party agreeing to buy it the long. Forward contracts are used very widely in the foreign exchange market, because they can prevent exchange rate risk comparatively effectively.

The difference between forwards and futures (the next concept) is often told as "over-the-counter versus on-exchange," but the real cost lies elsewhere: a forward is a bilateral credit contract, so its price carries, besides the risk of the underlying asset, the counterparty's default risk and the collateral arrangement. This is why this seemingly primitive instrument has not yet been replaced by standardised products — what a firm needs is an amount and a date that fit its own cash flow exactly, whereas futures offer only integer multiples of a standard lot. Chinese firms will find that the very availability of forwards is shaped by regulation: the renminbi foreign exchange forward was launched after the interbank foreign exchange market was established in 1997, the foreign exchange swap followed in the same period after the July 2005 exchange rate reform, and the renminbi option on foreign exchange started in 2010, while participants have long been limited to institutional clients and interbank market members, so that corporate hedging must pass through the bank layer, and the quote therefore includes the bank's position costs and capital occupation. Only by understanding this can one read the policy meaning behind the multi-ministry push of the 2020s "to lower the cost of corporate exchange rate hedging and extend derivative services to small and medium enterprises": designing a forward is not the hard part, the hard part is getting those willing to sell insurance willing to sell it in small amounts and to a dispersed client base.

### Futures

A futures contract is a standardised contract for buying and selling that developed on the basis of the forward contract.
The main difference between the two is that a futures contract is a standardised contract issued by an exchange, traded centrally on the exchange, and whose performance is guaranteed by the exchange.

The technical content of "the exchange guarantees performance" is daily settlement without outstanding debt (mark-to-market) and central counterparty clearing: the exchange inserts itself between buyer and seller to become "the seller to all buyers and the buyer to all sellers," transferring margin daily at the settlement price, and the losing side must top up, failing which it is forcibly liquidated. This mechanism (its prototype being the CBOT's 1865 design) concentrates default risk from the counterparty into the clearing house, and at the same time brings two consequences that the analysis later in this chapter cannot avoid. First, leverage is made explicit: only a few percentage points of margin are needed to hold a notional position, so a small adverse price move can wipe out equity entirely — the February 1995 "327 Treasury Bond Futures" affair exploited precisely the basis between spot and futures along with market dominance, and under the margin system amplified the losses, ultimately causing Treasury bond futures to be suspended for nearly eighteen years (not restarted until March 2015); the regulatory tone of China's futures market thereafter (position limits, reporting on large accounts, price limits) was all shaped by this history. Second, the risk is not gone but transferred: when short-term violent price swings make the margin call itself a selling pressure, the clearing mechanism rebounds on its holders. The September–October 2022 liability-driven investment (LDI) crisis of British pension funds is the latest instance — the interest rate swaps and futures held to hedge rising rates were required to post billions of pounds of additional margin as long-end yields shot up, forcing the sale of long government bonds, pushing yields up further, a loop that ran until the Bank of England came in to buy bonds. Futures and swaps make "hedging" valid for an individual position, but at the system level they create a new dependence on liquidity.

### Swaps

A swap is a transaction in which the two swap parties reach an agreement and, within a given period, convert with each other their currencies, the basis of borrowing interest rates and other assets.
Swap agreements are mainly of two kinds, currency swaps and interest rate swaps. A currency swap is one in which the two parties exchange two currencies at a given exchange rate and agree to buy back their original currencies at that rate within a future given period. An interest rate swap is one in which the two parties exchange the interest income (outgoings) on the claims (debts) they hold. The principal values of these two claims (debts) are the same, but the interest payment terms are different, and the exchange satisfies the different needs of both sides.

The reason swaps exploded after 1981 is that what they solve is "cashing in comparative advantage" rather than "price insurance": two borrowers of different credit ratings do not have the same relative financing cost across fixed and floating, and across different currencies, so each borrows in the market where it has the advantage and swaps back the liability it needs, both coming out cheaper than borrowing directly — the 1981 currency swap between the World Bank and IBM was of this kind (the World Bank wanted low-cost funding in francs and marks, IBM needed to convert the fixed rate it had borrowed outside the United States into a floating one). A reminder is in order here: the economic meaning of a swap differs from that of forwards and futures alike — it is essentially a bundle of forward contracts, so its risk exposure accumulates over time rather than being settled day by day. Before 2008 over-the-counter swaps had almost no central counterparty, no margin, and uncontrolled notional principal (by Bank for International Settlements statistics, the notional of global over-the-counter interest rate derivatives had already reached about 300 trillion dollars by mid-2007); the Financial Products division of American International Group (AIG) needed government rescue because the margin call on credit default swaps was triggered in September 2008, which was precisely the cost of that structure. Post-crisis reform rewrote this concept of the chapter directly: at the September 2009 G20 Pittsburgh summit it was pledged that standardised interest rate swaps would be cleared through a central counterparty, and the Dodd–Frank Act (Title VII) in the United States and the European Market Infrastructure Regulation (EMIR) in the European Union successively built up central clearing, margin and trade reporting systems. China's renminbi interest rate swap started in the interbank market in 2006 (no margin required, contracted by inquiry and master agreement), and central clearing pilots were introduced gradually after 2014, while the development of its credit derivatives and swap markets lagged noticeably behind the contemporaneous expansion of the bond stock — one reason why the debt risk of local governments and property firms was hard to price and disperse. Incidentally, on the LIBOR used in the examples of this section: that quotation benchmark was abolished because of manipulation scandals after 2008 and the shrinkage of the lending market (phased out from 2021 to 2023), and the dollar switched to the Secured Overnight Financing Rate (SOFR, published from April 2018) and other new benchmarks, so that the reference rates on the floating legs of this chapter's instruments were reset accordingly — the largest technical restructuring of swap and futures pricing in recent years.

### Options

An option contract grants its holder (that is, the purchaser of the option) a right, enabling him to buy (a call option) or sell (a put option) from the seller of the option contract a definite quantity of assets at an agreed price within a future agreed period (but without obligation to do so).
To obtain this right the holder of the contract must pay a certain option premium.

The option is the only one of this chapter's five instruments whose "rights and obligations are asymmetric," and for that reason it is also the hardest to handle conceptually: the gains and losses of the two sides of forwards, futures and swaps are symmetric, whereas the option buyer exchanges a limited cost (the premium) for a right-tail or left-tail distribution of returns. To price it one cannot rely on "supply and demand" but must rely on replication — construct a portfolio with the underlying asset and with borrowing whose payoffs are identical to the option's in every state, and the cost of that portfolio is the option price (the concretisation of the no-arbitrage logic; see "The methodological background of financial engineering" in this chapter). Black and Scholes (1973, with Merton giving the rigorous version the same year) derived the continuous-time pricing formula along precisely this line of thought, its premises being that the underlying price follows a geometric Brownian motion, that there is no friction, and that continuous rebalancing is possible; it is the divergence of these four conditions from reality that produces the later "implied volatility smile" (implied volatilities at the same expiry but different strike prices are not identical, a phenomenon present for a long time after the October 1987 American stock crash) and skewness risk, and it is also why volatility itself became a tradeable asset class. At the institutional level there is one more distinction worth remembering: an American option can be exercised at any time before expiry while a European one only on the expiry date; index options are mostly European and single-stock options mostly American; China's SSE 50 ETF option (February 2015, the first on-exchange equity option) is European, while commodity options (soybean meal and corn from 2019) are American — and both spreads and margin treatment differ accordingly. For policy discussion, the option gives a perspective no other instrument offers: it puts a price directly on "how large the uncertainty is," which is why the implied volatility of the CSI 300 index options is often used as a reading of the degree of market panic, and together with chapter 3's inflation expectation surveys and chapter 8's exchange rate option risk reversal it forms three routes to "making expectations observable".

## Problems and analysis

### How does venture capital put money into a firm that has no profit yet?

Venture capital refers to the capital operated by venture capital funds, and the investment process of venture capital is roughly divided into five steps:

- (1) Deal origination, that is, the venture capitalist learns of a potential investment opportunity.
- (2) Screening of investment opportunities, that is, making an initial selection of a small number from among many potential investment opportunities for further analysis.
- (3) Evaluation, that is, assessing the potential risk and return of the selected project. If the result of the evaluation is acceptable, the venture capitalist and the entrepreneur proceed together to the fourth step, deal design.
- (4) Deal design. Deal design includes determining the amount, form and price of the investment, among other things. Once the deal is done, one enters the last step — investment management.
- (5) Post-investment management. The key to post-investment management is to bring the firm into the operation of the capital market so that the necessary mergers and acquisitions and the issuance and listing are carried out smoothly.

The two that matter most among the five are (4) and (5), because they are what set venture capital apart from "buy some shares and wait for them to rise." The problem the fourth step has to handle is this: if the judgement cannot be made before investing, then postpone the judgement and write it into the contract — staged investment (funding added at milestones, with the ability to cut losses at any time), convertible preferred shares or convertible bonds (priority repayment on liquidation, conversion into ordinary shares to share the upside after growth), board seats and veto rights (access to information and the right to intervene), and vesting of founders' equity together with non-compete clauses. The fifth step points out a premise often overlooked: the reason this set of contracts can deliver a high return is that an exit channel exists by which book equity can be turned into cash. Steps (1)–(3) are therefore only a matter of allocating screening costs: because the failure rate of projects is extremely high (the industry often quotes a distribution of "two or three out of ten succeed"), venture capital must show power-law characteristics, with a few successful projects covering all costs, which also explains why it demands a high growth rate and an ending of "either being listed or being acquired." Points (4) and (5) taken together explain the structural conclusion of this chapter: venture capital and the capital market are mutually dependent — without a smooth issuance and M&A market, step (5) fails, and however refined the protective clauses of step (4) are, they cannot save the pricing. The US Small Business Investment Company Act of 1958 and the cut in the capital gains tax from 49.5% to 28% in 1978–1979, together with allowing pension funds into venture capital (the 1979 Employee Retirement Income Security Act's interpretive revision of the prudence rule), followed by the two waves driven by Apple's listing in 1981 and by Netscape's after 1995, are all sequences of "exit channel + tax regime" moving first and the scale of investment moving after. China's corresponding chain is: the State Council approved the founding of China's New Technology Venture Capital Company in 1985, indigenous institutions rose after 1993 (such as the Shenzhen Innovation Investment Group, founded in 1999), ChiNext provided an exit outlet in 2009, the STAR Market and registration in 2019 wrote "firms not yet profitable may list" into the rules, and from 2014 government guidance funds (entering their peak of establishment around 2015, and by the mid-2020s already accounting for the main share of primary market fundraising) replaced part of the dollar funds; while the 2018 new asset management rules restricting maturity mismatch and nesting, the tightening of regulation in some sectors after 2021 and the narrowing of the offshore listing channel, compressed step (5) directly, and the resulting "surge in buyback and valuation-adjustment disputes" (the 2012 Haifu case establishing the adjudicative line that "a valuation adjustment with the company is void, one with the shareholder is valid," refined by the 2019 Minutes of the National Court Conference on Civil and Commercial Trials) is precisely the legal reaction to exit being blocked. This whole string of facts and steps (4) and (5) of this chapter are the two faces of the same proposition.

### Must high returns always come with high risk? How is risk quantified?

- (1) Risk and return are a pair of contradictions that any investor must consider in decision-making; risk and return are symmetric, high risk brings high return and low risk brings low return, and all returns have to be adjusted for risk.
	In 1952 Markowitz was the first to describe the relation between risk and return in comparatively precise mathematical language, laying the foundation for asset pricing theory.

- (2) Every investor must form an expectation of return before investing, and so, analysed from a mathematical standpoint, the expectation of return is often expressed as the expected rate of return of the investment.
	Because investment faces future uncertainty, the expected rate of return is the investor's composite estimate, made before investing, of the rate of return under the various uncertain future states. From a mathematical standpoint the expected rate of return is in fact the mathematical expectation of the random variable that is the rate of return, and its formula is: $E(r)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}P(i)$

	Where $E(r)$ is the expected rate of return of an asset; $i=1,2,3,\cdots,n$; $n$ is the number of possible states that may be encountered, $r_i$ is the rate of return of the asset in the $i$-th state; $P(i)$ is the probability that the $i$-th state occurs, and the sum of the probabilities of the $n$ states is equal to 1.

- (3) Risk is the possibility of a loss in the value of an investment; it reflects in substance the uncertainty of future returns, and the magnitude of risk can be reflected by the deviation from expected return.
	Mathematically the concept of variance can be used for quantitative analysis. Introducing the mathematical concept of variance makes it possible to analyse risk quantitatively. The formula for variance is: $\sigma^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(r_{i}-E(r)\right)^{2} P(i)$

	In practice it is customary to measure risk by the square root of variance, that is the standard deviation, namely:

	$${Standard deviation}=\sigma=\sqrt{\sigma^{2}}$$

The slogan "high risk, high return" needs two qualifications, or it will be repeatedly slapped in the face by the evidence. First, what is priced is only the non-diversifiable part of risk (see the next question); for a single asset a large standard deviation does not necessarily correspond to a high expected return — a long-run high-volatility share can keep underperforming a low-volatility portfolio, and this phenomenon (the low volatility anomaly; see the discussions in Blume 1970 and in Baker, Bradley and Wurgler 2011) has been one of the counter-examples to the CAPM all along. Second, measuring risk by variance tacitly assumes a strong premise: that the distribution of returns is symmetric and that the investor cares only about the mean and variance, the second moment. But real asset return distributions are leptokurtic, fat-tailed and negatively skewed (the over-20% single-day fall in American stocks on 19 October 1987, the four circuit breakers of March 2020, and the thousands of A-share limit-downs in June–July 2015 are all nearly impossible under a normality assumption), and so in practice it is necessary to supplement down-side and tail indicators (Value at Risk, VaR; Conditional Value at Risk, CVaR; maximum drawdown), and it is precisely this road that the April 1993 Group of Thirty report on derivatives and the 1996 Basel Committee amendment on internal models for market risk take. There is also a more fundamental disagreement: all the probabilities here are "objectively given distributions," but the uncertainty in reality often cannot even be written down as a distribution (Knight's 1921 distinction between risk and uncertainty; Keynes's *General Theory*, chapter 12), which is exactly why "Limits and what follows" is still kept after this chapter's four questions.

### Why does diversification reduce risk? Can it eliminate all risk?

- (1) A basic assumption of finance is that the investor is risk-averse, and one effective way to control risk is to diversify investment, that is, the investor can lower risk by constructing a diversified portfolio of investment assets; this principle can be shown through the portfolio's expected rate of return and the covariance formula for portfolio risk.

- (2) The expected rate of return of a portfolio is the weighted average of the expected rates of return of all the assets in the portfolio.
	The formula for the expected rate of return of a portfolio is:

	$$E(r_p)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}E(r_{i})A_{i}$$
	Where $E(r_p)$ is the expected rate of return of the portfolio;
	$A_{i}$ is the share of the $i$-th asset in total assets;
	$m$ is the number of asset types in the portfolio.

	Since the expected return is a weighted average, adding asset types and spreading them by weight does not lower the portfolio's expected return; what is diluted is the idiosyncratic risk of individual assets — it enters the portfolio variance as the square of the weight ($A_i^2\sigma_i^2$), and falls much faster than the expected return as the number of assets increases. This is the precise meaning of "buying more varieties can lower risk, but not at the cost of earning less."

- (3) The risk-covariance formula shows that the risk of a portfolio relates not only to the risk of individual assets, and is not merely the simple weighted average of individual standard deviations, but also to the relationships among the various assets. When an investor combines assets with low mutual correlation the risk is smaller.

	The formula for the standard deviation of the portfolio's rate of return is:

	$$\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m} \sum_{k=1}^{m} A_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{k}} \sigma_{\mathrm{jk}}}$$

	$m$ is the number of asset types in the portfolio;
	$A_j$ is the share of asset $j$ in the portfolio;
	$A_k$ is the share of asset $k$ in the portfolio;
	$\sigma_{jk}$ is the covariance of the rates of return of assets $j$ and $k$. Covariance, so named, reflects the degree to which two assets move together, and its formula is:

	 $$\sigma_{jk}=r_{jk}\sigma_{j}\sigma_{k}$$

	Where $r_{jk}$ is the correlation coefficient of the rates of return of asset $j$ and asset $k$ (usually written $\rho_{jk}$ in the literature).

The covariance of a portfolio reflects the degree to which the portfolio's assets move together, so the smaller the portfolio's covariance, the smaller the risk of investing in the portfolio's assets. The covariance of a portfolio in turn depends on the correlation coefficients among the portfolio's assets: the smaller the correlation among the portfolio's assets, the smaller the coefficient and the lower the portfolio's risk. This principle shows that risk can be lowered through a diversified investment portfolio.

The key step is to break the double sum apart: among the $m$ terms there are $m$ variance terms and $m(m-1)$ covariance terms, and if all assets have equal weights, then

$$\sigma_p^2=\frac{1}{m}\overline{\sigma^2}+\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\overline{cov}$$

That is, portfolio variance is "average variance divided by $m$" plus "average covariance multiplied by $(1-1/m)$". As $m$ increases the first term tends to zero, while the second tends to a non-zero limit — **average covariance**. This is the answer to this question: diversification can only eliminate the part that is mutually uncorrelated (idiosyncratic risk, unsystematic risk), not the part brought by common factors (systematic risk); and beyond a few dozen assets the marginal benefit of adding more is negligible (empirical estimates usually put the limit somewhere between two or three dozen and a few hundred, depending on cross-correlations). This conclusion has three direct effects on the other concepts of the chapter. First, it makes it so the CAPM need only pay a premium for systematic risk (the next question). Second, it explains why the convergence of correlation to 1 during a crisis is especially lethal: in 2008 and in March 2020 equities, corporate bonds, commodities and private real estate all fell together, and the diversification computed in advance failed (a rising correlation coefficient means a rising $\overline{cov}$); the inputs to the Markowitz framework are estimated from history, and are therefore precisely distorted when they are most needed. Third, it draws the distinction between "diversification" and "asset allocation across broad classes": buying more assets within the same broad class is nearly useless, and it is crossing classes (equities and government bonds, maturities and currencies, and the cross-border allocation of chapter 8) that changes $\overline{cov}$; much of the spread of "fund dollar-cost averaging into a basket of shares" in China between 2005 and 2015 and of the net value drawdown disputes over equity-bearing wealth management products after 2022 stems from investors mistaking "many tickers" for "low correlation".

### What does the CAPM set out to solve? What is its cost?

- (1) The CAPM, the Capital Asset Pricing Model, seeks on the basis of portfolio theory the equilibrium price of each capital asset when the capital market is in equilibrium — that is, assuming that people share the same expectations of expected return and risk, and all choose an optimal portfolio according to the principle of diversification, what is the risk premium of a security at the equilibrium state.

- (2) Under the assumptions above, at the equilibrium state of the capital market, analysis of investors' collective behaviour yields the equilibrium prices of all efficient securities and portfolios, that is the capital market line, as shown in Figure 5-1.

![](../img/exercises/currency/5-1.png)

In Figure 5-1, $r_{f}$ is the risk-free lending rate, the tangent point $M$ is the market portfolio, which in theory should include all listed securities. The ray $r_{f}M$ is the capital market line, representing all efficient investment opportunities available to the investor in the securities market, or in other words the best risk–return combination for all investors. Though everyone would like to lie above the capital market line, competitive forces will push asset prices to move until everyone arrives at some point on the line.
The formula for the CML is:

$$E(r_{p})=r_{f}+\frac{E(r_m)-r_f}{\sigma_{m}}\sigma_{p}$$

$${Slope of the CML}=\frac{E(r_m)-r_f}{\sigma_m}$$

The basic idea of the CAPM is this: at equilibrium the reward people receive for bearing risk must, since investors are risk-averse, sum to a positive total across all risky assets.

- (3) The risk premium of a single security is proportional to the degree of its contribution to market risk, and what measures the risk of a single security is its $\beta$ value. The $\beta$ value reflects the sensitivity of a single security to market fluctuation. According to the CAPM, in the equilibrium state the risk premium of any asset equals its $\beta$ value multiplied by the risk premium of the market portfolio. The equation is:
$$E(r_j)-r_f=\beta_j[E(r_m)-r_f]$$

or:

$$E(r_j)=r_f + \beta_j[E(r_m)-r_f]$$

- (4) The CAPM uses the equilibrium method and provides the theoretical basis for asset pricing in financial markets. This model is widely applied in the practice of investment management and corporate finance, but it also has some problems, the main one being that the market portfolio in the model includes all assets and is hard to replicate accurately in empirical research.

The model's contribution lies in turning a constraint into a tool: since diversification can eliminate idiosyncratic risk, the market in the long run pays only for risk that cannot be diversified, and so the pricing problem collapses to one dimension — how much $\beta$ are you exposed to. The difficulty mentioned in point (4) has a proper name: the Roll critique (Roll, 1977) holds that the true market portfolio should contain all wealth, equities, bonds, real estate, human capital and so on, while it is unobservable by nature, so that any "test of the CAPM" is in fact testing the proxy index one has chosen, and the model itself can be neither confirmed nor refuted. The empirical history of the forty years since has largely unfolded on that prediction: Fama and French (1992, 1993), contemporaries of the CAPM, explained the cross-section of equity returns far better than $\beta$ using just size and book-to-market; the measured returns of low-$\beta$ portfolios came in above the model's prediction and those of high-$\beta$ portfolios below (the market portfolio line measures flatter in the data); and from the 1980s "anomalies" such as momentum (Jegadeesh and Titman, 1993), value and earnings quality accumulated to a point that luck cannot explain. The academic response split in two: one branch kept extending the factor set (Carhart's four-factor model of 1997, Fama and French's five-factor model of 2015, then Hou–Xue–Zhang's q-factor model and the 2020s argument over whether the "factor zoo" needs multiple-testing correction — Harvey, Liu and Zhu 2016 maintaining that new factors need a stricter statistical bar); the other abandoned the equilibrium picture in favour of the no-arbitrage approach of the Arbitrage Pricing Theory (Ross, 1976) and the consumption Capital Asset Pricing Model (Lucas 1978, Breeden 1979), the latter defining risk as "comovement of marginal utility in bad times" and stalling on Mehra and Prescott's (1985) "equity premium puzzle" (no reasonable risk-aversion coefficient can explain an equity premium of around six percentage points, and the subsequent long-run risk models of Campbell and Cochrane (1999), Barro (2006) and others are patches applied for that purpose). The practical value of the CAPM therefore lies not in predictive accuracy but in supplying one uniform well of pricing: the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) in corporate valuation, the allowed rate of return in utilities and financial regulation, the Sharpe and Jensen ratios for evaluating fund managers' performance, and the discount rate in M&A still take $\beta$ as the default measure — and what this chapter truly needs the reader to carry away is exactly this judgement: who, in what setting, treats the CAPM as a cost parameter.

## Synthetic essay

### What functions do financial markets perform in the running of the economy?

- (1) The development of financial markets has promoted the scale and efficiency of the conversion of savings into investment, provided ample liquidity for financial assets, and promoted continuous innovation in financial instruments

In a modern economy where specialisation deepens continuously and economies of scale grow increasingly important, the smooth conversion of savings into investment bears on whether the whole real economy can circulate soundly. The traditional financial system relies on financial intermediation dominated by commercial banks, that is indirect finance, to perform its function, but as information technology develops and competition intensifies, capital markets have shown great vitality both in lowering transaction costs and in the ability to process information. Moreover, financial markets can increase the liquidity of traditional financial instruments through flexible trading.

The development of financial markets has not only created the conditions for expanding the scale of financial assets but also promoted innovation in financial instruments. A financial instrument is a standardised contract combining expected return with risk, and diversified financial instruments, by carving up more finely the risks inherent in a given economy's production technology, enable investors with different attitudes to risk and expected return to find the investment best matching their needs, while at the same time the diversified financing needs of financial instrument issuers can likewise be met as fully as possible, which helps raise the efficiency of converting savings into investment.

- (2) The asset pricing mechanism of financial markets can guide funds to flow rationally and feed back the actual state of the economy, thereby raising the efficiency of resource use

A basic principle in the investment activity of micro-economic agents such as firms is to pursue "a positive net present value," that is, the sum of the discounted values of an investment's future cash flows should exceed the investment outlay of the current period; the trading of financial markets can reflect the supply and demand of funds in the market, reflect the risk preferences of different investors, and thereby determine the rates of return on different financial assets, that is the discount rates of future returns, forming asset prices and guiding funds and real resources to flow more rationally.

- (3) The development of financial markets provides the foundation for establishing an indirect system of financial regulation

Establishing an indirect system of financial regulation must rely on a developed financial market to transmit the central bank's policy signals, so that changes in the prices of the financial market can guide the behaviour of the various micro-economic agents. Among the central bank's monetary policy instruments, open market operations, with short-term treasury bills as the main trading instrument, are the principal means, by which the central bank absorbs and releases base money, affecting commercial banks' excess reserves and interbank lending rates, and thereby the credit expansion capacity of financial institutions. At the same time, within a developed financial market, owing to arbitrage, there is a high degree of correlation among the sub-markets, and changes in the money market will quickly be reflected in the capital market, with the movement of capital market prices further extending the reach of monetary policy. As the share of the various financial assets in financial institutions' reserve positions and liquidity holdings rises, financial institutions will enter the operation of financial markets more widely, and the range and force of the central bank's open market operations will be continually strengthened as financial markets develop.

- (4) The development of the stock market provides a window for effectively evaluating a firm's operating condition, and a venue for the firm to carry out a capital operation strategy

The development of the stock market also provides the market capitalisation of listed firms. Changes in a listed firm's market capitalisation can reflect not only the level of its net assets but also its future profitability. Changes in market capitalisation are continuous, and by continuous observation investors can judge a firm's operating condition more accurately, and through buying and selling its shares exert external market pressure on the firm's management. In addition, the development of the stock market promotes the subdivision of ownership in firms, helps listed firms with asset restructuring, and achieves the efficient flow and consolidation of economic resources.

The common premise of the four functions is that "prices carry information," and this is exactly the point on which contemporary argument is most heated. Item (2) depends on the no-arbitrage of section 3 and the formation of expectations in section 5: if prices are dominated by short-term funds, leverage and liquidity shocks, they are no longer a reliable source of discount rates but become amplifiers of wrong signals — the abnormal volatility of China's stock market in June–July 2015 (forced liquidation of off-exchange financing triggering a downward spiral, after which discussion of stabilisation mechanisms and market stabilisation funds has continued to the present) and the cascading liquidations of micro- and small-cap and quantitative strategies in early 2024 are two demonstrations of the same mechanism. Item (3) carries an additional institutional condition in China: the withdrawal of commercial banks from the exchanges and the founding of the interbank market in 1996–1997 were precisely to give open market operations and interest rate transmission an institutional carrier (chapter 7, monetary policy), while bringing government bond trading into the policy toolkit in 2024 and the discussion around 2025 of "a Chinese version of yield curve control" are attempts to extend this function to the long end. The market capitalisation signal of item (4) distorts when delisting and short-selling mechanisms are inadequate: if poor firms are not expelled and the cost of fraud is below its return, market capitalisation is no longer "a window on operating condition" but may be only a function of financing scale — which is also why the registration reform since 2019 and the new Securities Law in force from March 2020 (establishing registration and a China-style securities class action / representative litigation, and sharply raising the cost of violation) place their emphasis on the two ends of disclosure and delisting. Item (1) in turn is a mutual condition of this chapter's section 5 "five steps of venture capital": when liquidity is insufficient risk capital dares not enter, and the function of "promoting innovation" is in fact a downstream result of the other three.

### What is the methodological background of financial engineering? Why does it use "no arbitrage" rather than supply and demand pricing?

Financial engineering refers, since the 1980s, to the process in which the financial sector combines modern financial theory with engineering methods, creatively designing, developing and applying new financial products in response to investors' different risk preferences.

Financial engineering is an emerging interdisciplinary field, and its theory and methods are built on modern financial theory, information technology and engineering methods.

- (1) The portfolio theory proposed by Markowitz in 1952 defined risk and return from a mathematical standpoint and introduced mathematical tools into financial research;

In 1958 Modigliani and Miller proposed the MM theorem, giving modern financial theory a "no-arbitrage equilibrium" method distinct from the supply and demand analysis of traditional economics. By "no-arbitrage equilibrium" is meant that when the price of some asset violates the "law of one price" of "equal value, equal price," the market is in a disequilibrium state and an arbitrage opportunity appears, and the arbitrage motive of market participants pushes the market to equilibrium; at the state of market equilibrium there is no arbitrage opportunity, which is why it is called "no-arbitrage equilibrium". The basic method of financial analysis is to combine the "position" in some financial asset (that is, a holding or a shortage of some asset) with positions in other financial assets in the market, constructing a portfolio position that cannot generate a risk-free profit when the market is in equilibrium, and thereby calculating the value of that position at market equilibrium, that is its equilibrium price.

- (2) The development of information technology provides the necessary material foundation for financial engineering, for example the spread of computers and large-scale computation and data processing

capabilities, which supply effective means for developing and implementing various new financial products and for solving financial and accounting problems, enabling the practical application of portfolio theory and financial engineering; the development of communications technology enables information to spread rapidly nationwide and globally, linking financial markets into a single whole. All of these have greatly lowered the transaction costs of financial markets and raised the efficiency of the market.

- (3) "Financial engineers," on the support of the theory and technical conditions above, and drawing on modern engineering methods, creatively designed various new financial instruments for the different risk preferences and risk-bearing capacities of market investors, endowing them with a particular liquidity and risk–return combination.

The reason "no arbitrage" could replace supply and demand analysis and become the default method of pricing is hard to dispute: most financial instruments have no independent supply and demand curves — for a structured product cut into tranches from a number of bonds, its "demand" is only its spread over an existing tradeable portfolio, and its "supply" can be replicated at will, so the only price that holds is the one that does not yield a risk-free profit when replicated. Supply and demand determine the non-replicable part, such as the risk premium and the liquidity premium (exactly what the CAPM and the Arbitrage Pricing Theory set out to handle), while no-arbitrage determines the replicable part — the coexistence of the two pricing logics is the easiest boundary in this chapter to lose on reading. The limits of the method showed themselves in 2007–2008: no-arbitrage pricing assumes the replicating portfolio can be continuously rebalanced and assumes that usable repos and collateral exist (that is, no frictions), while the collateral shortage and margin rules of the crisis broke "equal value, equal price" badly (spread differences between different bonds of the same issuer, divergence between credit default swaps and spot spreads). A more direct methodological criticism is that the parameters of the model (default correlation, the stability of volatility) are themselves estimated from history, and the structured products failed precisely in states not covered by history. This is also why "off-balance-sheet business and securitisation" in chapter 4 and this section must be read together: financial engineering solved the technical question of "how to compute," without answering "who bears the loss when the computation is wrong" — the Volcker Rule and central clearing of derivatives in the Dodd–Frank Act of the United States in 2010, and the emergency intervention of the Bank of England in the British LDI affair of November 2022, are both institutional responses to the latter half of the question.

## Limits and what follows

- **The pricing part of this chapter stops at the CAPM, but the field since has been one of competing pluralism.** The failure of the single-factor model in cross-section (see the section above) set factor models, consumption-based pricing and behavioural finance each growing apart: Shiller (1981) put excess volatility on the table with the observation that "the volatility of real dividends and stock prices far exceeds what realised dividends could explain"; the prospect theory of Kahneman and Tversky (1979) and the behavioural finance research advanced by Thaler (who received the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2017) explain psychological mechanisms such as the "disposition effect, loss aversion and overconfidence" that push prices away from their no-arbitrage values; Fama and French (1996) argued that most anomalies can be explained by multi-factor risk premia, suggesting that part of the "anomaly" may be a product of data mining. The more robust consensus today is that factor models are used as tools in asset management and investment practice, but no one any longer treats them as a description of equilibrium. Market structure itself has also changed — after 2000 electronic trading rose to more than eighty per cent of volume, and high-frequency market making, dark pools, the American "flash crash" of 6 May 2010, and the confrontation of January 2021 between GameStop retail investors and short-selling funds have together turned "who supplies liquidity and who amplifies the crash" into a new object of study; none of this lies within the framework of this chapter.
- **The institutional face of derivatives markets was rewritten after 2008, and the concepts of this chapter must be reread under the new rules.** Over-the-counter interest rate derivatives were, before the crisis, private agreements with no central counterparty and no margin (by the Bank for International Settlements' reckoning the notional of global over-the-counter derivatives still reached the order of 600 trillion dollars at the end of 2022, more than eighty per cent of it in interest rate products), and after the 2009 Pittsburgh summit they turned to standardised central clearing, margin and trade reporting (Title VII in the United States, EMIR in the European Union, and in China the interbank central clearing pilots from 2014 and the relevant authorisations in the Financial Stability Law draft of 2023). Benchmark reform is another matter: LIBOR was retired because of manipulation scandals (exposed successively from 2012, with several banks fined billions of dollars), and secured overnight rates such as SOFR took over as the reference for new floating-rate debt and interest rate swaps (completed in stages from 2021 to 2023), so that the "benchmark fallback" clauses of existing contracts turned overnight from a technical detail into a legal and audit question. After China's June 2013 "liquidity crunch" and the abnormal volatility of the stock market in 2015, and the 2016 bond market nominee-holding affair (the redemption risk triggered by the "Guohai Securities rubber stamp nominee holding" at the end of December 2016), trade reporting, nominee holding and leverage limits in the interbank and bond markets were likewise tightened sharply — the judgement this chapter takes for granted, that "the more standardised a financial instrument the better," must in the regulatory view be qualified: standardisation also shifted risk onto the single clearing house and onto a small clique of primary dealers supplying liquidity.
- **The claim that "financial deepening necessarily raises efficiency" needs conditions.** McKinnon's (1973) and Shaw's (1973) theory of financial repression was once the prescription for market building in developing countries (liberalise interest rates, develop direct finance), and the results of Korea's mid-1980s liberalisation of interest rates, Japan's 1980s deregulation of capital markets, and Latin America's 1970s–80s liberalisation experiments differed enormously: Chile's liberalisation came with the 1981–1982 bank crisis, Argentina's late-1970s experiment ended in inflation and dollarisation, while Korea completed high growth while keeping credit directed. Contemporary explanation reverses the order: what market efficiency depends on is the stock of institutions for disclosure, the enforcement of bankruptcy, accounting and investor protection (the law and finance series of La Porta and others from 1998, and the post-2000 literature on enforcement capacity and contract enforcement), not "liberalise and it works." This explains why China advanced the registration system reform of 2019–2023 together with class action and delisting arrangements, rather than liberalising the issuance side alone as the early liberalisation arguments advised.
- **One particular structure of China's financial markets is still operating: market segmentation is decided by the market access of financial institutions rather than by price.** The separation of the exchange market and the interbank market (the dual-market structure formed after commercial banks withdrew from the exchanges in 1997), the after-image of "dual-track" interest rates in the credit market (the transmission from policy rate through market rate to lending rate is still being cleared, the LPR reform of 2019 and the self-disciplined adjustment of deposit rates from 2024), the long-narrow credit spreads between bond issuers (the risk pricing between interest rate bonds, state-owned enterprise bonds and private enterprise bonds only opening up step by step after 2018), and the market-based resolution of local government financing vehicle and property debt risk (the impact of the 2018–2020 defaults of Yongmei, Huachen and others on regional spreads and refinancing) all show that the mechanism of "prices guiding the allocation of resources" described in this chapter is only partially realised in China. Read the four functions of this chapter as criteria for judgement rather than as descriptions of the present state, or one is led to the conclusion that "the functions are already in place".
