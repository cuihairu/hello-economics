# Chapter 5. Financial markets

> **The core question of this chapter**: What kinds of financial markets are there, and what functions do they perform? How does the money market differ from the capital market? How are financial instruments innovated? How are risk and return priced?

## The arc of this chapter

The markets and instruments of this chapter were built in batches, and almost every batch answers a specific difficulty. The prototype of the exchange-traded securities market is the concentrated trading in Amsterdam of the shares of the Dutch East India Company (VOC), founded in 1602 — the problem was not that firms lacked money, but that merchants who had subscribed for shares in a sea venture could not get out, so there was a need for a place where illiquid equity could be turned into tradable instruments; London's origin was coarser (in 1792 twenty-four brokers signed an agreement under a walnut tree on Wall Street, the official Philadelphia market having refused them the trade in government bonds), but it too was born to solve the "no counterparty to be found" problem of government bonds and bank shares. The futures and options branch comes from agricultural produce: the seasonality of grain production and the volatility of its price left both traders and processors unable to budget, and in eighteenth-century Japan the Dojima rice market had already produced futures contracts carrying official certification together with "buy long, sell short" trading; the Chicago Board of Trade (CBOT) was founded in 1848, and in 1865 it introduced standardized contracts alongside a margin and clearing system — the two institutional inventions of "standardization plus a central counterparty" are the substance that distinguishes futures from forwards. After the collapse of the Bretton Woods system and the take-off of inflation in the 1970s the remaining instruments appeared in concentration: in 1972 the International Monetary Market of the Chicago Mercantile Exchange launched foreign exchange futures (exchange rates no longer being fixed, firms for the first time needed to buy insurance systematically); in 1974–1977 interest rate futures went on the market (rate volatility being the same story); in April 1973 the Chicago Board Options Exchange began trading tradable options, and in the same year Black and Scholes gave the pricing formula; in 1981 Salomon Brothers brokered the first widely known currency swap between the World Bank and IBM (both sides wanting to get round the market regulation they were each subject to and to realize comparative advantage) — the four derivatives among the core concepts of this chapter, laid out chronologically, follow exactly the sequence "exchange-rate volatility → interest-rate volatility → nonlinear risk → realization of comparative advantage." Asset pricing theory was erected in the same period: Markowitz (1952) wrote risk as variance, Modigliani and Miller (1958) gave the no-arbitrage method, Sharpe (1964), Lintner (1965) and Mossin (1966) produced the CAPM, and Fama (1970) consolidated the efficient market hypothesis — these five things explain the whole structure of the four questions under "Problems and analysis" in this chapter. China's path is a list of alternating regulatory and market construction: treasury bills resumed in 1981; the Shanghai and Shenzhen Stock Exchanges opened in turn in 1990; centralized supervision established in 1992–1993; the national interbank lending market in 1996 and the interbank bond market in June 1997 (commercial banks being required to withdraw from exchange-traded repurchase); the futures exchanges cleaned up, rectified and merged into three in 1999–2000; the share-split reform in 2005; Shibor quoting running in 2007; the ChiNext board in 2009; CSI 300 stock index futures in 2010; the STAR Market and the registration-system pilot in 2019; the Beijing Stock Exchange in 2021; and the full introduction of the share issuance registration system in February 2023. Read the two lines together and the point of this chapter is not to remember what the markets are called, but to see the conditions on which each kind of instrument depends: a tradable price, a mechanism for handling default, and a counterparty willing to bear risk — miss any one of the three and the instrument is only a paper concept.

## Core concepts

### Financial instruments

Financial instruments are also called financial assets. They are written documents that certify the amount, maturity and price of a financial transaction and are legally binding upon the rights and obligations of both the creditor and the debtor.
Financial instruments should generally possess the basic features of liquidity, riskiness and yield.
Liquidity is the ability of a financial instrument to be converted quickly into money without suffering a loss;
riskiness is the likelihood that, under the influence of credit risk and market risk, the buyer of a financial instrument suffers an economic loss;
yield is the ratio of the return obtained from holding a financial instrument to the principal.

The phrase "written document" needs updating today: the great majority of financial instruments have long ceased to exist on paper, and their substance is a **transferable contract right with legal enforcement behind it**. That decides the three entrances to the analysis of financial instruments. First, whether transfer is possible is decided by law and by the registration and settlement system, not by technology — the same receivable can or cannot be broken up and sold depending on whether there is a genuine sale with bankruptcy remoteness (see asset securitization in this chapter, chapter 4). Second, liquidity and riskiness are not independent attributes of one another: what "converting into money quickly without loss" presupposes is that the market continuously maintains two-way quotes, and it is the quotes that vanish first in a panic. On 16 September 2008 the Reserve Primary Fund, because it held Lehman Brothers commercial paper, saw its net asset value per share fall to 0.97 dollars (breaking below its one-dollar par value from 16 September 2008 on), and the American commercial paper market froze that week — the textbook case of "an asset close to cash suddenly losing liquidity"; the same logic appears in China as the interbank and non-standard assets of the "liquidity squeeze" of June 2013, and as the wave of wealth-management redemptions during the bond adjustment of November 2022. Third, yield carries information only when stated together with risk — which is the whole reason for section 4 of this chapter (risk and return) and for the CAPM. As for the two words "financial instrument" and "financial asset": the former emphasizes instrumentality and contract structure (the issuer's view), the latter is the accounting and regulatory classification of a held asset (the holder's view); textbooks use both, and one switches between them by context.

### The money market

The money market is the market for the short-term financing of funds of one year and under, and it includes the sub-markets of interbank lending, the interbank bond market, large negotiable certificates of deposit, commercial paper and treasury bills. The financial instruments of this market have very short maturities, close to money, which is why it is called the money market. The money market is an important venue for financial institutions to adjust their liquidity, and it is the foundation of a central bank's monetary policy operations.

The four words "close to money" are the key to understanding how this chapter joins onto the earlier ones: what the participants of the money market borrow is not funds but **the surplus and shortfall of reserves and cash**, so the interest rate of this market is the one the central bank can manipulate directly (the benchmark rate of chapter 2 and the open market operations of chapter 7 both land here). It is also for this reason that the market is extremely sensitive to credit quality: treasury bills and interbank certificates of deposit usually carry a very small spread, yet on 16–17 September 2019 the American repurchase rate jumped overnight from 2% to about 10% intraday (the tax calendar, government bond settlements and new regulatory rules all drawing down reserves, together with banks' contraction of repurchase), which shows that this "close to money" channel has to be maintained by ample reserves; the across-the-board surge in dollar money market rates of March 2020, when the Federal Reserve had to intervene with unlimited repurchase operations and the Money Market Fund Liquidity Facility (MMLF), is the extreme version of the same thing. Since 1996 (the lending centre) and 1997 (the interbank bond market), China's interbank lending and repurchase markets have formed the practical carrier of the policy rate, and DR007 (the weighted seven-day repurchase rate of deposit-taking institutions pledged with rate-bearing bonds) has become the preferred indicator for observing the funding situation — the practical landing of the characterization as "the central bank's operating foundation." What needs qualification is the classification of the "interbank bond market": this market trades both seven-day repurchase and thirty-year government bonds, so by maturity it spans both concepts of this chapter at once, and its overall inclusion in the money market is according to its function as a "venue of liquidity management."

### The capital market

The capital market generally refers to the market with trading maturities of more than one year; it includes mainly the stock market and the bond market, and it meets the medium- and long-term investment needs of industrial and commercial enterprises together with the needs of the fiscal deficit.
Because the trading maturities are long and the funds are used mainly to form real capital, it is called the capital market.

The emphasis of this definition falls on the second half: what the capital market handles are **commitments reaching across an uncertain future**, so its prices are not "prices of funds" but "judgements about the future." This brings two features that must be distinguished from those of the money market. The first is that the amplitude of price fluctuation is large and cannot be removed by maturity — a share has no maturity date, and any change in its discount rate or growth expectation goes directly into the price, which is why the exchange rate theory of chapter 8 and the asset pricing of this chapter share the logic of "expectation dominance." The second is that the market's survival depends on a two-way flow of issuance and exit; if there is only financing without return and clearing, valuation detaches from cash flow. The reform history of China's capital market turns on this point almost entirely: when the exchanges were set up in the early 1990s they were positioned as a financing channel for the difficulties of state-owned enterprises; the share-split reform of 2005 addressed the structural defect of "equal shares but unequal rights, with non-tradable shares in the majority"; margin trading and off-exchange financing amplified trading in 2013–2015; the STAR Market of 2019 and the full registration system of 2023 shifted the power of pricing from review to disclosure and market-based bookbuilding, while the delisting system, long weak, is the one link that has not yet closed. Another contemporary change is that the boundary of "more than one year" is being blurred by the policy operations themselves: from 2024 the People's Bank of China has included open market purchases and sales of government bonds among its liquidity adjustment tools, so medium- and long-term bonds have come to double as money market instruments — a further instance that the line between the two concepts of this chapter can move.

### Forwards

A forward is an agreement to buy or sell a definite quantity of a given asset at a definite price at some definite time in the future.
In a forward contract the asset agreed to be bought and sold is called the underlying asset, the agreed price is called the contract price, the party agreeing to sell the underlying asset at the agreed price is called the short side, and the party agreeing to buy the underlying asset at the agreed price is called the long side. Forward contracts are used very widely in the foreign exchange market, because they can prevent the risk of exchange-rate fluctuation quite effectively.

The difference between forwards and futures (the next concept) is often told as "over-the-counter versus on-exchange," but the real cost lies elsewhere: a forward is a bilateral credit contract, so in its price, besides the risk of the underlying asset, there is layered the counterparty's default risk and the collateral arrangement. This is why an instrument that looks so primitive has not been superseded by standardized products — what a firm needs is an amount and a date that fit its own cash flow exactly, and futures come only in whole multiples of a standard lot. Chinese firms will find that the availability of forwards is itself subject to regulation: after the establishment of the interbank foreign exchange market in 1997, forward foreign exchange in renminbi was opened up; in July 2005, alongside the exchange-rate reform, foreign exchange swaps were launched; renminbi options against foreign currency began in 2010; and the participating parties have long been confined to institutional clients and members of the interbank market, so that firms' hedging must go through the layer of the bank, and the quote therefore includes the bank's position cost and capital occupation. Only understanding this can one read the policy meaning of the multi-ministry drive of the 2020s to "lower firms' cost of hedging exchange-rate risk and widen derivatives services to small and medium enterprises": designing a forward is not hard; what is hard is getting those willing to sell insurance to be willing to sell it for small amounts and to dispersed clients.

### Futures

A futures contract is a standardized contract of sale that has developed on the basis of the forward contract.
The main difference between the two is that a futures contract is a standardized contract issued by an exchange, traded centrally on the exchange, and the performance of which is guaranteed by the exchange.

The technical content of "the exchange guarantees performance" is daily settlement without default (marking to market) and clearing by a central counterparty: the exchange inserts itself between buyer and seller and becomes "the seller to all buyers and the buyer to all sellers," transferring margin daily at the settlement price, so that the losing side must top up its position or else be closed out by force. This mechanism (in prototype the CBOT's design of 1865) concentrates default risk from the counterparty into the clearing house, and brings two consequences that the analysis later in this chapter cannot get around. The first is that leverage becomes explicit: a notional position can be held with a margin of only a few percentage points, so that a small adverse move in the price can wipe out the entire equity — the "February 1995 government bond futures No. 327" incident in China did exactly this, using the spot-futures price spread to manipulate and market positioning to amplify losses under the margin system, ultimately leading to the suspension of government bond futures for nearly eighteen years (they were not resumed until March 2015), and the subsequent regulatory tone of the Chinese futures market (position limits, large-position reporting, daily price limits) is shaped by this history. The second is that risk is not destroyed but transferred: when a sharp short-term price move makes the margin call itself a selling pressure, the clearing mechanism turns back on the holder. The liability-driven investing (LDI) crisis in British pension funds of September–October 2022 is the latest instance — the interest rate swaps and futures held to hedge a rise in rates were called upon for billions of pounds of additional margin as long-end yields surged, the forced selling of long government bonds pushed yields further up, and the loop ran until the Bank of England entered the market to buy bonds. Futures and swaps make "hedging" valid for an individual position, but at the systemic level they create a new dependence on liquidity.

### Swaps

A swap is a transaction in which the parties reach an agreement and, within a definite period, convert with each other their kinds of currency, the basis of their borrowing interest rates, and other assets.
Swap agreements are mainly of two kinds, currency swaps and interest rate swaps. A currency swap is one in which the two parties exchange two currencies at an agreed rate and agree to repurchase the original currencies from each other at that rate within a definite period in the future. An interest rate swap is one in which the two parties exchange the interest income (expenditure) on the claims (debts) they hold. The principal values of these two claims (debts) are the same, but the terms of interest payment differ, and the exchange can meet the different needs of both sides.

The reason swaps took off after 1981 is that what they solve is the "realization of comparative advantage," not "price insurance": two borrowers of different credit standing do not have the same relative financing costs in fixed-versus-floating or different-currency markets, so each borrows in the market where it has the advantage and swaps back into the liability it needs, and both are cheaper than borrowing directly — the currency swap between the World Bank and IBM in 1981 was exactly of this kind (the World Bank wanted cheap funds in francs and marks, and IBM needed to convert the fixed-rate borrowing into a floating one outside the United States). It should be noted here that the economic meaning of a swap differs from that of forwards and futures: in substance it is a bundle of forward contracts, so its risk exposure accumulates over time rather than being settled day by day. Before 2008 over-the-counter swaps had almost no central counterparty, no margin, and an out-of-control notional principal (according to Bank for International Settlements statistics, the global notional amount of over-the-counter interest rate derivatives had reached the order of 300 trillion dollars by mid-2007), and the fact that AIG's Financial Products division needed government rescue because the margin clauses of credit default swaps were triggered in September 2008 is precisely the cost of that structure. The post-crisis reform directly rewrote this concept of the chapter: at the G20 Pittsburgh Summit in September 2009 the commitment was made that standardized interest rate swaps would be cleared through a central counterparty, and Title VII of the American *Dodd–Frank Act* and the EU *European Market Infrastructure Regulation* (EMIR) succeeded each other in building centralized clearing, margin and trade reporting systems. China's renminbi interest rate swaps launched in the interbank market in 2006 (no margin required, concluded by inquiry and master agreement), with centralized clearing pilots gradually introduced after 2014; the development of its credit derivatives and swap markets lagged markedly behind the expansion of the bond stock in the same period — which is one reason why the debt risk of local governments and property developers is hard to price and disperse. One note on the LIBOR appearing in this section's example: that quoted benchmark was retired because of manipulation scandals after 2008 and the shrinkage of the lending market (phased out of publication in 2021–2023), and the dollar switched to new benchmarks such as the Secured Overnight Financing Rate (SOFR, published from April 2018); the reference rates on the floating legs of the instruments in this chapter were reset accordingly, which is the largest technical restructuring of swap and futures pricing in recent years.

### Options

An option contract confers upon its holder (that is, the buyer of the option) a right to buy (a call option) or sell (a put option) from the seller of the option contract a definite quantity of an asset, at an agreed price, within a future period agreed upon — a right that may be exercised but need not be.
To obtain this right the holder of the contract has to pay a definite option premium.

The option is the only one of the five instruments in this chapter that is "asymmetric in rights and obligations," and for that reason the hardest to handle conceptually: in forwards, futures and swaps the gain and loss of the two sides are symmetric, whereas the option buyer exchanges a limited cost (the option premium) for the right or the left tail of the return distribution. To price it one cannot rely on "supply and demand" but must rely on replication — construct out of the underlying asset and borrowing a portfolio whose payments are the same as those of the option in every state, and then the cost of that portfolio is the price of the option (the concretization of the no-arbitrage logic; see "The methodological background of financial engineering" in this chapter). Black and Scholes (1973; Merton gave the rigorization in the same year) derived their continuous-time pricing formula along exactly this line of thought, on the assumptions that the underlying price follows geometric Brownian motion, that there is no friction, and that continuous rebalancing is possible; the departure of these conditions from reality is the source of the later "implied volatility smile" (the implied volatilities at different strikes for the same expiry are not the same, and have persisted since the crash in American equities of October 1987) and of skewness risk, and it is also the reason why volatility itself has become a tradeable asset class. At the institutional level there is one distinction worth remembering: an American option can be exercised at any time before expiry, a European only on the expiry date; index options are mostly European while single-stock options are mostly American, and China's SSE 50 ETF option (February 2015, the first on-exchange equity-type option) is European while the commodity options (soybean meal, corn and the like from 2019) are American — the treatment of spreads and margin differs accordingly. For policy discussion, options offer a vantage point no other instrument gives: they put a price directly on "how great the uncertainty is," which is why the implied volatility of CSI 300 index options is often taken as a reading of the market's degree of panic, and together with the inflation-expectation surveys of chapter 3 and the exchange-rate option risk reversals of chapter 8 forms three routes to making expectations observable.

## Problems and analysis

### How does venture capital put money into a company that has no profits yet?

Venture capital refers to capital operated by venture capital funds. The investment process of venture capital divides roughly into five steps:

- (1) Origination of the deal, that is, the venture capitalist learns of a potential investment opportunity.
- (2) Screening of investment opportunities, that is, selecting a small number out of the many potential opportunities for further analysis.
- (3) Evaluation, that is, assessing the potential risk and return of the selected project. If the evaluation is acceptable, the venture capitalist and the entrepreneur proceed together to the fourth step, deal design.
- (4) Deal design. Deal design includes fixing the amount, form and price of the investment and so on. Once the deal is completed, the last step is entered — investment management.
- (5) Post-investment management. The key of post-investment management is to bring the firm into the operation of capital markets so that the necessary mergers and acquisitions and the issuance and listing can be realized smoothly.

Of the five steps the most important are the fourth and the fifth, because they are what distinguish venture capital from "buying some shares and waiting for them to rise." The problem the fourth step has to handle is this: if the judgement cannot be made before investing, then postpone the judgement and write it into the contract — staged investment (staging, adding on milestones, with the option to cut losses at any time), convertible preferred shares or convertible bonds (repaid with priority at liquidation, converting into ordinary shares after growth to share the upside), board seats and veto rights (information and intervention rights), vesting of founder equity over time and non-compete clauses. The fifth step points to a premise often overlooked: this set of contracts yields a high return only because there exists an exit route that can turn book equity into cash. The first through third steps are therefore only a question of the allocation of screening costs: since the failure rate of projects is very high (the industry often cites a distribution of "two or three successes out of ten"), venture capital inevitably has a power-law character, and a few successful projects have to cover all costs, which also explains why it demands high growth rates and an ending of "listing or acquisition." The fourth and fifth together explain the structural conclusion of this chapter: venture capital and the capital market depend on one another — without a smooth market for issuance and acquisition, the fifth step fails, and no matter how finely drawn the protective clauses of the fourth step, they cannot save the pricing. In the United States, the *Small Business Investment Companies Act* of 1958 and the cut in the capital gains tax from 49.5% to 28% in 1978–1979, together with the admission of pension funds into venture capital (through the interpretive revision of the prudent-man rule of the *Employee Retirement Income Security Act* of 1979), and then the two booms triggered by the listing of Apple in 1981 and of Netscape after 1995, are all sequences in which "exit route plus tax regime" moved first and the scale of investment followed. The corresponding chain in China is: the State Council approving the establishment of China New Technology Venture Capital Company in 1985; the rise of indigenous institutions after 1993 (such as Shenzhen Capital Group, founded in 1999); the ChiNext board providing an exit in 2009; the STAR Market and the registration system in 2019 writing "unprofitable firms may list" into the rules; government guidance funds after 2014 (entering a peak of establishment around 2015, and by the mid-2020s holding the main share of primary market fundraising) displacing part of the dollar funds; while the new asset management rules of 2018 restricting maturity mismatch and layering, and the tightening of regulation in some sectors after 2021 together with the narrowing of overseas listing routes, directly compressed the fifth step, and the sharp rise in disputes over buy-backs and valuation adjustment agreements that followed (the Haifu case of 2012 establishing the line of judgement that a valuation adjustment agreement with the company is void but with the shareholders is valid, refined by the *Minutes of the National Courts' Conference on Civil and Commercial Adjudication* of 2019) is precisely the legal reaction to blocked exit. This whole string of facts and steps (4) and (5) of this chapter are the two sides of one proposition.

### Does high return always come with high risk? How is risk quantified?

- (1) Risk and return are a pair of opposites that any investor must take into account in making a decision; investment and return are symmetric — high risk brings high return, low risk brings low return — and every return has to be adjusted by risk.
	In 1952 Markowitz was the first to describe the relation between risk and return in comparatively precise mathematical language, laying the foundation of asset pricing theory.

- (2) Before investing each investor must form an expectation of the return, so that from the mathematical point of view the expectation of return is often expressed as the expected rate of return on the investment.
	Because investment faces the uncertainty of the future, the expected rate of return is the investor's overall estimate, before investing, of the rate of return under the various uncertain states of the future. From the mathematical point of view the expected rate of return is in fact the mathematical expectation of the rate of return as a random variable, and the formula is: $E(r)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}P(i)$

	Here $E(r)$ denotes the expected rate of return of a given asset; $i=1,2,3,\cdots,n$; $n$ denotes the $n$ possible states of affairs, $r_i$ the rate of return of the asset in the $i$-th state, and $P(i)$ the probability of the $i$-th state occurring, the probabilities of the $n$ states summing to 1.

- (3) Risk is the likelihood of a loss in the value of the investment. It reflects in fact the uncertainty of future return, and the deviation from the expected return can be used to measure the magnitude of the risk.
	Quantitatively, the mathematical concept of variance can be used. Introducing the mathematical concept of variance allows the risk to be analysed quantitatively, and the formula for variance is: $\sigma^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(r_{i}-E(r)\right)^{2} P(i)$

	In practice it is customary to measure risk by the square root of the variance, that is, the standard deviation, namely:

	$$\mathrm{standard\ deviation}=\sigma=\sqrt{\sigma^{2}}$$

The slogan "high risk, high return" needs two qualifications, or it will be repeatedly contradicted empirically. The first is that only the undiversifiable part of the risk is priced (see the next question); for a single asset, a large standard deviation does not necessarily correspond to a high expected return — a stock with long-standing high volatility can persistently underperform a low-volatility portfolio, a phenomenon (the low-volatility anomaly; see Blume 1970 and the discussion by Baker, Bradley and Wurgler, 2011) that has always been one of the counter-examples to the CAPM. The second is that measuring risk by variance assumes a strong premise: that the distribution of returns is symmetric and that the investor cares only about the second moment, the dispersion. But the real distribution of asset returns is leptokurtic, heavy-tailed and negatively skewed (the fall of more than 20% in American equities on 19 October 1987, the four circuit breakers of March 2020, and the thousand stocks pinned to their limits-down in A-shares in June–July 2015 are all nearly impossible under the normality assumption), so that practice must supplement downside and tail measures (value at risk, VaR; conditional value at risk, CVaR; maximum drawdown), and the April 1993 report of the Group of Thirty on derivatives and the 1996 amendment of the Basel Committee on internal models for market risk follow precisely this route. There is also a more fundamental disagreement: all the probabilities here are "objectively given distributions," but the uncertainty of reality often does not even admit of a distribution being written down (Knight's 1921 distinction between risk and uncertainty; chapter 12 of Keynes's *General Theory*), which is precisely why this chapter still needs to keep a "Limits and what follows" after its four questions.

### Why does diversification reduce risk? And can it eliminate all risk?

- (1) A basic assumption of finance is that the investor is an averter of risk, and one effective way to control risk is diversification: the investor can lower risk by constructing a diversified portfolio of investment assets. This principle can be shown by the formula for the expected rate of return of a portfolio and by the formula for the covariance of the risk of a portfolio.

- (2) The expected rate of return of a portfolio is the weighted average of the expected rates of return of all the assets in it.
	The formula for the expected rate of return of a portfolio is:

	$$E(r_p)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}E(r_{i})A_{i}$$
	Here $E(r_p)$ is the expected rate of return of the portfolio;
	$A_{i}$ is the weight of the $i$-th asset in the total assets;
	$m$ is the number of kinds of assets in the portfolio.

	Since the expected rate of return is a weighted average, adding kinds of assets and spreading them by weight does not lower the portfolio's expected return; what is thinned out is the specific risk of the individual asset — it enters the variance of the portfolio in the square of its weight ($A_i^2\sigma_i^2$) and falls much faster than the expected return as the number of assets increases. That is the exact meaning of "buying more varieties lowers risk, but need not be paid for by earning less."

- (3) The covariance formula for risk shows: the risk of a portfolio relates not only to the risk of the individual assets — not merely the simple weighted average of their standard deviations — but also to the relations among the assets. When the investor forms a portfolio by choosing assets that are little correlated with one another, the risk is smaller.

	The formula for the standard deviation of the portfolio's rate of return is:

	$$\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m} \sum_{k=1}^{m} A_{\mathrm{j}} A_{\mathrm{k}} \sigma_{\mathrm{jk}}}$$

	$m$ is the number of kinds of assets in the portfolio;
	$A_j$ is the weight of asset $j$ in the portfolio;
	$A_k$ is the weight of asset $k$ in the portfolio;
	$\sigma_{jk}$ is the covariance of the rates of return of assets $j$ and $k$. Covariance, so called, reflects the degree to which two assets move together, and the formula is:

	 $$\sigma_{jk}=r_{jk}\sigma_{j}\sigma_{k}$$

	Here $r_{jk}$ is the coefficient of correlation between the rates of return of asset $j$ and asset $k$ (usually denoted $\rho_{jk}$ in the literature).

The covariance of a portfolio reflects the degree to which its assets move together, so that the smaller the covariance of the portfolio, the smaller the risk of investing in the portfolio's assets. The covariance of a portfolio in turn depends on the coefficients of correlation among its assets: the smaller the correlation, the smaller the coefficient, and the lower the risk of the portfolio's assets. This principle shows that risk can be lowered by diversifying the investment portfolio.

The key step is to split the double sum apart: among the $m$ terms there are $m$ variance terms and $m(m-1)$ covariance terms, so that if the assets are equally weighted, then

$$\sigma_p^2=\frac{1}{m}\overline{\sigma^2}+\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\overline{cov}$$

that is, the variance of the portfolio is "the average variance divided by $m$" plus "the average covariance multiplied by $(1-1/m)$." As $m$ grows the first term tends to zero, while the second tends to a non-zero limit — the **average covariance**. This is the answer to the question: diversification can only eliminate the part that is mutually uncorrelated (specific risk, unsystematic risk), and cannot eliminate the part brought by common factors (systematic risk); and beyond a few dozen holdings the marginal return of adding more is negligible (experience usually places the limit somewhere between two or three dozen and a few hundred, depending on the correlation among the individual stocks). This conclusion has three direct effects on the other concepts of the chapter. The first is that it leaves the CAPM owing compensation only for systematic risk (the next question). The second is that it explains why the tendency of correlations to 1 during a crisis is so fatal: in 2008 and in March 2020 equities, corporate bonds, commodities and private real estate all fell together, and the diversification calculated in advance failed (a rise in the correlation coefficients is a rise in $\overline{cov}$), so that the inputs of the Markowitz framework, estimated from history, are distorted precisely at the moment when they are most needed. The third is that it gives the difference between "diversification" and "allocation across asset classes": buying more holdings within one class is nearly useless, and it is crossing classes (equities and government bonds, maturities and currencies, and the cross-border allocation spoken of in chapter 8) that changes $\overline{cov}$; much of the spread of "investing a basket of stocks through periodic fund purchase" in China between 2005 and 2015, and of the argument over the drawdowns in the net value of wealth-management products with equity exposure after 2022, comes from investors mistaking "many holdings" for "low correlation."

### What problem does the CAPM set out to solve? And what does it cost?

- (1) The CAPM, the capital asset pricing model, seeks on the basis of portfolio theory the equilibrium price of each capital asset when the capital market is in equilibrium: given that all people form the same expectations of the expected rate of return and of risk, and all choose an optimal portfolio according to the principle of diversification, what is the risk premium of a security when equilibrium is reached.

- (2) Under these assumptions, in the equilibrium state of the capital market, an analysis of the collective behaviour of investors yields the equilibrium price of all efficient securities and portfolios — the capital market line, as shown in Figure 5-1.

![](../img/exercises/currency/5-1.png)

In Figure 5-1, $r_{f}$ denotes the risk-free lending rate, and the tangency point $M$ is the market portfolio of securities, which in theory should include all the listed securities. The ray $r_{f}M$ is the capital market line, representing all the efficient investment opportunities available to the investor in the securities market, that is, the best risk-return combination for every investor. Although everyone wishes to reach a point above the capital market line, competitive forces push the prices of assets so that everyone ends up at some point on the line.
The formula for the CML is:

$$E(r_{p})=r_{f}+\frac{E(r_m)-r_f}{\sigma_{m}}\sigma_{p}$$

$${\mathrm{the\ slope\ of\ CML}}=\frac{E(r_m)-r_f}{\sigma_m}$$

The basic idea of the CAPM is the reward which people receive for bearing risk at equilibrium: since investors are averse to risk, the total risk premium of all risky assets must be positive.

- (3) The risk premium of a single security is proportional to the degree of its contribution to market risk, and what measures the risk of a single security is its $\beta$ value. The $\beta$ value reflects the sensitivity of a single security to the fluctuation of the market. According to the CAPM, in the equilibrium state, the risk premium of any asset equals its $\beta$ value multiplied by the risk premium of the market portfolio. The equation is:
$$E(r_j)-r_f=\beta_j[E(r_m)-r_f]$$

Or:

$$E(r_j)=r_f + \beta_j[E(r_m)-r_f]$$

- (4) The CAPM uses the equilibrium method and provides a theoretical basis for asset pricing in financial markets. The model has been applied widely in the practice of investment management and corporate finance, but it also has some problems, mainly that the market portfolio in the model includes all assets, which is hard to simulate accurately in empirical research.

The contribution of this model lies in turning a constraint into a tool: since diversification can eliminate specific risk, the market will in the long run pay only for risk that cannot be diversified, and so the pricing problem is reduced to one dimension — how much $\beta$ you are exposed to. The difficulty mentioned in point (4) has a name: the Roll critique (Roll, 1977) points out that the true market portfolio should include all wealth — equities, bonds, real estate, human capital and the like — but that it is never observed, so that any "test of the CAPM" is in fact a test of the proxy index the tester has chosen, and the model itself can be neither confirmed nor refuted. The following forty years of empirical history have largely unfolded along this prediction: Fama (Eugene Fama) and French (Kenneth French, 1992, 1993), contemporaries of the CAPM, explain the cross-section of stock returns far better than $\beta$ does with two variables, size and the book-to-market ratio; the measured return of low-$\beta$ portfolios is higher than the model predicts and that of high-$\beta$ portfolios lower (the line for the market portfolio is flatter in measurement); and in the 1980s momentum (Jegadeesh and Titman, 1993), value, earnings quality and so on

## Synthetic essay

### What functions do financial markets perform in the operation of the economy?

- (1) The development of financial markets promotes the scale and efficiency of the conversion of savings into investment, provides financial assets with ample liquidity, and promotes the continual innovation of financial instruments.

In a modern economy with deepening specialisation and increasingly important economies of scale, the smooth conversion of savings into investment concerns whether the real economy as a whole can circulate in a healthy way. The traditional financial system relies on financial intermediation — indirect finance centred on commercial banks — to perform its functions; but with the development of information technology and the intensification of competition, the capital market shows vigorous vitality both in lowering transaction costs and in its power to process information. Moreover, financial markets can increase the liquidity of traditional financial instruments through flexible trading.

The development of financial markets not only creates the conditions for the expansion of the scale of financial assets but also promotes the innovation of financial instruments. A financial instrument is a standardised contract combining an expected return with risk; the diversification of financial instruments draws a finer partition of the risk inherent in the production technology of an economy, so that investors with different attitudes towards risk and expected return can find the investment that best fits their needs, while the diversified financing needs of the issuers of financial instruments are likewise met as far as possible, which helps to raise the efficiency of the conversion of savings into investment.

- (2) The asset pricing mechanism of financial markets can guide funds to flow reasonably, feed back the actual state of economic operation, and thereby raise the efficiency of the use of resources.

For the investment activity of microeconomic agents, firms being the representative case, one basic principle is to pursue a "positive net present value," that is, the discounted sum of the future cash flows of an investment should exceed the investment outlay in the current period; the trading of financial markets can reflect the supply of and demand for funds in financial markets, reflect the risk preferences of different investment agents, and thereby determine the rates of return on different financial assets, that is, the discount rate applied to future returns, forming asset prices and guiding funds and real resources to flow more reasonably.

- (3) The development of financial markets provides the foundation for the establishment of an indirect system of financial regulation.

The establishment of an indirect system of financial regulation must rely on a developed financial market to transmit the policy signals of the central bank, so that the behaviour of the various microeconomic agents can be guided through changes in prices in financial markets. Among the monetary policy instruments of a central bank, open market operations, with short-term treasury bills as the main instrument of trading, are the principal means: on the basis of them the central bank absorbs and issues base money, affecting the excess reserves of commercial banks and the interest rate in the interbank lending market, and thereby the credit-expansion capacity of financial institutions. At the same time, within a developed financial market, owing to the effect of arbitrage there is a high degree of correlation among the sub-markets, so that changes in the money market are quickly reflected in the capital market, and through changes in quotations in the capital market the scope of the influence of monetary policy is further widened. As the share of the various financial assets in the reserve positions and liquidity buffers of financial institutions rises, financial institutions will intervene more widely in the operation of financial markets, and the scope and force of the open market operations of the central bank will be continuously strengthened with the development of the financial market.

- (4) The development of the stock market provides a window through which the operating condition of a firm can be evaluated effectively, and provides a place for firms to implement a strategy of capital operation.

The development of the stock market provides the market capitalisation of listed companies. Changes in the market capitalisation of a listed company not only reflect the level of the company's net assets but also its future earning power. Changes in market capitalisation are continuous, so that investors, through continuous observation, can judge a company's operating condition more accurately, and through the buying and selling of shares can bring external market pressure to bear on a company's management. In addition, the development of the stock market promotes the subdivision of property rights within the firm, helps the restructuring of assets of listed companies, and enables economic resources to flow and be consolidated efficiently.

The common premise of the four functions is that "prices carry information," and this is precisely where contemporary debate is most heated. Item (2) depends on the no-arbitrage of section 3 and on the formation of expectations in section 5: if prices are dominated by short-term funds, leverage and liquidity shocks, they are no longer a reliable source of discount rates, but become an amplifier of false signals — the abnormal volatility of the Chinese stock market in June–July 2015 (forced liquidation of off-exchange margin lending triggering a downward spiral, with discussion of stabilisation mechanisms and of a stabilisation fund arrangement continuing to the present) and the cascading forced liquidation of small- and micro-cap stocks and quant strategies in early 2024 are two demonstrations of the same mechanism. Item (3) carries an additional institutional condition in China: the withdrawal of commercial banks from the exchanges and the establishment of the interbank market in 1996–1997 were precisely to give open market operations and interest rate transmission an institutional carrier (chapter 7, monetary policy), while the inclusion of government bond buying and selling in the policy toolkit in 2024 and the discussion around 2025 of a "Chinese version of yield curve management" are attempts to extend this function to the long end. ### What is the methodological background of financial engineering? And why does it use "no arbitrage" rather than supply-and-demand pricing?

Financial engineering refers to the process by which, since the 1980s, the financial sector has combined modern financial theory with engineering methods and, in view of the different risk preferences of investors, has creatively designed, developed and applied new financial products.

Financial engineering is an emerging interdisciplinary field, its theory and methods being founded on modern financial theory, information technology and engineering methods.

- (1) The portfolio theory proposed by Markowitz in 1952 defined risk and return from a mathematical angle and brought mathematical tools into financial research;

In 1958 Modigliani and Miller proposed the MM theorem, providing modern financial theory with a "no-arbitrage equilibrium" method distinct from the supply-and-demand analysis of traditional economics. By "no-arbitrage equilibrium" is meant: when the price of some asset violates the law of one price of "equal value, equal price," the market is in an off-equilibrium state and an arbitrage opportunity arises, and the arbitrage motives of market participants drive the market to equilibrium; in an equilibrium state of the market no arbitrage opportunity exists, whence the name "no-arbitrage equilibrium." The basic method of financial analysis is to combine the "position" in some financial asset (that is, a holding or a shortfall in some asset) with positions in other financial assets in the market, and to construct a combined position which, when the market is in equilibrium, cannot generate a profit without bearing risk, and from this to compute the value of that position in market equilibrium, that is, its equilibrium price.

- (2) The development of information technology has provided the necessary material foundation for financial engineering, for example the spread of computers and the improvement of large-scale computation and data processing

ability, which has provided effective means for developing and implementing the various new financial products and for solving financial and accounting problems, in the practical application of portfolio theory and in financial engineering; the development of communications technology has enabled information to spread rapidly nationally and globally, and has knitted the financial markets into one body. All of this has greatly lowered the transaction costs of financial markets and raised the efficiency of the market.

- (3) Under the support of the above theoretical and technical conditions, "financial engineers," in view of the different risk preferences and risk-bearing capacities of market investors, have drawn on modern engineering methods to creatively design various new financial instruments, giving them a specific liquidity and a specific risk-return combination.

The reason "no arbitrage" has been able to replace supply-and-demand analysis as the default method of pricing is hard: most financial instruments have no independent supply and demand curves — for a structured product sliced from several bonds, its "demand" is merely the spread of it relative to an already tradable portfolio of assets, and its "supply" can be replicated at will, so that the only price that holds up is the price at which no riskless profit can be replicated. Supply and demand determine the non-replicable parts, such as the risk premium and the liquidity premium (exactly the objects the CAPM and the arbitrage pricing theory set out to handle), while no arbitrage determines the replicable part — the coexistence of the two pricing logics is the boundary that is easiest to lose when reading this chapter. The boundary of this methodology was exposed in 2007–2008: no-arbitrage pricing assumes that the replicating portfolio can be continuously rebalanced, and assumes that usable repurchase and collateral exist (that is, that there is no friction), while in the crisis the shortage of collateral and the rules on margin broke "equal value, equal price" by a wide margin (differences in trading prices among different bonds of the same issuer, divergences between credit default swaps and cash spreads). A more direct methodological criticism is that the parameters of the models (default correlation, the stability of volatility) are themselves estimated from history, whereas structured products fail precisely in states that history does not cover.

## Limits and what follows

- **The pricing part of this chapter stops at the CAPM, whereas what follows it is already a scene of multiple competition.** The failure of the single-factor model in cross-section (see the previous section) prompted factor models, consumption-based pricing and behavioural finance each to grow: Shiller (1981) put excess volatility on the table with the finding that "the volatility of real dividends and of stock prices far exceeds what the dividends subsequently realised can explain"; the prospect theory of Kahneman and Tversky (1979) and the behavioural finance research advanced by Thaler (Richard Thaler, awarded the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 2017) explain the psychological mechanisms — the disposition effect, loss aversion, overconfidence — by which prices depart from their no-arbitrage value; and Fama and French (1996) maintain that most anomalies can be explained by risk compensation for several factors, indicating that some "anomalies" may merely be the product of data mining. The more robust current consensus is that factor models are used as tools in asset management and in investment practice, but that no one any longer takes them as a description of equilibrium. At the same time the structure of the market itself has changed — after 2000 the share of electronic trading rose above eighty per cent, and high-frequency market making, dark pools, the "flash crash" of the American stock market on 6 May 2010 and the confrontation between retail investors and short-selling funds in GameStop (GameStop) in January 2021 have together made "who supplies liquidity, and who amplifies a crash" new objects of study, none of which lies within the framework of this chapter.
- **The institutional face of the derivatives market was rewritten after 2008, and the concepts of this chapter need to be re-read under the new rules.** Over-the-counter interest rate derivatives before the crisis were private agreements with no central counterparty and no margin (according to Bank for International Settlements statistics the global notional amount of over-the-counter derivatives still reached the order of 600 trillion dollars at the end of 2022, more than eighty per cent of it being interest rate products), and after the Pittsburgh Summit of 2009 moved towards standardised centralised clearing, margin and trade reporting (Title VII in the United States, EMIR in the European Union, and in China the centralised clearing pilots in the interbank market from 2014 and the related authorisations in the 2023 draft of the *Financial Stability Law*). Benchmark reform is another matter: LIBOR was retired because of manipulation scandals (successively exposed from 2012, with several banks fined billions of dollars), and secured overnight rates such as SOFR took over as the reference for new floating-rate debt and interest rate swaps (completed in phases between 2021 and 2023), which turned the "benchmark fallback" clauses of existing contracts overnight from a technical detail into a legal and audit problem. After the "liquidity crunch" of June 2013 in China, the abnormal volatility of the stock market in 2015 and the bond-market nominee-holding incidents of 2016 (the redemption risk triggered at the end of December 2016 by the "forged chop of Guohai Securities" nominee-holding case), trade reporting, nominee holding and limits on leverage in the interbank and bond markets were likewise tightened sharply — the judgement "the more standardised the financial instrument, the better," which this chapter takes for granted, must under a regulatory view be qualified by one further sentence: stand
- **"Financial deepening necessarily raises efficiency" requires conditions.** The theory of financial repression by McKinnon (1973) and Shaw (1973) was once the prescription for market building in developing countries (liberalise interest rates, develop direct finance), and the results given by the liberalisation of interest rates in South Korea in the mid-1980s, the loosening of the capital market in Japan in the 1980s and the liberalisation practice of Latin America in the 1970s and 1980s differed enormously: the liberalisation in Chile came with a banking crisis in 1981–1982, the experiment of Argentina in the late 1970s ended in inflation and currency substitution, whereas South Korea achieved high growth while maintaining the direction of credit. Contemporary explanations reverse the order: what market efficiency depends on is the institutional stock of information disclosure, bankruptcy enforcement, accounting and investor protection (the law-and-finance series by La Porta and others from 1998, and the literature from the 2000s on enforcement capacity and contract enforcement), rather than "liberalisation is effective in itself." This explains why the reform towards a registration system in China between 2019 and 2023 was advanced together with the supporting measures for collective litigation and delisting, and not, as the early liberalisation proposals suggested, by opening up only the issuance side.
- **A special structure of the Chinese financial market is still in operation: the tiering of the market is determined by market access for financial institutions, not by price.** The segmentation between the exchange market and the interbank market (the two-market structure formed after commercial banks withdrew from the exchanges in 1997), the afterimage of the "two-track interest rate" in the credit market (transmission from policy rate to market rate to lending rate is still being opened up, with the LPR reform of 2019 and the self-disciplined adjustment of deposit rates from 2024), the credit spreads among bond issuers being chronically narrow (the pricing of risk between interest-rate bonds, the credit bonds of state-owned enterprises and the bonds of private enterprises having only gradually opened up after 2018), and the market-based handling of local-government financing vehicle and property developer debt risk (the impact of the default events of Yongmei, Huachen and others in 2018–2020 on regional spreads and refinancing) all show that the mechanism of "prices guiding the allocation of resources" described in this chapter is only partially realised in China. When reading the four functions of this chapter, they must be taken as criteria of judgement rather than a description of the present state, in order not to reach the conclusion that "the functions are already in place."
