# Chapter 6. The financial regulatory system

> **The core question of this chapter**: why must the financial industry be regulated? What are the principles and content of financial regulation? How is safety balanced against efficiency?

## The arc of this chapter

The map of financial regulation was not designed; it was drawn by one crisis after another. After nearly ten thousand banks failed in the United States in 1929–1933 there came the Glass–Steagall Act, with its separation of business lines and federal deposit insurance; after the German bank Herstatt was wound up after the market closed in 1974, central banks everywhere realised for the first time that a cross-border bank failure could drag the whole payment system under, and the Basel Committee was founded that same year; in 1988 the Latin American debt crisis and the pressure of international competition among banks that could no longer make ends meet forced out the first Basel Accord, which used a single 8% capital adequacy ratio to level standards across countries; in the 1990s successive huge losses from derivatives (Bank of Barings collapsed in 1995) plus the Asian financial crisis produced the New Basel Accord, which breaks risk down in finer detail and adds two new pillars. So this chapter's two theories (the social interest theory and the financial risk theory) are not abstract justifications but after-the-fact summaries of the costs above; and the two accords are not abstract standards but products of specific crises. Only once that is understood can you read Chapter 4's "why banks are special" and Chapter 7's reason for monetary policy having to depend on a sound banking system.

## Core concepts

### The cost of financial regulation

The cost of financial regulation divides roughly into two parts, explicit costs and implicit costs. Generally the stricter the financial regulation, the higher its cost.
The costs of financial regulation include:
- (1) Enforcement costs.
	It refers to the costs incurred by the financial regulatory authority in the actual course of carrying out regulation.
	It appears as the administrative budget of the regulatory body, that is the explicit or direct cost mentioned above.
- (2) Compliance costs.
	It refers to the extra cost and loss that financial institutions bear in order to meet regulatory requirements, usually an implicit cost.
	It appears mainly as the efficiency loss financial institutions suffer when complying with regulatory rules. For example, lowering the efficiency of using funds in order to meet statutory reserve requirements; or restricting the development of new products and the improvement of service quality because regulation suppresses financial innovation.

The reason these two kinds of cost are counted separately is that they are politically asymmetrical: enforcement costs are written in the regulator's budget and compliance costs show up on bank balance sheets, and both can be computed clearly and attract opposition; whereas the benefit of regulation is a "crisis that did not happen," which is invisible and cannot be credited to anyone. This asymmetry explains why regulation keeps swinging between tightening and loosening — after a crisis the costs are forgotten and regulation is tightened, and after a quiet period the costs become visible and regulation relaxes (see "Limits and what follows" at the end of this chapter).

## Problems and analysis

### What basic principles should financial regulation follow?

The basic principles of financial regulation are mainly:

- (1) The principle of management by law.
- (2) The principle of reasonable and moderate competition — the focus of regulation should lie in protecting, sustaining, cultivating and creating a fair, efficient, moderate and orderly competitive environment.
- (3) The principle of combining self-restraint with external compulsion.
- (4) The principle of combining security and stability with economic efficiency. In addition, financial regulation should pay attention to adapting to a changed market environment and promptly adjusting outdated regulatory content, methods and instruments.

### How does the social interest theory justify the necessity of financial regulation?

The social interest theory holds that the basic starting point of financial regulation is first of all to safeguard the interests of the public at large, and the high dispersion of the public's interests determines that this duty can only be discharged by an institution authorised by the state.
Historical experience shows that, other things being equal, a bank can increase its profitability by expanding its assets and liabilities and by enlarging the ratio of assets to capital. This inevitably comes with increased risk. But since not all of the risk cost is borne by the bank itself but by the whole financial system and even the whole social and economic system, the bank has ample motive to raise its profitability by increasing risk. If necessary regulation and restriction are not imposed, the public's interests are very likely to be harmed.

It can also be summarised this way:
Because market defects exist, it is necessary for government, representing the public interest, to intervene in economic life to a certain extent, correcting or eliminating market defects through controls so as to raise the efficiency of social resource allocation and reduce the loss of social welfare.

### How does the financial risk theory justify the necessity of financial regulation?

The financial risk theory holds that the character of financial risk determines that regulation must be imposed, so as to ensure the safety and stability of the whole financial system.

- (1) A bank's capital accounts for only a small proportion, and large amounts of asset business must be supported by liabilities.
	In the course of its operations, changes in factors such as interest rates, exchange rates, liability structure and borrowers' ability to repay mean the banking industry is constantly facing all manner of risks and becomes a centre where risk concentrates. Moreover the impulse of financial institutions to expand assets blindly in pursuit of higher returns further aggravates the high risk and inherent instability of the financial industry. When the public loses confidence in it and runs on its deposits, the bank runs into a payment crisis or even bankruptcy.

- (2) The financial industry is subject to the chain reaction of payment crises.
	Under market economy conditions all strata of society and all sectors of the national economy are closely linked through creditor and debtor relations. Hence a problem at any link in the financial system can very easily cause a chain reaction and thereby trigger a widespread financial crisis. Going further, one country's financial crisis affects other countries and may trigger regional or even world-wide financial turmoil.

- (3) The risks of the financial system directly affect the stability of the monetary system and the macro economy.

## Synthetic essay

### What problem was the Basel Accord issued to solve? What does it prescribe?

In July 1988 the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision adopted *Agreement on the Harmonisation of Capital Measurement and Capital Standards*, the famous Basel Accord.

- (1) The purpose of the Basel Accord

	- ① By setting the ratio of a bank's capital to its assets, and fixing the calculation method and standards, to strengthen the healthy development of the international banking system;
	- ② To set unified standards in order to eliminate unfair competition among the banks of different countries in the international financial market.

- (2) The main content of the Basel Accord

	① On the composition of capital.
		Bank capital is divided into two tiers, core capital and supplementary capital:
		- Tier 1 core capital comprises share capital and disclosed reserves, and this part must amount to at least 50% of total capital;
		- Tier 2 supplementary capital comprises undisclosed reserves, asset revaluation reserves, and general or loan-loss provisions.
	
	② On the calculation of risk weighting.
		The Accord sets risk measurement standards for the various assets on the balance sheet and each off-balance-sheet item, and links capital to the risk-weighted computation, so as to assess the appropriate size of capital a bank should hold.
	
	③ On the target standard ratio.
		The Accord requires banks to build up and adjust the required capital base gradually over a five-year transition period. By the end of 1992 the standard ratio of bank capital to risk-weighted assets was to be 8%, of which core capital at least 4%. The influence of this Accord is wide and far-reaching: since its issue, not only has capital regulation of transnational banks had to follow the standards the Accord prescribes, but within individual countries their monetary authorities have also required banks to observe this norm, in some cases making it explicit in the form of legislation.

### Why did the New Basel Accord have to replace the 1988 standard? What does each of the three pillars govern?

On 16 January 2001 the Basel Committee released the second draft of the new capital accord and again sought opinion worldwide from the banking industry and regulatory bodies. Later that year it was decided that the draft would be finalised in 2002 and implemented in 2005, comprehensively replacing the 1988 Basel Accord and becoming the newest rule for banks in various countries to manage risk in the new international financial environment.

- (1) The background to the New Basel Accord

Since the 1990s the operating environment and regulatory environment of international banking have changed greatly, mainly in three respects.

- ① The method used in the Basel Accord for fixing risk weights met new challenges.
	This shows itself in the fact that while credit risk still exists, the destructive force of market risk and operational risk on banking has grown increasingly visible. With bank capital to risk assets at broadly normal ratios, market risk centred on financial derivatives trading occurred frequently, inducing several major bank failures and huge losses in international banking. The Basel Accord considered mainly credit risk and took insufficient account of market and operational risk.

- ② The warning of crises.
	The outbreak of the Asian financial crisis and the financial turmoil triggered by its spread made financial regulatory authorities and international banking feel acutely that revising the current international financial regulatory standards could no longer wait.
	- On the one hand, the previous requirements for capital adequacy had to be improved as soon as possible;
	- on the other, international cooperation in financial regulation had to be strengthened, so as to maintain the stability of the international financial system.

- ③ Technical feasibility.
	Both the academic community and the banking industry itself had done extensive detailed exploratory work on measuring and pricing banking risk, and had built some fairly scientific and feasible mathematical models. The emergence of modern risk quantification models made it technically possible for the Basel Committee to draw up a new capital framework. The new draft accord is far more complex than the 1988 Basel Accord, but also more comprehensive. It ties the assessment of capital adequacy more closely to the risks a bank faces, so that it can better reflect a bank's true risk position. The new accord emphasises not only the importance of the capital adequacy standard but also, through three mutually complementary "pillars," aims to raise the safety and stability of the financial system effectively.

- (2) The three pillars of the New Basel Accord

- ① Pillar One — minimum capital requirements.
	Minimum capital requirements consist of three basic elements: the definition of capital subject to regulatory constraints, risk-weighted assets, and the minimum ratio of capital to risk-weighted assets. Of these the definition of capital and the 8% minimum capital ratio did not change. But on the calculation of risk-weighted assets, the new accord, on the basis of the previous consideration of credit risk alone, goes further to consider market risk and operational risk.

- ② Pillar Two — supervision by regulatory authorities.
	This is to ensure that each bank establishes rational and effective internal assessment procedures for judging its own risk position, and on that basis assesses whether its capital is adequate. Regulatory authorities supervise and inspect factors such as the bank's risk management and resolution situation, the handling of inter-relationships among different risks, the nature of the markets it operates in, and the validity and reliability of its income, so as to judge comprehensively whether the bank's capital is adequate.

- ③ Pillar Three — market discipline.
	The core of market discipline is disclosure. The effectiveness of market discipline depends directly on how sound the disclosure system is; only by establishing a sound information disclosure system for banking can market participants estimate a bank's risk management position and solvency. To raise the effectiveness of market discipline the Basel Committee devoted itself to issuing a uniform disclosure framework.

People generally supported the new capital accord framework and favoured adopting a more risk-sensitive capital management regime. But it was also widely held that the new accord was too complex to implement in a unified way immediately. In fact countries implemented it at different times. Considering that China is a developing country, especially one in the process of transitioning from a planned to a market economy, the China Banking Regulatory Commission decided to postpone implementation of the new capital accord until 2010.

## Limits and what follows

- **Why regulation exists, theory has not agreed on.** This chapter's social interest theory and financial risk theory both belong to the "public interest theory" tradition — regulation exists to correct market defects. Stigler's (1971) regulatory economics offers the opposite picture: regulators get captured by the industry they regulate, and the rules often serve the incumbents rather than the public. Deposit insurance best shows both sides being partly right: when it was born in 1934 it did stop the run, but it also removed depositors' motive to watch the bank and shifted losses ultimately onto taxpayers, and the "too big to fail" bind of 2008 is the price of that design.
- **The efficacy of the Basel Accords has been tested by reality over and over, and over and over slapped.** The 1988 Accord levelled standards with an 8% that did not distinguish asset quality, and banks soon learned "regulatory arbitrage": keep the low-risk-weight assets for their own book, securitise the high-risk assets out off-balance-sheet; the 2004 New Accord, to raise risk sensitivity, allowed banks to use internal rating models, which gave legitimacy to optimistic models — and the structured products and off-balance-sheet vehicles that failed in the crisis were precisely the parts with the lowest capital charges. Kane's "dialogue of regulation" — regulate, innovate to evade, re-regulate — was verified to the letter here.
- **The timeline of the texts needs updating.** The new accord was finally finalised in June 2004 and originally scheduled for end-2006 implementation, then postponed again and again after the crisis; the Basel III Accord reached after 2008 (approved at the G20 Seoul summit in November 2010) no longer talks only about the quantity of capital but proposes a Common Equity Tier 1 ratio of 4.5%, a conservation buffer of 2.5% and a countercyclical buffer of 0–2.5%, and adds a leverage ratio and a liquidity coverage ratio and a net stable funding ratio. China's corresponding landing was the *Regulations on Capital Management of Commercial Banks* issued by the former CBRC in 2012 and effective from January 2013, after which the macroprudential assessment (MPA), regulation of systemically important banks and governance of shadow banking all went beyond this chapter's capital-adequacy-centred framework.
