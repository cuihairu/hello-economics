# Chapter 4. The financial intermediary system

> **The core question of this chapter**: What kinds of financial intermediaries are there? How do commercial banks operate? Why are a central bank and a lender of last resort needed?

## The arc of this chapter

The institutions discussed in this chapter did not appear at the same time, and their reasons for coming into being were different; laying out the timeline makes it easy to see why each of the concepts that follow matters. The earliest forms of commercial bank appeared in the tradition of goldsmiths and Italian money houses of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, doing the business of "taking deposits and making loans" that any direct finance could equally well have provided; what actually pushed banking to the centre of the economy was the Industrial Revolution — as the scale of enterprises and the maturity of loans both lengthened, there was a need for a class of institutions able to hold client relationships over the long term and to supervise borrowers. Gerschenkron's (1962) study of late-developing countries gives the strongest version of the argument: in Germany, Japan and Russia, where capital markets did not yet exist, banks were not the supplement to the market but the market's creator — they made choices on behalf of inexperienced households, supervised on behalf of investors who could not diversify, and even held shares in industrial and commercial enterprises. Central banks came much later and far more by accident: the Bank of England was founded in 1694 to raise money for the war against France, and for more than a century afterwards it remained an ordinary joint-stock bank, until the four crises of 1825, 1847, 1857 and 1866 forced it to act as the mainstay of other banks, whereupon the "lender of last resort" settled from practice into duty (Bagehot set out the rule in *Lombard Street*, 1873); the Federal Reserve came later still (the *Federal Reserve Act* of 1913), and China not until the People's Bank of China was founded in 1948 and took on the exclusive central banking function in 1984. Deposit insurance and separated business were the products of a single crisis: after nearly ten thousand US banks failed between 1929 and 1933, the *Banking Act* of 1933 at once established the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC, opened in 1934) and the separation principle set by *Glass–Steagall* — the first so that depositors would no longer have to supervise banks, the second so that banks would no longer take deposits to gamble on securities. What is interesting is that more than forty years later both legs were withdrawn in reverse: the innovation spawned by interest-rate volatility and regulatory evasion (see the section "Financial innovation" in this chapter) rendered the 1933 architecture obsolete, and the United States liberalized step by step between 1986 and 1999 (the *Financial Services Modernization Act* of 1999 formally abolishing the separation limits), while Britain and Switzerland had never practised strict separation; after 2008 there was again a partial retreat (the US Volcker Rule of 2010, the retail-wholesale ring-fencing recommended by the UK Vickers Committee in 2013). China's restructuring of the system had two nodes, 1994 and 1998–2005: in 1994 the three policy banks were founded, separating policy business from the commercial banks; in 1998 special government bonds of 270 billion yuan were issued to replenish the capital of the four large banks, and the four major asset management companies were set up to strip out about 1.4 trillion yuan of non-performing assets, followed by recapitalization, restructuring and listing in 2003–2005 — without these two steps, "the commercial bank as an autonomously operating enterprise," as this chapter has it, would have been a matter of name only in China. Asset securitization (first undertaken by Ginnie Mae in 1970, extended to credit cards and auto loans in the 1980s) and off-balance-sheet business (the eurocurrency market of the 1960s, loan commitments and securitization services in the 1980s) are the two peaks of this "getting round the balance sheet" main line, and precisely the part that went wrong in the 2008 crisis. Read this line through, and the six concepts of this chapter stop being a row of parallel nouns and become a list of crises.

## Core concepts

### The financial institutional system

The financial institutional system is the overall structure of the various institutions performing different functions in the financial field of a country. The standard classification takes as its criteria whether an institution may take deposits and whether it is subject to prudential supervision: one class is depository institutions, including central banks, commercial banks, policy banks, savings and loan institutions and credit cooperatives; the other is non-depository financial institutions, including securities companies, fund management companies, insurance companies, pension funds, trust and investment companies, financial leasing companies, finance companies, consumer finance companies and so on; besides these there are market infrastructures such as regulators, exchanges and clearing and settlement institutions. China's present structure can be summarized as "one bank, one bureau, one commission" together with policy banks, the large state-owned commercial banks, joint-stock banks, city commercial banks and rural commercial banks, and the various non-bank institutions: after the restructuring of state organs in 2023, the National Financial Regulatory Administration took over the duties of the former banking and insurance regulator, while the China Securities Regulatory Commission retains supervision of capital markets. The reason the overall structure must be dealt with first is that the four concepts later in this chapter (off-balance-sheet business, asset securitization, universal banks, deposit insurance) all concern "boundaries between institutions"; unless one knows in advance where the boundaries are drawn, one cannot see which line these innovations are crossing. The challenge posed by Gurley and Shaw (1960) is directly relevant: they argued that many of the differences between banks and non-bank institutions are merely historical regulatory arrangements, and that once regulation is relaxed the boundary between "intermediation" and "market" blurs — a claim verified both in America after 1980 and in China after 2012, and the premise on which the very concept of "shadow banking" rests.

### Off-balance-sheet business

Off-balance-sheet business refers to any business that is not entered in the bank's balance sheet and does not affect the totals of assets and liabilities.
In the broad sense, off-balance-sheet business comprises both the traditional intermediary business and some risky business produced by financial innovation, such as swaps, options, futures, forward rate agreements, note issuance facilities, loan commitments, standby letters of credit and the like.
Off-balance-sheet business as usually referred to means specifically the latter, risky kind — off-balance-sheet business in the narrow sense.

The two kinds must be kept apart, because in the eyes of the regulator they are wholly different things. Intermediary business such as settlement, agency and custody earns fees and creates no contingent claims or debts; loan commitments, note issuance facilities, standby letters of credit and the notional principal of derivatives, by contrast, become on-balance-sheet assets or liabilities under certain conditions — they are "a balance sheet that has not yet happened." This is precisely the economic reason off-balance-sheet business exists: the bank sells the risk commitment out and earns the fee back, without occupying capital or reserves. The trouble also lies exactly here. When the Continental Illinois bank in the United States went wrong in 1984, regulators only then discovered how enormous its off-balance-sheet positions were; in 2007–2008, the structured investment vehicles (SIVs) and liquidity facility commitments that a Western bank had previously moved onto the balance sheet were forced back onto the balance sheet, invalidating the capital adequacy ratio on the books overnight — the words "off balance sheet" form no barrier in a panic. Hence the two new indicators of *Basel III* in 2010 (a leverage ratio that disregards risk weights and brings off-balance-sheet exposures in through credit conversion factors; and a liquidity coverage ratio requirement to hold down reliance on wholesale funding) are in fact remedies designed against this very concept, and this is where this chapter meets chapter 6 on financial regulation. China's corresponding experience is the bank wealth management and interbank special vehicles of 2013–2016: banks moved credit off the balance sheet through wealth management products, entrusted loans and trust beneficial rights, getting round the loan-to-deposit ratio, sectoral restrictions and capital occupation; the rectification of 2017 and the *Guiding Opinions on Regulating the Asset Management Business of Financial Institutions* (the new asset management rules) of 2018, which broke rigid redemption and restricted fund pools and layering, were a closing-in on that same logic.

### Asset securitization

Asset securitization is a form of financing in which existing credit assets are pooled together and re-divided into securities that are then resold to investors in the market, so that the assets disappear from the balance sheet of the original holder. As the main holders of direct credit assets, commercial banks and savings and loan institutions are best suited to conduct such business. The essential meaning of asset securitization is the process of converting loans or receivables into negotiable financial instruments.
The asset securitization system comprises the following: ordinary asset-backed securities, credit card-backed securities, asset-backed debt, asset-backed preferred shares and asset-backed commercial paper.

The problem securitization is meant to solve is quite concrete: a bank's loans are long-term, non-tradable assets locked into the borrower relationship, whereas the funding it needs must be free to flow back at any time. Selling the loans recovers funds, releases capital occupation and spreads concentration risk across the market. The first modern securitization product was the pass-through certificate issued by Ginnie Mae (GNMA) in the United States in 1970 (its underlying assets were government-guaranteed mortgage loans); in 1977 Fannie Mae entered the Federal National Mortgage Association market, and in 1985 the first securitization with auto loans as the underlying asset appeared (spearheaded by bank credit card and car rental receivables), after which the field extended to credit card receivables, student loans, equipment leasing and accounts receivable. Whether its mechanism holds depends on one thing: whether investors can see clearly the cash flows and default characteristics of the underlying assets. For this the designers built three components — a true sale with bankruptcy remoteness (loading the assets into a special purpose vehicle so that the originator's failure does not affect the cash flows), tranching (senior, mezzanine and junior tranches, cutting the order of losses to holders of different risk appetites), and credit enhancement such as overcollateralization and spread accounts. The failure of 2007–2008 occurred precisely where all three components broke down together: tranching was misread as "the senior tranche is always safe," because the correlation among the underlying loans was disregarded; bankruptcy remoteness was eroded by the implicit liquidity support the originators kept providing; and most crucially, the quality of the subprime loans themselves was no longer verified by anyone through layer upon layer of resale. The "originate-to-distribute" model shifted the lender's interest from "can the borrower repay?" to "can this loan be sold?" — this is the cost of the concept in this chapter that must be remembered. China's credit asset securitization was piloted in 2005 by the China Development Bank (CLOs) and the China Construction Bank (residential mortgage loans), suspended in 2008 because of the crisis and resumed in 2012; since then it has run mainly on "filing plus the interbank market," and underlying assets have extended to corporate receivables, finance leasing, consumer finance and infrastructure tolling rights; in the stock structure of recent years, residential mortgage loans and entrusted loans remain the main varieties — the difference from the United States being that Chinese securitization serves capital-occupation management more, rather than serving housing-affordability policy as it does in America.

### The universal commercial bank

A universal commercial bank is one in which the commercial bank may conduct every kind of financial business, including deposits and loans of every maturity and type, the buying and selling of every kind of security, and financial business such as trust and payment settlement.

Standing opposite it is the functionally specialized form (separated business): the law confines financial institutions to distinct categories, each with its own proper business, and commercial banks may not underwrite securities or trade them for their own account. The dispute between the two concepts is not a matter of academic taste but the result of three layers of experience. The first is the long practice of Germany and Switzerland: universal banks, by holding shares in industrial and commercial enterprises and sending directors to take part in governance, successfully mobilized capital for the late-developing industrial countries in the late nineteenth century; but the failure of the Danat-Bank in 1931 showed that when a bank itself falls into difficulty, the risk between it and the enterprises amplifies in both directions. The second is America's judgement of 1933: congressional hearings attributed part of the credit contraction of the Great Depression to investment banks underwriting poor securities with depositors' money, and the *Glass–Steagall Act* therefore forbade member banks to underwrite or deal in securities, while at the same time establishing deposit insurance. The third is the reverse withdrawal after 1980: interest-rate volatility compressed bank margins (see "the direct causes of financial innovation" below), and the separation limits left banks unable to provide the full set of services clients needed; Japan pushed universalization in the 1970s and 1980s through the "comprehensive research institutes of finance," and the *Financial Services Modernization Act* of 1999 in the United States abolished the separation clauses and allowed financial holding companies to hold banking, securities and insurance business at once. The attitude after 2008 was again not one-sided: the US Volcker Rule (passed in 2010, phased in from 2013 to 2015) restricts banks' proprietary trading and their holdings in private equity and hedge funds, the UK Vickers Committee (2011–2013) recommended separating retail deposit business from wholesale investment banking activity at the level of the legal entity (ring-fencing), while Germany kept the universal model unchanged. That is, the question "separated or mixed, which is better" has not in fact been settled — it has only been restated as a more precise one: how much market risk should retail banks, protected by deposit insurance and a lender of last resort, take on. China's present arrangement is "separated business and separated supervision as the main rule, with pilots in financial holding companies and comprehensive operation coexisting," and the concern behind this choice is exactly the question above.

### The lender of last resort

The lender of last resort (also called the ultimate lender) refers to the responsibility of accommodation that the central bank ought to discharge in a moment of crisis — it should meet the demand for high-powered money so as to prevent the contraction of the money stock caused by panic.
The role of the lender of last resort:
- first, to support commercial banks and other financial institutions that have fallen into difficulty over the turnover of funds, so as to prevent a bank run from spreading and ultimately bringing down the whole banking system;
- second, by providing commercial banks with short-term financial accommodation, to adjust the scale of credit and the money supply and thereby transmit and implement the intent of macroeconomic control.

All the difficulty of this concept lies in "how to rescue," not "whether to rescue." The three rules Bagehot gave in *Lombard Street* (1873) remain to this day the baseline of discussion: lend in a panic, against good collateral, at a penal rate of interest, and restrict the objects of assistance to institutions that are temporarily short of liquidity rather than insolvent. Their deeper point is that they handle two failure modes at once: not lending lets a liquidity panic turn into a solvency crisis (in 1907 the United States relied on the personal coordination of J. P. Morgan, and in 1930–1931 the Federal Reserve watched the wave of bank failures without acting), while lending too freely removes the incentive to prudence from banks and depositors alike. The second failure mode is exactly the main line of academic criticism of the lender of last resort since Friedman and Schwartz (1963), and "too big to fail" in 2008 turned it into a policy issue — rescue spares creditors and counterparties from loss, so the market never prices the high-risk liabilities of large banks. The approach after the crisis was not to give blanket rescue but to supply a whole set of substitute arrangements: the orderly liquidation authority (OLA) established by the *Dodd–Frank Act* of 2010 in the United States, and from 2013 the Federal Reserve's reform making the discount window standing (the Primary Dealer Credit Facility, PDCF, activated in March 2020); from 2014 emergency liquidity assistance (ELA) in the euro area requires the approval of the European Central Bank; and the EU *Bank Recovery and Resolution Directive* (BRRD, 2014) established bail-in in preference to external recapitalization. China's corresponding institution is that the People's Bank of China has discharged the function since the 2000s through tools such as relending and temporary liquidity facilities, and in the *Financial Stability Law (draft)* of 2022 the "lender of last resort" clause was written into statute (the bill had still not been passed by 2024, and the institution is still under construction); the takeover of BaoShang Bank in 2019, with redemption by tranches ending rigid redemption of interbank claims, was a genuine resolution drill. As for why the boundary of "who qualifies for help" keeps moving, see chapter 6 on the financial regulatory system and the section on deposit insurance in this chapter.

### The deposit insurance system

The deposit insurance system is an institutional arrangement that protects depositors' interests and stabilizes the financial system.
Under this institutional arrangement, deposit-taking financial institutions take out insurance with the deposit insurance agency, at the prescribed premium rate and according to the amount of deposits they take; when a deposit-taking institution goes bankrupt and cannot meet depositors' withdrawal demands, the deposit insurance agency bears the responsibility of paying the statutory insurance money.

What this institution is meant to solve is a problem of game theory: a run can kill in a single day a bank that is not in fact bankrupt, while each depositor's individually rational choice is to withdraw first — Diamond and Dybvig (1983) used this model to show that the design of the deposit contract, "deposit first, withdraw later, redeemable at any time," itself contains multiple equilibria, and both running and not running are equilibria. The function of deposit insurance is to delete the panic equilibrium outright: if small deposits are certain of repayment in full, depositors have no reason to queue at midnight. Its cost is equally clear: depositors thereafter have no motive to supervise the bank, and risk is transferred to the insurance agency and to whoever ultimately pays, so it must be designed together with prudential supervision, risk-differentiated premium rates and limited repayment (this is why this chapter and chapter 6 cannot be read apart). The concrete parameters: legislation in the United States in 1933, the founding of the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation in 1934, and the *Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act* of 1980 raising the insured limit from 40,000 to 100,000 dollars; after the temporary full guarantee of 2008 the limit was made permanent at 250,000 dollars in the *Dodd–Frank Act* of 2010, and the failures of Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank in 2023 reopened the argument over whether unlimited protection for small-bank depositors counts as a new implicit guarantee (the Treasury covered the full amount on the grounds of a "systemic risk exception" while at the same time recanting and not rescuing shareholders and senior creditors); under the EU single guarantee mechanism the standard insured amount is 100,000 euros. China's path is that the *Deposit Insurance Regulation* came into force on 1 May 2015, with a maximum payout limit of 500,000 yuan (covering about 99.6% of deposit accounts), differentiated premiums based on a benchmark rate, and the Deposit Insurance Fund Management Co., Ltd. (founded in 2019) bearing the functions of early correction and risk resolution; the two cases of BaoShang Bank in 2019 and the Henan village and township banks in 2022 tested precisely who bears the large amounts and "non-deposit" products beyond that limit.

## Problems and analysis

### What sets a commercial bank apart from an ordinary enterprise?

What distinguishes the commercial bank from the ordinary industrial or commercial enterprise lies in the content of its specific operating activities and its functional role.

- (1) Acting as a credit intermediary between enterprises

By taking deposits, the bank mobilizes and pools the idle monetary capital of society, and then, through lending or investment, supplies this monetary capital for use by the capitalists who operate industry; the bank becomes the intermediary between the lender and the borrower of monetary capital. This helps make full use of the existing monetary capital.

- (2) Acting as a payment intermediary between enterprises

By opening accounts for each enterprise, the bank acts as the middleman for monetary settlement and monetary receipts and payments between enterprises. Here the bank appears in the capacity of the enterprise's account keeper and cashier, thereby accelerating the turnover of enterprise capital.

- (3) Turning the savings and incomes of all social groups into capital

The savings and incomes originally destined for consumption are pooled through the bank and supplied to entrepreneurs for use as capital. This can enlarge the total of social capital.

- (4) Creating credit instruments of circulation

On the basis of the businesses above, the commercial bank becomes the creator of banknotes and deposit money. The right of commercial banks to issue banknotes was later abolished, but their creation of deposit money on the basis of organized cheque transfer plays an ever more important role in economic life.

Of the four, the real watershed is the fourth: only banks can create, by book entry, deposits that serve as a means of payment — which is also why the money multiplier of chapter 1 applies only to the banking system, while securities companies and funds do not count as "creating money" (they merely move existing purchasing power between different holders). Items (1) and (3) answer "why not direct finance": it is feasible in theory for enterprises to issue bonds, but households lack both information and bargaining power, and having the bank do the screening, supervision and maturity transformation (borrowing short and lending long) is cheaper in transaction costs. Item (2) is the most easily overlooked yet the most public-good-like — once payment and clearing stop, the claims and debts of the whole society cannot be settled, and it is precisely for this reason that the function ultimately fell into the hands of the central bank (see "The reasons the central bank came into being" in this chapter). Gerschenkron's (1962) study of late industrialization pushes these functions to the extreme: in an age when capital markets were absent, the bank did not merely "match" savings with investment but undertook the entrepreneur's function in its stead — and this is also the key background for understanding the role of China's state-owned banks before 1979, and why policy banks had to be established in 1994.

### Where does a bank's money come from, and where does it go?

- (1) A commercial bank's asset business

A commercial bank's asset business is the business of putting to use the monetary funds it has gathered through its liability business. This is the main channel for earning income. Of the funds gathered, apart from a certain portion that must be kept as cash and as deposits with the central bank to meet customers' demands for withdrawal and transfer settlement, the remainder is put to use mainly by way of discounting, lending and securities investment.

- (2) A commercial bank's liability business

A commercial bank's liability business is the business that forms its sources of funds. Its total sources of funds comprise two parts: own capital and outside funds taken in.

Own capital includes the share capital raised by issuing stock, together with reserve funds and undistributed profits. This part is also called equity capital. Of a deposit-money bank's sources of funds, own funds form a very small share, and yet they are the foundation for taking in outside funds. Outside funds consist mainly of deposits taken in, borrowing from the central bank, borrowing from other banks and from the money market, and issuing medium- and long-term financial bonds.

To read this table as "money comes in cheap and goes out dear" misses two points. The first is that the liability side decides what shock can kill a bank: for a bank relying mainly on wholesale funding such as interbank borrowing and bond issuance, its liabilities stop rolling over the very day the market's confidence in it disappears — Northern Rock in August 2007 (Britain's first bank to be rescued after its wholesale funding dried up, with depositors queuing at the door to withdraw cash), Lehman Brothers in September 2008, and Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023 (about two thirds of whose liabilities were deposits of an uninsured, wholesale character, and highly concentrated in a single industry) are all the same mechanism; a bank relying mainly on household time deposits is comparatively stable, which explains why "deposit competition" is never a small matter in any country's regulation. The second is that a small share of equity capital does not mean it is unimportant: the function of own capital is to absorb unexpected losses, and the *Basel Accord* of 1988 setting the core capital ratio at 4% and total capital at 8% is precisely an admission that the structure of "an institution with only 8% of its own money operating 92% of other people's money" is itself a source of systemic risk. The Chinese context adds one more thread: commercial banks long expanded their liability side on an extraordinary scale of branches and the implicit endorsement of government credit; the rapid rise in the share of interbank liabilities in 2014–2016 (some banks approaching the 1/3 ceiling later set by the regulator) and the jump in interbank certificate-of-deposit rates among small and medium banks after the BaoShang Bank affair of 2019 tell precisely how fragility on the liability side becomes tiered pricing across the whole system.

### Why do banks grow ever more "universal"? The division between the two operating models and the trend

- (1) The two types of commercial banking model

Classified by operating model, commercial banks divide into:
- Functionally specialized banks
- Universal banks

The basic feature of the functionally specialized form is that the law confines financial institutions to distinct categories, each with its own proper business: some specialize in long-term finance, some in short-term finance, some in the buying and selling of marketable securities, some in trust business, and so on.
The greatest difference between the commercial bank under the functionally specialized system and other financial institutions lies in:
- ① Only commercial banks can take demand deposits usable by cheque;
- ② Commercial banks generally take the granting of short-term industrial and commercial credit of under one year as their main business. The universal commercial bank, also called the comprehensive commercial bank, may conduct every kind of banking business, including deposits and loans of every maturity and type, and may even conduct a full range of securities business. The functionally specialized model is called the separated-business, separated-supervision model; correspondingly, the universal model is called the mixed-business, mixed-supervision model.

- (2) The trend in the development of commercial banking models

Since the 1970s, and especially in the past decade and more, accompanying the rapidly developing wave of financial liberalization and the unending emergence of financial innovation, in countries practising separated business the operation of commercial banks has grown steadily more universal and comprehensive.

The driving forces of this trend can be split into three, and each can be matched to a concrete institution: first, arbitrage on regulation (US Regulation Q capped deposit interest rates, and from 1978 inflation made the effective ceiling negative, so banks broke through by way of NOW accounts, automatic transfer of funds services (ATS), money market mutual funds and repurchase agreements, ultimately bringing about the *Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act* of 1980 and the completed removal of the ceiling in 1986); second, change on the demand side (once pension funds and mutual funds grew large, corporate financing turned to bonds and commercial paper, and banks were forced to extend into securities underwriting, dealing and advisory — the "disintermediation" of 1970s–80s America); third, technology and global competition (London's "Big Bang" of 1986 abolished fixed commissions and admitted foreign capital and universalization, pressing the other countries to follow). But the word "trend" needs one qualification: after 2008 mixed-scope operation did not continue to advance monotonically but turned into the compromise of "integrated at group level, segregated at the level of the legal entity" — the financial holding company holds separated subsidiaries, and firewalls are set between retail banking and investment banking activity, with the paths differing in the United States (the Volcker Rule), the United Kingdom (ring-fencing, legislated in 2013, in force in 2019) and the European Union (after the Liikanen report of 2014 a turn to milder resolution proposals, and in 2021 no adoption of a mandatory structural separation). China's comprehensive-operation pilots likewise proceed within the "subsidiary model" (banks setting up fund, leasing, wealth management, consumer finance and AIC subsidiaries, with the financial asset investment companies (AICs) widening the equity investment pilot from 2020), overlaid with the market access and consolidated supervision of the *Interim Measures for the Supervision and Administration of Financial Holding Companies* (in force from October 2020). In other words, the answer to the dispute over models is not "which replaces which" but "at which layer the scope of protection is drawn".

### What directly gave rise to financial innovation?

Financial innovation is a trend developing rapidly in Western finance.
The trend began as early as the late 1960s; in the 1970s innovation activity of every kind grew daily more active, and by the 1980s it had formed a global trend and a wave. The rise and headlong development of the wave of financial innovation brought far-reaching effects upon the financial system as a whole, upon macroeconomic financial adjustment, and even upon the whole economy.
The direct causes giving rise to financial innovation are:

- (1) Innovation to evade risk

From the 1960s on, the operation of Western banks faced enormous changes in the economic environment. Prolonged high inflation brought a rise in market interest rates, and violent fluctuation with it. The violent fluctuation of interest rates made investment income extremely unstable. This reduced the attraction of long-term investment, and at the same time put the financial institutions holding such assets in a predicament. Banks, in order to keep liability business such as deposits, had to bear more interest expense, while their long-term asset business, bound by the contractually fixed rate, could not yield enough income to support them. Accordingly, banks created, by way of innovation, variable-rate debt instruments — variable-rate bonds, variable-rate certificates of deposit, variable-rate mortgage contracts, variable-rate loans and the like — and developed the forward market in debt instruments, futures trading in debt instruments, and the options market in debt instruments, so as to evade the risk.

- (2) Innovation driven by technological progress

Since the 1960s and 1970s, modern information and communication technology with computer technology at its core has developed at great speed and found wide application, powerfully sustaining and propelling the rise and vigorous growth of financial innovation.

First, technological progress gave rise to innovation in banks' settlement and clearing systems and in payment arrangements, and thereby to innovation in financial services: the bank cards of every shape, for instance, and the launch of a series of new accounts such as ATM (automated teller machine), ATS (automatic transfer of funds service) and NOW (negotiable order of withdrawal) accounts; the appearance of self-service banking, telephone banking and internet banking; and the interbank electronic funds transfer system and the point-of-sale terminal transfer system. Without electrification, networking and information technology as its basis, none of this is conceivable.

Second, the new technology supplied the technical guarantee for the innovation of ever more complex financial instruments.

In addition, the application of the new technology let financial transactions break rapidly through the limits of time and space, linking the transacting parties of almost every corner of the world into one world-wide financial market.

- (3) Innovation to evade administrative regulation

The evasion of unreasonable and obsolete financial administrative regulation also gave rise to financial innovation. As noted above, after the great crisis of the 1930s the countries of the West legislated one after another, subjecting the operation of banking to extremely strict administration and restriction. In fierce competition, the financial industry, seeking its own development, exploited the loopholes in the regulations and launched many new forms of business.

The three causes do not weigh equally. The "dialectic of regulation" proposed by Kane (1981) ranks the third as the core engine: regulation amounts to a tax on specific business, innovation is evasion of that tax, and once the evasion succeeds the regulator patches up — swaps, options, eurocurrencies, money market funds and off-balance-sheet conduits appeared one after another over forty-odd years, and for almost each one the specific clause it got round can be found (eurodollars getting round the Interest Equalization Tax and Regulation Q, SPVs getting round capital occupation, wealth management products getting round credit quotas and sectoral restrictions). The best way to test this cycle is to look back at the earlier concepts of this chapter: off-balance-sheet business and asset securitization themselves carry all three motives at once (evading risk, priceable by technology, evading regulation). This is also why "innovation" and "progress" must be judged separately — the part of the structured products that went wrong in 2008 did satisfy the definition of innovation; only, what it evaded were capital and disclosure constraints that ought to have existed. The Chinese experience after 2020 adds another case: internet platform deposit products (required from January 2021 to stop being sold through third-party internet platforms, and the *Interim Measures for the Administration of Online Small-Loan Business (draft for comment)* of November 2020 raising the equity ratio in joint lending to 30%) show that innovation which evades regulation, once corrected, often changes the structure of the whole intermediary system by way of regulatory upgrading.

## Synthetic essay

### Why did the central bank come into being? What functions does it perform?

- (1) The reasons the central bank came into being
	- ① The need for unified issue of banknotes.
	In the early stage of the development of banking, numerous banks all engaged in the issue of banknotes.
	But the following problems arose:
		- a. The numerous small banks had weak credit strength, and the banknotes they issued often could not be honoured, so that especially in times of crisis the circulation of money very easily fell into disorder.
		- b. Many scattered small banks had their credit activities confined within a region, so the banknotes they issued could circulate only in a limited area. As the capitalist economy developed, there was a demand for a more stable currency, and also for banknotes to become a general credit instrument of circulation able to circulate widely in the national market. In fact, in the course of development there had already appeared some large banks of high repute, whose banknotes were crowding the small banks' banknotes out of circulation. On this basis the state proceeded, in the form of statute, to restrict or abolish the issuing right of ordinary banks, and to concentrate the right of issue in a central bank devoted solely to the issue of banknotes.

	- ② With banks standing in rows and banking business expanding, claims and debts became intricate and complex; if bill exchange and clearing could not be handled in a timely and reasonable way, the smooth running of the economy would be obstructed. A nationally unified, authoritative and impartial clearing institution was therefore objectively needed to serve it.

	- ③ In the course of the business cycle, commercial banks often fell into the predicament of being unable to move their funds about, and sometimes went bankrupt in consequence. A powerful financial institution was therefore objectively needed to stand behind the many other banks and supply them with monetary funds when necessary — that is, to provide liquidity support.

	- ④ As in every other industry, competition in banking was fierce, and moreover the turmoil their bankruptcies and failures in competition brought upon the economy was far greater than that of non-bank industries. A specialized institution representing the will of the government, devoting itself to the management, supervision and coordination of the financial industry, was therefore objectively needed.

- (2) The functions of the central bank

	- ① The bank of issue
	The bank of issue means monopolizing the right to issue banknotes and becoming the sole institution issuing cash nationwide. At present the cash of almost every country in the world is issued by its central bank, and the minting and issue of hard subsidiary coin is likewise mostly managed by the central bank. The issue of banknotes is an important source of funds for the central bank, giving it the financial strength to regulate financial activity.

	- ② The bank of banks
	The business inherent to banks — handling "deposits, loans and remittances" — is likewise the central bank's main content of business, except that the objects of the business are not ordinary enterprises and individuals but commercial banks and other financial institutions. As the bank of banks, its functions are shown specifically in three respects:

	 - a. Concentrating reserve deposits. Usually the law provides that commercial banks and the relevant financial institutions must deposit a portion of their deposits with the central bank as reserve requirements. The purpose is, on the one hand, to guarantee the solvency of the deposit-taking institutions and, on the other, to help the central bank adjust the scale of credit and control the money supply. How much is to be deposited is usually set by fixing a statutory ratio of the deposits taken in by commercial banks and the relevant financial institutions; sometimes several ratios are set for different kinds of deposits. At the same time, the central bank has the power, according to the needs of macroeconomic adjustment, to change and adjust the ratio of reserve deposits. Countries differ considerably in the weight they give to this system.

	- b. The lender of last resort. Around the middle of the nineteenth century, the unceasing economic disturbances and financial crises led people to recognize that a financial panic or the interruption of the chain of payments is often the fuse that sets off an economic crisis, and it was therefore proposed that the central bank should bear the responsibility of "lender of last resort." The role of the lender of last resort:
		- first, to support commercial banks and other financial institutions that have fallen into difficulty over the turnover of funds, so as to prevent a bank run from spreading and ultimately bringing down the whole banking system;
		- second, by providing commercial banks with short-term financial accommodation, to adjust the scale of credit and the money supply and thereby transmit and implement the intent of macroeconomic control.

	- c. Organizing national clearing. The modern payment and clearing system has developed alongside the development of the central banking system. The central banks of most countries expressly stipulate that they bear the duty of organizing payment and clearing.

	- ③ The bank of the state

	The bank of the state means that the central bank, on behalf of the state, carries out fiscal and financial policy, manages the receipts and payments of the treasury, and provides the state with various financial services.
		- a. Managing the treasury.
		- b. Handling the issue of state bonds.
		- c. Giving credit support to the state finances, mainly in the following two ways:
				- lending directly to the state finances;
				- purchasing government bonds.
		- d. Safeguarding the foreign exchange and gold reserves, and conducting the trading and management of foreign exchange and gold.
		- e. Formulating and implementing monetary policy.
		- f. Formulating and supervising the enforcement of the relevant financial management rules and regulations.

	In addition, as the bank of the state the central bank also represents the government in taking part in international financial organizations, attends all kinds of international conferences, engages in international financial activity and signs international financial agreements on the government's behalf; in economic and financial activity at home and abroad it acts as the government's adviser, providing economic and financial intelligence and recommendations for decision.

There is one conspicuous gap in timing between the four reasons for the central bank's emergence and its three functions, and it is worth spelling out: reasons ① and ② had already held true in the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries, while reason ③ was not accepted as a duty until the middle of the nineteenth century (the Bank of England first acted in 1825, and was criticized for not doing so when Overend Gurney failed in 1866 — Bagehot's rule was precisely a response to that episode); reason ④ and the monetary policy within function ③ are products of the twentieth century (the Federal Reserve's chief task at its founding in 1913 was to supply an elastic currency and clearing facilities, and treating monetary policy as a deliberately managed macroeconomic instrument came only after the 1930s; central bank independence became a consensus later still — the *Treasury–Federal Reserve Accord* of 1951, the Bank of England obtaining operational independence in 1997, and the *Treaty on European Union* of 1992 writing in the prohibition of overdraft facilities for governments). This point decides how the chapter is to be read: the central bank's "natural functions" were in fact appended crisis by crisis. The appending also leaves an unresolved tension: "giving credit support to the state finances" within function ③ is expressly forbidden or strictly restricted in many modern countries (Article 123 of the EU *Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union* forbids member states from obtaining overdraft facilities from the European Central Bank, and China's *Law of the People's Bank of China* has forbidden overdrafts to the government since 1995 and bars purchases of government bonds in the primary market), the reason being that "monetizing the fiscal deficit would destroy at once the policy credibility of chapter 7 and the price stability of chapter 3" — while the fiscally driven monetary issuance of the United States in 2020 and the Bank of Japan's holding of about half of the government bonds show that the prohibition can be got round in an emergency, and this boundary is precisely the frontier of the current macroeconomic argument.

## Limits and what follows

- **There is no once-and-for-all answer to "why intermediaries are needed."** The view dominant before the 1960s (Gurley and Shaw, 1960) held that the substantive difference between banks and non-bank institutions was exaggerated, that the intermediary was merely a conduit converting savings into investment, and that its existence sprang mainly from regulatory rents and historical convention. The evidence of the half century since has swung both ways: on the one hand, the models of information asymmetry and liquidity mismatch (Diamond and Dybvig 1983; Diamond 1984 on delegated monitoring) gave the intermediary a solid efficiency rationale; on the other, direct finance and technology platforms have kept eating into the standardizable business (after the 1980s the financing of large American firms moved almost entirely off bank credit, and after 2007 loan origination shifted from banks to non-bank institutions, while the payment link was dispersed among card networks, third-party payment and stablecoins). The more credible conclusion at present is that the more a business depends on relationship-based soft information (small and medium enterprises, mergers and acquisitions, cross-border trade finance), the more it stays inside the intermediary; and the more standardizable the business (mortgage lending, consumer instalments, payments), the more easily it is replaced by market and technology — this is both the key to understanding the list of concepts in this chapter and the basis for judging where the banking system is heading.
- **The dispute between separated and mixed business was reopened after 2008, but it did not return to 1933.** The conclusion the crisis tested out is more specific than "mixed business is dangerous": what went wrong were the institutions relying on short-term wholesale funding and holding large amounts of hard-to-price assets (the investment banks and the structural vehicles), not the traditional deposit-and-loan banks; at the same time the separated system did not escape the crisis either (the roughly 25 depository institutions that failed in the United States in 2008 were concentrated in California and Florida, out of a single concentration in property). The direction of reform was therefore not to restore a *Glass–Steagall*-style prohibition but a combination of "market access limits + consolidated prudence + resolution mechanisms" (the Volcker Rule, the Vickers ring-fence, the milder line after the EU Liikanen report, and the total loss-absorbing capacity, TLAC, promoted by the Financial Stability Board from 2011). China's merger of the banking and insurance regulators in 2018 and the founding of the National Financial Regulatory Administration in 2023 take the path of "institutional regulation moving toward functional regulation, with conduct regulation filled in," which does not conflict with this chapter's judgement of a "trend toward universalization"; what conflicts is the pace and the toolkit.
- **The contemporary forms of most concepts in this chapter lie outside the regulatory framework.** In 2014 the Financial Stability Board gave shadow banking a definition (the entities and activities outside the traditional banking system that perform credit intermediation functions like a bank's) and divided the activities into three classes; the main body of China's shadow banking once consisted of bank wealth management products, entrusted loans, trust conduits and interbank special purpose vehicles, and it contracted markedly after the "three-three-forty" inspections of 2017, the new asset management rules of 2018, the clearing of P2P in 2020 and the tightening of property financing in 2020–2022 (the banking and insurance regulator disclosed in 2021 that the scale of asset management products had been pressed down by about 22 trillion yuan from its peak). At the same time, new forms too large in scale to ignore have appeared: private credit and direct lending funds (estimated in the United States at more than 1.5 trillion dollars by the mid-2020s), settlement in stablecoins (USDT and USDC together once exceeding 170 billion dollars in market capitalization), and the internet platforms' loan facilitation and joint lending in China. These activities perform functions (1) and (4) of this chapter (credit intermediation and the creation of money-like instruments) yet are not covered by the deposit insurance and lender-of-last-resort arrangements later in this chapter — the extension of the term "intermediary system" is much wider than the framework of this chapter, written in terms of banks and the central bank.
- **A main line of China's financial intermediary system is "structural reshuffling" rather than replacement of concepts.** The restorations and new foundations from 1979 (the Agricultural Bank of China in 1979, the Bank of China and the China Construction Bank split off from the People's Bank as specialized banks, the Bank of Communications restored in 1987, the three policy banks in 1994); the abolition of loan quotas and the introduction of asset-liability ratio management in 1997–1998; the four major asset management companies taking over about 1.4 trillion yuan of bad assets in 1999; the capital injections by Huijin and the share reform and listing in 2003–2005; the large-scale restructuring of city commercial banks and rural credit cooperatives in the 2010s; the resolution of risks in small and medium banks and the reform of the provincial credit unions since 2019 (provincial rural commercial joint banks or unified legal persons being formed in Zhejiang, Henan, Liaoning and elsewhere in the 2020s); together with the two reshufflings of the regulators (banking and insurance supervision merged into the CBIRC in 2018, the National Financial Regulatory Administration established in 2023 and the supervision of financial holding companies moved into it from the People's Bank), and the *Financial Stability Law (draft)* submitted for deliberation from December 2022 and still not passed — every step corresponds to the landing or the mending in China of some concept in this chapter. To read this chapter taking the four-piece set "commercial bank – central bank – deposit insurance – lender of last resort" as a group of static definitions is to miss what the Chinese experience truly offers: this structure was built up step by step over thirty years, and the question "why must banks be able to go bankrupt, and who pays when they do" is still under construction today.
