# Chapter 4. The financial intermediary system

> **The central questions of this chapter**: what types of institution make up the financial intermediary system? How does a commercial bank operate? Why are a central bank and a lender of last resort needed?

## The arc of this chapter

The institutions discussed in this chapter did not appear at the same time, and each was born for a different reason; lay the timeline out and every later concept becomes clear as to why it matters. The earliest forms of the commercial bank appear in the goldsmith and Italian money-dealer traditions of the 17th and 18th centuries, resting on "taking deposits and making loans" — a business that any scheme of direct financing could also perform; what truly pushed the bank to the centre of the economy was the industrial revolution — the scale of enterprises and the maturity of loans lengthened together, and a class of institution was needed that could hold a customer relationship over the long term and supervise borrowers. Gerschenkron's (1962) study of late-developing countries gives the strongest version of the point: in Germany, Japan and Russia, where capital markets did not yet exist, the bank was not a supplement to the market but its creator — it made choices for inexperienced households, supervised for investors who could not diversify, and even held shares in industrial and commercial firms. The central bank came much later and far more accidentally: the Bank of England was founded in 1694 to finance the war against France, and for the hundred-odd years that followed it was just an ordinary joint-stock bank, until the four crises of 1825, 1847, 1857 and 1866 forced it to stand behind other banks — only then did "lender of last resort" settle out of practice into a duty (Bagehot gave the rules in *Lombard Street*, 1873); the Federal Reserve came later still (the Federal Reserve Act, 1913), and China not until the founding of the People's Bank of China in 1948 and its exercising the central-bank function exclusively from 1984. Deposit insurance and separate operations were products of the same crisis: after nearly ten thousand American banks failed between 1929 and 1933, the Banking Act of 1933 at once created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC, opened for business in 1934) and established the separation principle of the Glass-Steagall Act — the former so that depositors need not supervise banks, the latter so that banks would stop taking deposits into the stock market. The interesting part is that more than forty years later the two legs were withdrawn in reverse: the innovation spawned by interest-rate volatility and regulatory avoidance (the "financial innovation" section of this chapter) made the architecture of 1933 obsolete, and from 1986 to 1999 the United States loosened it step by step (the Financial Services Modernization Act of 1999 formally abolished the separation), while the United Kingdom and Switzerland never had strict separation to begin with; and after 2008 part of it came back (the Volcker Rule in the United States, 2010; the Vickers Committee's retail and wholesale separation in the United Kingdom, 2013). China's restructuring of the system had two nodes, 1994 and 1998–2005: in 1994 three policy banks were founded, stripping policy business out of the commercial banks; in 1998, 270 billion yuan of special treasury bonds were issued to replenish the capital of the four big banks and four asset management companies were set up to strip out roughly 1.4 trillion yuan of non-performing assets, with recapitalisation, restructuring and listing following in 2003–2005 — without those two steps, "the commercial bank as an enterprise that manages itself" as described in this chapter would have been nominal in China. Asset securitisation (first with Ginnie Mae in 1970, extended to credit cards and auto loans in the 1980s) and off-balance-sheet business (the euro-currency market of the 1960s, loan commitments and securitisation services of the 1980s) are the two peaks of this main line of "getting round the balance sheet" — and precisely the parts that went wrong in the crisis of 2008. Read this line through and the six concepts of this chapter are no longer nouns set side by side, but a list of crises, one after another.

## Core concepts

### The financial institutional system

The financial institutional system is the overall structure of the various institutions performing different functions in the financial field within a country. The accepted classification takes as its standard whether an institution can take deposits and whether it is subject to prudential regulation: one class is deposit-taking corporations, comprising the central bank, commercial banks, policy banks, savings and loan institutions and credit cooperatives; another class is non-deposit-taking financial institutions, comprising securities firms, fund management companies, insurance companies, pension funds, trust and investment companies, financial leasing companies, finance companies, consumer finance companies and the like; besides these there are regulators, exchanges, clearing and settlement institutions and other market infrastructure. China's current structure can be summarised as "one bank, one bureau, one commission" plus policy banks, large state-owned commercial banks, joint-stock banks, city commercial banks and rural commercial banks, and the various non-bank institutions: after the State Council's institutional reform of 2023, the National Financial Regulatory Administration took over the duties of the former CBIRC, while the China Securities Regulatory Commission retained capital-market supervision. The reason the overall structure has to be explained first is that the four concepts later in this chapter (off-balance-sheet business, asset securitisation, the universal bank, deposit insurance) all concern "the boundaries between institutions"; without first knowing where the boundaries are drawn, one cannot see which line these innovations are crossing. Gurley and Shaw's (1960) challenge bears directly on this: they pointed out that many of the differences between banks and non-bank institutions are merely historical arrangements of regulation, and that once controls are loosened the boundary between "intermediation" and "the market" blurs — a claim borne out in the United States after 1980 and in China after 2012, and also the premise on which the concept of "shadow banking" stands.

### Off-balance-sheet business

Off-balance-sheet business refers to any business that is not entered on the bank's balance sheet and does not affect the totals of assets and liabilities.
Off-balance-sheet business in the broad sense includes both the traditional intermediary business and some of the risk-bearing business produced by financial innovation, such as swaps, options, futures, forward rate agreements, note issuance facilities, loan commitments and standby letters of credit.
What is usually meant by off-balance-sheet business refers specifically to the latter, risk-bearing class, that is, off-balance-sheet business in the narrow sense.

The two classes must be kept apart, because in the eyes of the regulator they are entirely different things. Intermediary business such as settlement, agency and custody earns fees and creates no contingent claims or debts; loan commitments, note issuance facilities, standby letters of credit and the notional principal of derivatives, on the other hand, become on-balance-sheet assets or liabilities under certain conditions — they are "balance sheets that have not yet happened". This is precisely the economic reason off-balance-sheet business exists: the bank sells the risk commitment, earns the fee back, and does so without occupying capital or reserves. The trouble lies here too. When Continental Illinois of the United States got into trouble in 1984, the regulators only then discovered how enormous its off-balance-sheet positions were; in 2007–2008, the structured investment vehicles (SIVs) and liquidity facility commitments that Western banks had earlier moved off the balance sheet were forced back onto it, invalidating the capital adequacy on the books overnight — the two characters "off balance sheet" are no isolation in a panic. Hence the two new measures of the Basel III accord of 2010 (a leverage ratio that ignores risk weights and brings off-balance-sheet exposures in at a credit conversion factor; a liquidity coverage ratio required to hold down dependence on wholesale funding) are in fact remedies designed against this very concept, and this is where this chapter joins chapter 6 on financial regulation. China's counterpart experience is the 2013–2016 special treatment of bank wealth management and the interbank business: banks moved credit off the balance sheet through wealth management, entrusted loans and trust beneficial rights, getting round the loan-to-deposit ratio, industry restrictions and capital occupation, and the rectification of 2017 together with the 2018 Guiding Opinions on Regulating the Asset-Management Business of Financial Institutions (the new asset-management rules), which broke guaranteed redemption and restricted fund pools and nesting, were precisely a convergence upon the same logic.

### Asset securitisation

Asset securitisation is a form of financing in which credit assets that already exist are pooled together and re-divided into securities and then resold to investors in the market, so that the asset disappears from the balance sheet of its original holder. As the principal holders of direct credit assets, commercial banks and savings and loan institutions are the best placed to carry out this business. The essential meaning of asset securitisation is the process of converting loans or receivables into marketable financial instruments.
The asset securitisation system includes the following: ordinary asset-backed securities, credit-card-backed securities, asset-backed debt, asset-backed preferred shares and asset-backed commercial paper.

The problem securitisation has to solve is very concrete: a bank's loans are long-term, non-tradable assets locked in by borrower relationships, while the funds it needs are those that can flow back at any time. Selling the loans recovers funds, releases the capital occupation, and spreads concentration risk out to the market. The first modern securitisation product was the pass-through certificate issued in 1970 by Ginnie Mae (GNMA) in the United States (its underlying assets were government-guaranteed residential mortgage loans); Fannie Mae entered the Federal National Mortgage Association market in 1977, the first securitisation with auto loans as the underlying (originated from bank credit-card and car-rental receivables) appeared in 1985, and it afterwards spread to credit-card receivables, student loans, equipment leasing and trade receivables. Whether its mechanism works turns on one thing: whether investors can see clearly the cash flows and default characteristics of the underlying assets. To that end the designers built in three components — true sale and bankruptcy remoteness (placing the assets in a special purpose vehicle so that the collapse of the originator does not affect the cash flows), tranching (senior, mezzanine and junior tranches, cutting the order of losses to suit different risk appetites), and credit enhancement such as over-collateralisation and the excess-spread account. The failure of 2007–2008 happened exactly in the circumstances where all three components failed at once: tranching was misread as "the senior tranche is always safe", because everyone ignored the correlation among the underlying loans; bankruptcy remoteness was eroded by the implicit liquidity support the originators went on providing; and, most crucially, the quality of the subprime loans themselves was no longer verified by anyone amid the layer upon layer of resale. The "originate-to-distribute" model moved the lender's interest from "can the borrower repay" to "can this loan be sold", and that is the cost of this concept that must be remembered in this chapter. China's credit-asset securitisation was started on a pilot basis in 2005 by the China Development Bank (CLO) and China Construction Bank (personal housing mortgage loans), was suspended because of the crisis in 2008 and restarted in 2012, and since then has run mainly on a "filing system plus the interbank market" basis, with underlying assets extended to corporate receivables, financial leasing, consumer finance and infrastructure fee-revenue rights; in the outstanding structure of recent years, personal housing mortgage loans and entrusted loans are still the main varieties — the difference from the United States being that Chinese securitisation serves the management of capital occupation rather than, as in the United States, a policy of housing affordability.

### The universal commercial bank

A universal commercial bank is a commercial bank that may conduct every kind of financial business, including deposits and loans of every maturity and kind, all manner of securities trading, and such financial business as trusts and payment clearing.

Opposed to it is the functional division of labour type (separate operations): the law confines financial institutions to distinct categories, each with its own speciality, and commercial banks may not conduct securities underwriting and dealing on their own account. The quarrel between these two concepts is not an academic preference but the result of three bodies of experience laid on top of one another. The first is the long practice of Germany and Switzerland: universal banks, by holding shares in industrial and commercial firms and sending directors to take part in their governance, successfully mobilised capital for the late-developing industrial countries in the late 19th century, but the failure of the Danat-Bank in 1931 showed that when a bank itself gets into difficulty, the risk between it and its enterprises is amplified in both directions. The second is the American judgement of 1933: the congressional hearings blamed part of the credit contraction of the Great Depression on investment banks underwriting poor securities with depositors' money, and the Glass-Steagall Act accordingly forbade member banks to underwrite and deal in securities, while at the same time establishing deposit insurance. The third is the withdrawal in the opposite direction after 1980: interest-rate volatility compressed the banks' margin (see "the direct causes of financial innovation" below), and the separation rules left banks unable to provide the full set of services their clients needed; Japan pushed towards universalisation in the 1970s and 80s by way of the "comprehensive financial research institute", and the Financial Services Modernization Act of 1999 in the United States repealed the separation provisions and allowed financial holding companies to hold banking, securities and insurance business at the same time. After 2008 the attitude was not one-sided either: the Volcker Rule in the United States (passed in 2010, implemented in stages from 2013 to 2015) restricts banks' proprietary trading and their holdings of private equity and hedge funds; the Vickers Commission in the United Kingdom (2011–2013) recommended separating retail deposit business from wholesale investment-banking activity at the level of the legal entity (ring-fencing); and Germany has kept the universal model unchanged. That is to say, "separation or integration, which is better" has not in fact been settled; it has merely been restated as a more precise question: how much market risk should a retail bank protected by deposit insurance and the lender of last resort be allowed to carry? China's present arrangement is "separate operations and separate regulation as the mainstay, with financial holding companies and comprehensive-operation pilots existing alongside", and the concern behind that choice is exactly the question in the sentence above.

### The lender of last resort

The lender of last resort (also called the ultimate lender) refers to the duty of accommodation the central bank ought to discharge at the moment of crisis; it should satisfy the demand for high-powered money so as to prevent the contraction of the stock of money brought about by panic.
The functions of the lender of last resort:
- First, to support commercial banks and other financial institutions that have fallen into funding difficulties, so that a run on banks does not widen and ultimately bring down the banking industry as a whole;
- Second, by arranging short-term funding for commercial banks, to regulate the scale of credit and the supply of money, conveying and carrying out the intentions of macroeconomic control.

The whole difficulty of this concept lies in "how to rescue", not in "whether to rescue". The three rules Bagehot gave in *Lombard Street* (1873) remain the benchmark of the discussion to this day: lend in a panic, lend against good collateral, lend at a penalising high rate, and confine assistance to institutions that are temporarily short of liquidity rather than insolvent. The depth of these three rules is that they handle two failure modes at once: refusing to lend turns a liquidity panic into a solvency crisis (in 1907 the United States relied on the personal coordination of J.P. Morgan; in 1930–1931 the Federal Reserve watched the wave of bank failures and did nothing), while lending too freely strips both banks and depositors of their prudential motive. That second failure mode is precisely the main direction of scholarly criticism of the lender of last resort since Friedman and Schwartz (1963), and "too big to fail" in 2008 turned it into a policy issue — a rescue spares creditors and counterparties their losses, so the market will never price the high-risk liabilities of a large bank. The post-crisis handling was to abandon blanket rescue in favour of a whole set of substitute arrangements: the Orderly Liquidation Authority (OLA) established by the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 in the United States, and the Federal Reserve's reforms to make the discount window standing from 2013 (the Primary Dealer Credit Facility, PDCF, activated in March 2020); from 2014 emergency liquidity assistance (ELA) in the euro area requires the approval of the European Central Bank; and the EU's Bank Recovery and Resolution Directive (BRRD, 2014) established that bail-in takes precedence over external recapitalisation. China's counterpart arrangement is that the People's Bank of China has discharged the function since the 2000s through re-lending, temporary liquidity facilities and similar tools, and the Financial Stability Law (Draft) of 2022 wrote the "lender of last resort" clause into statute (the law had still not passed as of 2024, and the institution is still under construction); the takeover of Baoshang Bank in 2019, with redemptions paid by creditor group so as to break the implicit guarantee among interbank creditors, was a genuine resolution drill. As for why the boundary of "who qualifies for help" is always on the move, see chapter 6 on the financial regulatory system and the deposit-insurance section of this chapter.

### The deposit insurance system

The deposit insurance system is an institutional arrangement that protects the interests of depositors and stabilises the financial system.
Under this arrangement, deposit-taking financial institutions insure with the deposit insurance institution at the prescribed premium rate according to the amount of the deposits they take, and when a deposit-taking institution goes bankrupt and cannot satisfy depositors' demands for withdrawal, the deposit insurance institution bears the responsibility for paying the statutory insurance money.

What this institution has to solve is a problem of the game: a run can kill a bank that is in fact not insolvent within a single day, and each depositor's individually rational choice is to withdraw first — Diamond and Dybvig (1983) used this model to show that the design of the deposit contract, "deposit first, withdraw later, redeemable at any time", contains multiple equilibria in itself; running and not running are both equilibria. The effect of deposit insurance is to delete the panic equilibrium outright: if small deposits are certain to be paid in full, depositors have no reason to queue at midnight. Its cost is equally clear: depositors from then on have no motive to supervise the bank, and the risk is shifted onto the insurance institution and onto whoever ultimately pays, so it must be designed together with prudential regulation, risk-differentiated premiums and a limit on payouts (this is why this chapter and chapter 6 cannot be read separately). The specific parameters: the United States legislated in 1933 and founded the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation in 1934; the Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act of 1980 raised the cover from 40,000 U.S. dollars to 100,000 U.S. dollars; after the temporary full guarantee of 2008, the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 made 250,000 U.S. dollars permanent; and the failures of Silicon Valley Bank and Signature Bank in 2023 reopened the argument over whether unlimited protection for the depositors of small banks counts as a new implicit guarantee (the Treasury covered everything in full under a "systemic risk exception" while at the same time reversing itself on not rescuing shareholders and senior creditors); under the EU's single guarantee scheme the standard cover is 100,000 euros. China's path was for the Deposit Insurance Regulation to come into force on 1 May 2015, with a maximum payout of 500,000 yuan (covering about 99.6% of deposit accounts), a differentiated premium based on a base rate, and the Deposit Insurance Fund Management Co., Ltd. (founded 2019) charged with early correction and risk disposal; the two cases of Baoshang Bank in 2019 and the village banks of Henan in 2022 tested precisely who bears the large exposures and "non-deposit" products that fall outside this limit.

## Problems and analysis

### What sets a commercial bank apart from an ordinary enterprise?

What sets the commercial bank apart from ordinary industrial and commercial enterprises lies in the content of its particular operating activities and its functional role.

- (1) Acting as a credit intermediary between enterprises

By taking deposits the bank mobilises and concentrates the idle money capital of society, and then provides that money capital, by way of loans or investment, to capitalists engaged in industry; the bank thereby becomes the intermediary between the lender and the borrower of money capital. This helps to make full use of the money capital that already exists.

- (2) Acting as a payment intermediary between enterprises

By opening accounts for the various enterprises it acts as the intermediary for monetary settlement and for the receipt and payment of money between them. Here the bank appears in the capacity of the enterprise's account-keeper and cashier, and by this means it can accelerate the turnover of enterprise capital.

- (3) Converting the savings and incomes of all social strata into capital

Savings and incomes originally destined for consumption are gathered together through the bank and made available to entrepreneurs for use as capital. This can enlarge the total stock of social capital.

- (4) Creating instruments of credit circulation

On the basis of the businesses above, the commercial bank becomes the creator of banknotes and deposit money. The commercial bank's right to issue banknotes was later abolished, but its creation of deposit money on the basis of organising cheque transfers plays an ever more important role in economic life.

Of the four items, the real watershed is the fourth: only a bank can create, by bookkeeping alone, deposits that serve as a means of payment, and this is also why the money multiplier of chapter 1 applies only to the banking system, while securities firms and funds do not count as "creating money" (they merely move existing purchasing power between holders). Items (1) and (3) answer "why not finance directly": it is theoretically possible for an enterprise to issue bonds, but households lack both the information and the bargaining power, and having a bank do the screening, the supervision and the maturity transformation (borrowing short to lend long) costs less in transaction terms. Item (2) is the one most easily overlooked and the most public-good in character — once payment and clearing grinds to a halt, no creditor-debtor relationship in the whole of society can be settled, and it is precisely for this reason that the function finally passed into the hands of the central bank (see "the reasons the central bank came into being" in this chapter). Gerschenkron's (1962) study of late industrialisation pushes these functions to the extreme: in an age when capital markets were absent, the bank was not merely "matching" savings to investment; it took over the entrepreneurial function in their stead — and this is the essential background for understanding the role of China's state-owned banks before 1979, and why policy banks had to be set up in 1994.

### Where does a bank's money come from, and where does it go?

- (1) The asset business of the commercial bank

The asset business of the commercial bank is the business of putting to use the money it has gathered through its liability business. It is the main avenue for earning income. Of the funds gathered, apart from the portion that must be kept as cash and as deposits at the central bank to meet customers' demands for withdrawals and transfer settlement, the rest is put to use mainly by way of discounting, lending and securities investment.

- (2) The liability business of the commercial bank

The liability business of the commercial bank is the business that forms its sources of funds. Its sources of funds as a whole comprise two parts, own capital and the outside funds absorbed.

Own capital comprises the share capital raised by issuing stock, together with reserve funds and undistributed profits. This part is also called equity capital. In the sources of funds of a deposit money bank, own funds account for a very small share, yet they are the foundation on which outside funds are absorbed. Outside funds consist mainly of deposits taken, borrowing from the central bank, borrowing from other banks and from the money market, and the issue of medium- and long-term financial bonds.

To read this table as "in at low interest, out at high interest" misses two points. First, the liability side determines what kind of shock can kill a bank: a bank funded mainly by wholesale funding such as interbank borrowing and bond issues will find its liabilities stop being rolled over on the very day the market loses confidence in it — Northern Rock in August 2007 (the first bank in the United Kingdom to be rescued after a break in wholesale funding, with depositors queuing at the door to withdraw cash), Lehman Brothers in September 2008 and Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023 (about two-thirds of whose liabilities were deposits of an unsecured wholesale character, and highly concentrated in a single industry) are all the same mechanism; a bank funded mainly by household term deposits is comparatively steady, which explains why "deposit competition" is no small matter in the regulation of any country. Second, a small share of equity capital does not mean unimportance: the role of own capital is to absorb unexpected losses, and the Basel Accord of 1988 fixing the core capital ratio at 4% and total capital at 8% is precisely an acknowledgement that the structure of "an institution with 8% of its own money running 92% of somebody else's money" is itself a source of systemic risk. China's context adds one more: for a long time Chinese commercial banks expanded the liability side with an abnormally large branch network and the backing of government credit, and the rapid rise in the share of interbank liabilities in 2014–2016 (in some banks approaching the ceiling of one third that regulators later set) and the jump in interbank certificate-of-deposit rates at small and medium banks after the Baoshang Bank episode of 2019 are both about how fragility on the liability side becomes tiered pricing across the whole system.

### Why are banks ever more "universal"? The two operating models and the trend

- (1) The two types of commercial bank operating model

Classified by operating model, commercial banks fall into:
- functional division of labour type banks
- universal banks

The basic characteristic of the functional division of labour type is that the law confines financial institutions to separate categories, each with its own speciality: some deal exclusively in long-term finance, some exclusively in short-term finance, some exclusively in the buying and selling of securities, some exclusively in trust business, and so on.
The greatest difference between a commercial bank under the functional division of labour system and other financial institutions is:
- ①only the commercial bank may take demand deposits on which cheques may be drawn;
- ②the commercial bank generally takes the granting of short-term industrial and commercial credit of under one year as its main business. The universal commercial bank, also called the comprehensive commercial bank, may conduct every banking business, including deposits and loans of every maturity and kind, and may go so far as to conduct securities business in full. The functional division of labour model is called the separate operations, separate regulation model; correspondingly, the universal model is called the integrated operations, integrated regulation model.

- (2) The trend of development of the commercial bank operating model

Since the 1970s, and especially over the past decade and more, accompanied by the rapidly advancing tide of financial liberalisation and the endless emergence of financial innovation, in the countries that practise separate operations the business of commercial banks has tended increasingly towards universalisation and comprehensiveness.

The driving force behind this trend can be taken apart into three, and each can be matched to a specific institution: the first is arbitrage to get round controls (Regulation Q in the United States capped deposit interest rates; from 1978 inflation made the effective ceiling a negative rate, so banks broke through by way of NOW accounts, automatic transfer service (ATS), money market funds, mutual funds and repurchase agreements, which finally brought about the Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act of 1980 and the completion of the abolition in 1986); the second is change on the demand side (once pensions and mutual funds had grown large, corporate financing turned to bonds and commercial paper, and banks were forced to extend into securities underwriting, dealing and advice — the "disintermediation" of the United States in the 1970s and 80s); the third is technology and global competition (the "Big Bang" of London in 1986 abolished fixed commissions and allowed foreign capital and universalisation, pressing directly on other countries to follow). But the word "trend" needs a qualification: after 2008 integration did not go on advancing monotonically, but took on the compromise form of "consolidated at the level of the group, separated at the level of the legal entity" — financial holding companies hold separately operated subsidiaries, with firewalls between retail banking and investment-banking activity; the paths of the United States (the Volcker Rule), the United Kingdom (the ring-fence, legislated in 2013, in force in 2019) and the European Union (the Liikanen report, which after 2014 turned towards milder proposals, and in 2021 declined to adopt a mandatory requirement of structural separation) differ from one another. China's comprehensiveness pilots likewise proceed under the "subsidiary model" (banks setting up subsidiaries for funds, leasing, wealth management, consumer finance and AICs, with the pilot of equity investment by financial asset investment companies (AICs) enlarged from 2020), overlaid with the access and consolidated supervision of the Interim Measures for the Supervision and Administration of Financial Holding Companies (in force October 2020). In other words, the answer to the quarrel over models is not "who displaces whom" but "at which level the boundary of protection is drawn".

### What directly gave rise to financial innovation?

Financial innovation is a rapidly developing trend in Western finance.
The trend began as early as the late 1960s; in the 1970s the various innovative activities grew livelier by the day, and by the 1980s a global trend and wave had taken shape. The rise and rapid growth of the wave of financial innovation have had a far-reaching effect on the financial system as a whole, on macro-financial regulation and indeed on the whole economy.
The direct causes that gave rise to financial innovation are:

- (1) Innovation to evade risk

From the 1960s onwards, the operations of Western banks faced enormous changes in the economic environment. Sustained high inflation brought rising market interest rates, and the volatility was severe. Such severe volatility made investment returns extremely unstable. This reduced the attractiveness of long-term investment, and at the same time left the financial institutions holding such assets in a predicament. To hold on to its liability business such as deposits, the bank had to bear more interest expense, while on the asset side its long-term business, constrained by the originally contracted rates, had too little income to support it. So the bank, through innovation, created debt and credit instruments with variable rates, such as variable-rate bonds, variable-rate certificates of deposit, variable-rate mortgage contracts and variable-rate loans; developed forward markets for debt instruments; developed futures trading in debt instruments; and developed options markets for debt instruments, and so on, in order to evade risk.

- (2) Innovation driven by technological progress

From the 1960s and 1970s onwards, modern information technology and communications technology centred on the computer developed rapidly and came into wide application, powerfully supporting and driving the rise and vigorous growth of financial innovation.

First, technological progress brought innovation in bank settlement, clearing systems and payment arrangements, and thereby innovation in financial services: bank cards of every description, and the launch of a series of new accounts such as the ATM (automated teller machine), ATS (automatic transfer service) and NOW (negotiable order of withdrawal) accounts; the appearance of self-service banking, telephone banking and online banking; interbank electronic funds transfer systems and the transfer systems of point-of-sale terminals. Without electronic, networked information technology as its support, none of this is conceivable.

Second, new technology provides the technical guarantee for the innovation of ever more complex financial instruments.

Furthermore, the application of new technology enabled financial transactions to break through the limits of time and space with great speed, linking the transacting parties of almost every corner of the globe together in one worldwide financial market.

- (3) Innovation to evade administrative regulation

Evading unreasonable and outmoded financial administrative regulations has also given rise to financial innovation. As mentioned above, after the great crisis of the 1930s the Western countries legislated one after another, imposing extremely strict management and restriction on the conduct of the banking business. In the heat of competition, the financial industry, in order to secure its own development, exploited the loopholes in the regulations and launched many new forms of business.

The three causes are not equal in weight; the "dialectic of regulation" proposed by Kane (1981) names the third as the core engine: regulation amounts to a tax on a particular business, innovation is the avoidance of that tax, and once the avoidance succeeds the regulation is patched up again — over forty years and more, swaps, options, the euro-currency market, money market funds and off-balance-sheet conduits appeared in succession, and for almost every one of them one can find the specific clause it got round (the eurodollar got round the Interest Equalization Tax and Regulation Q, the SPV got round the capital occupation, wealth management got round the credit quota and the industry restrictions). The best way to test this cycle is to look back at the earlier concepts of this chapter: off-balance-sheet business and asset securitisation themselves possess all three motives at once (evading risk, pricing made possible by technology, evading regulation). This is also why "innovation" and "progress" must be judged separately — the structured products that went wrong in 2008 did fulfil the definition of innovation; what they evaded were the capital and information disclosure constraints that ought to have existed. China's experience after 2020 adds one more case: internet platform deposit products (required from January 2021 to stop being sold through third-party internet platforms, and the Interim Measures for the Administration of Online Micro-Lending Business (Draft for Comment) of November 2020 raised the contribution ratio of joint loans to 30%) show that once an innovation devised to evade regulation is corrected, it tends to change the structure of the whole intermediary system in the form of a regulatory upgrade.

## Synthetic essay

### Why did the central bank come into being? What functions does it perform?

- (1) The reasons for the emergence of the central bank
	- ①The necessity of a unified issue of banknotes.
	In the early period of the development of banking, numerous banks all engaged in the issue of banknotes.
	But the following problems existed:
		- a. The credit strength of the many small banks was weak, and the banknotes they issued could often not be redeemed, especially in times of crisis, which could easily throw the circulation of money into confusion.
		- b. Many scattered small banks had regional limits to the field of their credit activity, so the banknotes they issued could circulate only within a limited area. With the development of the capitalist economy, a more stable currency came to be demanded, and the banknote came to be required to become a general instrument of credit circulation able to move widely in the national market. In fact, in the course of development some large banks of outstanding repute had already appeared, and the banknotes they issued were crowding the banknotes of the small banks out of circulation. On this basis the state proceeded, by way of decree, to restrict or abolish the issuing rights of the ordinary banks, and to concentrate the right of issue in a central bank charged specifically with issuing banknotes.

	- ②Banks stood thick on the ground and the business of banking kept expanding, so that the relationships of debt and credit became intricately involved; if the exchange and clearing of bills could not be handled promptly and properly, it would impede the smooth running of the economy. Hence there arose objectively the need to establish a nationwide, authoritative and impartial clearing institution to serve them.

	- ③In the course of the business cycle, commercial banks often fell into the predicament of being unable to arrange their funds, and sometimes went bankrupt on that account. Hence there arose objectively the need for a strong financial institution to stand behind the many other banks, providing them with money when necessary, that is, providing the support of liquidity.

	- ④As in other industries, competition in the banking business was also fierce, and the disorder that their bankruptcies and failures in that competition inflicted on the economy was far greater than in the non-banking industries. There was therefore objectively a need for a specialised institution embodying the will of the government, charged with the management, supervision and coordination of the financial industry.

- (2) The functions of the central bank

	- ①The bank of issue
	The bank of issue is the one that monopolises the right to issue banknotes and becomes the only issuer of bank notes in the whole country. At present, the bank notes of almost every country in the world are issued by the central bank. The minting and issue of hard subsidiary coins is also mostly administered by the central bank. The issue of banknotes is an important source of funds for the central bank, and provides it with the financial strength with which to regulate financial activity.

	- ②The bank of banks
	The business inherent to banks — handling "deposits, loans and remittances" — is likewise the main content of the central bank's business, only that the objects of the business are not ordinary enterprises and individuals but commercial banks and other financial institutions. As the bank of banks, its functions appear concretely in three respects:

	 - a. Concentrating deposit reserves. The law normally provides that commercial banks and the financial institutions concerned must deposit a portion of their deposit reserves with the central bank. The purpose is, on the one hand, to guarantee the solvency of the deposit-taking institutions, and on the other, to make it easier for the central bank to regulate the scale of credit and control the supply of money. How much reserve is deposited is normally fixed as a statutory ratio of the deposits taken by the commercial banks and the financial institutions concerned; sometimes several ratios are fixed according to the different kinds of deposit. At the same time the central bank has the right, according to the needs of macroeconomic regulation, to change and adjust the ratio at which deposit reserves are held. Different countries differ considerably in how much weight they attach to this institution.

	- b. The ultimate lender. Around the middle of the 19th century, the unbroken succession of economic disturbances and financial crises made people realise that a financial panic or the breaking of the chain of payments is often the fuse that touches off an economic crisis, and it was accordingly proposed that the central bank should shoulder the responsibility of "ultimate lender". The functions of the ultimate lender are,
		- first, to support commercial banks and other financial institutions that have fallen into funding difficulties, so that a run on the banks does not widen and finally bring down the whole of the banking industry;
		- second, by arranging short-term funding for the commercial banks, to regulate the scale of credit and the supply of money, conveying and carrying out the intentions of macroeconomic control.

	- c. Organising the clearing of the whole country. The modern payment and clearing system developed together with the development of the central banking system. Most countries' central banks have explicit provisions, and bear the duty of organising payment and clearing.

	- ③The bank of the state

	The bank of the state means that the central bank acts for the state in carrying out fiscal and financial policy, serves as agent for the receipts and payments of the treasury, and provides the state with all kinds of financial services.
		- a. Acting as agent for the treasury.
		- b. Acting as agent for the issue of government bonds.
		- c. Granting credit support to the state fisc. Mainly by the following two methods:
				- lending directly to the state fisc;
				- purchasing government bonds.
		- d. Safeguarding the foreign exchange and gold reserves, and conducting and managing their buying, selling and administration.
		- e. Formulating and implementing monetary policy.
		- f. Formulating and supervising the enforcement of the relevant regulations on financial administration.

	In addition, as the bank of the state, the central bank also represents the government in joining international financial organisations, attending the various international conferences, engaging in international financial activity and signing international financial agreements on the government's behalf; in economic and financial activity at home and abroad it acts as the government's adviser, providing economic and financial information and advice on decisions.

Between the four reasons for emergence and the three functions there is one conspicuous gap in time, worth pointing out: reasons ① and ② were already established in the 17th to 19th centuries; reason ③ was not accepted as a duty until the middle of the 19th century (the Bank of England acted for the first time in 1825, and in 1866, when Overend Gurney failed, it was criticised for not having rescued it — Bagehot's rules were precisely a response to that episode); reason ④ and the monetary policy inside function ③ are products of the 20th century (the Federal Reserve's first task at its founding in 1913 was to provide an elastic currency and the convenience of clearing; treating monetary policy as a deliberately managed macroeconomic tool came only after the 1930s; central bank independence as a consensus came later still — the Treasury-Federal Reserve Accord of 1951 in the United States, the Bank of England obtaining operational independence in 1997, and the Maastricht Treaty of 1992 in the European Union writing in the prohibition of overdrafts by the fisc). This point decides how the chapter is to be read: the "natural functions" of the central bank were in fact added on, crisis by crisis. The process of addition also left tensions unresolved: "granting credit support to the state fisc", inside function ③, is expressly prohibited or strictly limited in many modern countries (Article 123 of the EU's Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union forbids member states to overdraw the European Central Bank, and China's Law on the People's Bank of China has since 1995 forbidden overdrafts by the government and purchases of government bonds in the primary market), the reason being that "monetising the fiscal deficit would destroy at once the policy credibility of chapter 7 and the price stability of chapter 3" — and the fact that the United States in 2020 put money out on a fiscally led basis, and that the Bank of Japan today holds about half of the government bonds outstanding, shows that the prohibition can be got round in an emergency; this boundary is exactly the frontier of the current macroeconomic argument.

## Limits and what follows

- **"Why an intermediary is needed" has no once-and-for-all answer.** The view that prevailed before the 1960s (Gurley and Shaw, 1960) held that the substantive difference between banks and non-bank institutions had been exaggerated, and that the intermediary was merely a pipe converting savings into investment, whose existence owed mainly to regulatory rents and historical practice. The evidence of the half-century since has swung at both ends: on one side, the models of information asymmetry and liquidity mismatch (Diamond-Dybvig 1983; Diamond 1984 on delegated monitoring) gave the intermediary a solid efficiency rationale; on the other, direct finance and technology platforms kept eating away at the business that can be standardised (in the United States after the 1980s the financing of large enterprises almost entirely left bank credit, and after 2007 loan origination shifted from banks to non-bank institutions; the payment leg was dispersed among card schemes, third-party payment and stablecoins). The most defensible conclusion at present is: the more a business depends on relationship-based soft information (small and medium enterprises, mergers and acquisitions, cross-border trade finance), the more it stays inside the intermediary; the more a business can be standardised (housing mortgages, consumer instalments, payment), the more easily it is displaced by market and technology — this is both the key to reading the list of concepts in this chapter and the basis for judging where the banking system is going.
- **After 2008 the argument over separation and integration was reopened, but it did not return to 1933.** What the crisis tested out was more concrete than "integration is dangerous": what went wrong were the institutions that relied on short-term wholesale funding and held large quantities of assets not easily priced (investment banks and structured conduits), not the traditional deposit-and-loan banks; and at the same time the separate-operations system did not escape the crisis either (the twenty-five or so savings and loan institutions that failed in the United States in 2008 were concentrated in California and Florida, and arose out of a single real-estate concentration). The direction of reform was therefore not the restoration of a Glass-Steagall-style prohibition, but the combination of "access restriction + consolidated prudential supervision + resolution machinery" (the Volcker Rule, the Vickers ring-fence, the milder package in the European Union after the Liikanen report, and the total loss-absorbing capacity TLAC pushed by the Financial Stability Board from 2011). China's merger of the banking and insurance regulators in 2018 and the founding of the National Financial Regulatory Administration in 2023 follow the path of "from institution-based regulation to functional regulation, with conduct regulation filled in", which does not conflict with this chapter's judgement on the "trend towards universalisation"; what conflicts is the pace and the toolbox.
- **Most of the contemporary forms of this chapter's concepts lie outside the regulatory framework.** In 2014 the Financial Stability Board gave a definition of shadow banking (entities and activities outside the traditional banking system that perform functions akin to the credit intermediation of banks) and divided the activities into three classes; the main bodies of China's shadow banking were once bank wealth management, entrusted loans, trust conduits and interbank special purpose vehicles, and after 2017 they contracted markedly with the "three-three-four-zero" inspections, the new asset-management rules of 2018, the clearing of P2P to zero in 2020 and the tightening of property finance from 2020 to 2022 (the CBIRC disclosed in 2021 that the scale of asset-management products had been cut by roughly 22 trillion yuan from its peak). At the same time, new forms that can no longer be ignored by size have appeared: private credit and direct lending funds (this segment in the United States was estimated in the middle 2020s at over 1.5 trillion U.S. dollars), settlement in stablecoins (the combined market value of USDT and USDC at one point exceeded 170 billion U.S. dollars), and China's internet-platform assisted lending and joint lending. These activities perform the functions of items (1) and (4) of this chapter (credit intermediation and the creation of money-like instruments), yet are not covered by the deposit insurance and lender of last resort arrangements later in this chapter — the extension of the phrase "intermediary system" is far wider than the bank-central bank frame in which this chapter is written.
- **One main line of China's financial intermediary system is "structural rearrangement" rather than the replacement of concepts.** The restoring and founding of institutions from 1979 (the Agricultural Bank of China in 1979, the Bank of China and the Construction Bank split out of the People's Bank of China as specialised banks, the Bank of Communications restored in 1987, the three policy banks in 1994); the abolition of the credit quota and the introduction of asset-liability ratio management in 1997–1998; the four great asset management companies taking over roughly 1.4 trillion yuan of non-performing assets in 1999; the recapitalisation by Huijin and the share reform and listing of 2003–2005; the large-scale restructuring of city commercial banks and rural credit cooperatives in the 2010s; the resolution of risks at small and medium banks and the provincial credit cooperative reform since 2019 (in the 2020s, Zhejiang, Henan, Liaoning and other provinces formed provincial rural commercial joint banks or unified legal persons); together with the two rearrangements of the regulators (merging banking and insurance supervision into the CBIRC in 2018, and in 2023 founding the National Financial Regulatory Administration and moving the supervision of financial holding companies out of the People's Bank of China), plus the Financial Stability Law (Draft), referred for deliberation from December 2022 and still not passed — every step corresponds to the landing or the patching of some concept of this chapter in China. If, in reading this chapter, one takes the four-piece set "commercial bank — central bank — deposit insurance — lender of last resort" as a group of static definitions, one will miss what Chinese experience really offers: this structure was built up step by step over thirty years, and the question within it, "why should a bank be able to fail, and who pays when it does", is still under construction to this day.
