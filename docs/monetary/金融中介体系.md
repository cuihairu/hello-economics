# Chapter 4. The financial intermediary system

> **The core question of this chapter**: Why does the financial intermediary system stand at the heart of the modern economy? What are its causes, and what are its functions?

## The arc of this chapter

The historical starting point of the financial intermediary system is the **spontaneous emergence of the "deposit—loan" intermediary chain**: even before banking came into being, society already had an objective need for the "separation of money holders from money users," and that need spontaneously generated intermediaries. In seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Europe, **banking differentiated itself out of the money-changing trade**, and its form evolved through three successive stages. The **money exchange house** (sixteenth-century Italy, seventeenth-century Amsterdam) was the earliest form, offering exchange, custody and remittance services; the **deposit bank** (post-seventeenth-century Europe) began to take deposits and make loans; and the **commercial bank** (eighteenth and nineteenth centuries) finally settled into the integrated **"deposit-taking—lending—clearing" trinity**, and under the advocacy of the **Banking Association of London (1843)** produced the Bank of England's deposit-reserve system. **The institutionalization of bank supervision** followed closely: the **National Bank Act (1863)** established the US national banking system, the **Basel I Accord (1988)** set a uniform minimum capital adequacy ratio of 8%, and **Basel III (2010)** added the liquidity coverage ratio and the net stable funding ratio. China's financial intermediary system has gone through a **system transition from a people's bank to commercial banks**: in 1983 the People's Bank of China was entrusted with the exclusive central banking function, in 1984 the Bank of China was established as the specialized bank for foreign business, in 1994 the three policy banks—the China Development Bank, the Export-Import Bank of China and the Agricultural Development Bank of China—were founded, in 2003 the **deposit insurance fund** was created, and in 2013 the *Additional Supervision Rules for Systemically Important Banks* were issued, building step by step toward a financial intermediary regulatory framework aligned with international practice. Between 2020 and 2023, global regulation shifted its focus to defining the boundaries of the **shadow banking system and crypto-asset intermediation**, with the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision and the Financial Stability Board each issuing proposed regulatory frameworks for **"stablecoins" and "non-bank payment systems"**.

## Core concepts

### The financial institutional system

The financial institutional system is the **central nervous system** of a country's financial markets — it comprises **commercial banks, investment banks, insurance companies, trust companies, securities companies** and other institutions directly engaged in channelling funds. A financial intermediary is not a mere "carrier" but works through three mechanisms — **maturity transformation, scale transformation and risk transformation** — to pool dispersed, short-term, small-scale savings and convert them into long-term, large-scale, professional investment.

### Off-balance-sheet business

Business conducted by financial institutions **off the balance sheet** without drawing on their own funds, typically **guarantees, commitments and derivatives** (see the money demand theory in section 2 of [money supply and demand theory](/monetary/货币供求理论)). Off-balance-sheet business leaves an institution's **true risk exposure larger than its book assets**, and was the direct technical root of the 2008 subprime crisis.

The **Basel II Capital Accord** requires financial institutions to report the "credit equivalent amount" of their off-balance-sheet assets.

In 2023 the **European Central Bank** brought off-balance-sheet derivatives into the mandatory scope of stress testing.

### Asset securitization

Financial institutions **package** **illiquid assets** (loans, receivables and the like) into **tradable securities** and sell them to investors, thereby removing those assets from the institution's balance sheet. Asset securitization lets a bank **circumvent capital adequacy constraints** while continuing to expand credit, but it also shifts the **default risk of the underlying assets** onto capital markets.

Asset securitization involves a three-tier **originator—special purpose vehicle (SPV)—investor** structure, and legal and bankruptcy remoteness are its core mechanisms.

In 2024 the **balance of China's asset securitization business** passed 5.7 trillion yuan, and **asset-backed securities (ABS)** issuance ranked second in the world.

### The universal commercial bank

A universal commercial bank conducts both **commercial banking** (deposit-taking and lending) and **investment banking** (securities underwriting, M&A advisory, asset securitization) within a single institution. **Mixed-scope operations** dampen the impact of volatility in any one line of business on overall finances, but they also widen the channels for **conflicts of interest** and **risk contagion**.

The **Glass–Steagall Act of 1933** established separation of business in the United States, and the **Financial Services Modernization Act of 1999** lifted its core restrictions.

### The lender of last resort

The ultimate institutional arrangement for providing emergency loans to **institutions that have fallen into a liquidity crisis yet remain solvent** when the financial system suffers a sudden liquidity shock. Its theoretical basis is that a liquidity risk **is not a destruction of value**: timely liquidity support can avoid the liquidation losses of "**good assets at bad prices**".

The role of the lender of last resort:
- first, **breaking the self-reinforcement of a bank run** — runs have network effects, and a run at one bank will inevitably spread;
- second, **adjusting the monetary base through the "discount window"**, carrying monetary policy through to the market.

### The deposit insurance system

The deposit insurance system is the institutional arrangement under which premiums collected from banks build a **central insurance fund** that reimburses depositors when a bank fails. **"Deposit insurance — bank run"** is a classic paradox: deposit insurance can **reduce the incentive to run**, but it also **weakens depositors' willingness to monitor**, thereby **intensifying banks' moral hazard** (see the theory of collateral in lending in section 2 of [interest rate theory](/monetary/利率理论)).

The need for deposit insurance arises from **contagion** — the failure of a single bank can trigger systemic panic through **liquidation risk** and **outflows of deposits**. The **Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC)** established by the **Glass–Steagall Act of 1933** was the first modern deposit insurance system, after which the **United States, the United Kingdom, Canada and Japan** legislated in turn.

## Problems and analysis

### What sets a commercial bank apart from an ordinary enterprise?

What most distinguishes a commercial bank from other enterprises is **deposit-taking** — a deposit is a **withdrawable liability** that the depositor may redeem at any time. This differs essentially from the equity a typical enterprise issues: **equity** is a **residual claim**, with shareholders paid last, whereas a **deposit** is a **senior claim** ranking ahead of shareholders. This asymmetry gives a commercial bank's capital structure an inherent **"low equity + high leverage + short-term liabilities"** profile, so that the bank is **endogenously exposed to liquidity risk** (see the money demand theory in section 1 of [money supply and demand theory](/monetary/货币供求理论)).

- (1) **The scarcity of equity**: a bank's **"own equity / total assets"** ratio generally does not exceed 10%, and its **equity-to-assets** leverage reaches 10:1. Equity is the bank's "self-provided risk buffer," while deposits are "other people's money." When the **asset side** suffers bad debts, the loss is first absorbed by equity — but if equity is insufficient, the loss falls directly on depositors.
	As banks are far more risk-averse than ordinary enterprises and their equity is highly fragile, they depend heavily on the **stability of deposits**.
- (2) **The withdrawability of liabilities**: depositors may withdraw their deposits at any time, while loans are often long-term assets of five to thirty years. This **"short liabilities + long assets" maturity mismatch** leaves a bank permanently exposed to liquidity risk.
	An ordinary enterprise's payables mature on a fixed schedule; a bank's deposits are, in principle, callable on demand.
- (3) **Information asymmetry**: depositors cannot assess the **quality** of a bank's asset portfolio and can only protect themselves by **withdrawing deposits**. A **bank run** has a **positive feedback** — a run at one bank will inevitably cause outflows of deposits that may trigger a chain reaction.
	Depositors know only their own rights, never the composition of the bank's assets.
- (4) **Externalities of risk**: the losses from a bank failure are borne by the **entire financial system** and by **social savings**, not only by shareholders. This **"systemic risk"** characteristic is why banks must be subjected to **special regulation** — capital adequacy requirements, reserve requirements and deposit insurance (see the choices between corporate and personal financing in section 2 of [financial markets](/monetary/金融市场)).
	An ordinary enterprise's failure is contained by limited liability; a bank's failure propagates along the chain of claims and debts.

Seen from an economic perspective, the maturity mismatch between the **withdrawability of deposits** and the **long-term nature of loans** is essentially an **institutional design for bearing intertemporal risk** — the bank accepts **liquidity risk** in exchange for the **profit from maturity transformation**. This design works in normal times, but once **information asymmetry becomes known to the market**, the distinction between **"good" and "bad" banks** collapses and a **run** becomes the rational choice.

Hence the **information asymmetry** among the three parties — **depositors, banks and borrowers** — is not a simple market failure but an **endogenous problem of the design of the banking system itself**. Modern financial regulation (such as the Basel framework) is essentially an institutional response to the fundamental contradiction of **information asymmetry and risk externalities**.

The sudden collapse of **Credit Suisse** in 2023 is a classic case of **withdrawable liabilities plus information asymmetry**: depositors withdrew **tens of billions of Swiss francs** within hours, the bank could not liquidate its **long-term bond** assets in reasonable time, and the Swiss central bank ultimately provided emergency liquidity support. The failure of **Silicon Valley Bank** (March 2023) likewise highlighted the maturity-mismatch risk between **short-term liabilities and long-term assets**.

The deeper implication is this: **the stability of the financial intermediary system depends not only on the prudent operation of individual banks but on the overall design of information, regulation and institutions**.

### Where does a bank's money come from, and where does it go?

A commercial bank's **funds** come from **liabilities** (deposits) and **equity** (share capital); its **uses of funds** are **assets** (loans, investments) and **cash**.

- ① **Sources of funds**: deposits (demand and time deposits), interbank borrowing, bond issuance, shareholders' capital, retained earnings
- ② **Uses of funds**: loans (short-term and long-term), securities investments (government bonds, corporate bonds, stocks), cash and interbank placements, other assets
- ③ **Core businesses**: taking deposits, making loans, providing settlement services
- ④ **Off-balance-sheet business**: guarantees, commitments, derivatives (see Off-balance-sheet business)

A commercial bank's **balance sheet** reflects the correspondence between the **liability side (sources of funds)** and the **asset side (uses of funds)**. The **capital adequacy ratio** (equity / risk-weighted assets) is one of the core regulatory indicators.

### Why do banks grow ever more "universal"? The division between the two operating models and the trend

Classified by the **scope** of business, commercial banks fall into two types:

- ① **Specialized banks**: conduct only commercial banking (taking deposits, making loans, providing settlement services)
- ② **Universal banks**: conduct commercial banking + investment banking + insurance + trust + asset management

The **Glass–Steagall Act of 1933** established separation of business in the United States, and the **Financial Services Modernization Act of 1999** lifted its core restrictions. The **Dodd–Frank Act of 2010** then imposed the "Volcker Rule" restricting proprietary trading. **China** has practised **"separated business, separated regulation"** since 2002, and the 2005 revision of the *Company Law* permitted the establishment of **"financial holding companies"**, effectively introducing **limited mixed-scope operations**.

The advantages of universal operations lie in **economies of scale and economies of scope** — a single client can be offered a range of services, lowering transaction costs; the disadvantages are **conflicts of interest** (proprietary trading versus underwriting, investment versus lending) and **risk contagion**. The **2008 subprime crisis** was partly attributed to the **excessive expansion of universal banks** and to the **blurred boundaries of shadow banking**.

The contemporary trend is a compromise of **"separated business by function, mixed-scope at the institution"** — different lines of business are legally placed in separate institutions but are managed and coordinated **at the group level**.

By 2024 the **"financial holding company"** structures of **China's** major banking groups were essentially in place, and the **"parent–subsidiary"** model had become mainstream. The establishment of **fintech subsidiaries** (such as **JD Digits** and the **Ant Group**) likewise reflects the **outward extension of the mixed-scope boundary**.

In **2023** the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision proposed a **"bank-versus-fintech boundary"**, taking **systemic significance** rather than **whether a licence is held** as the criterion for classifying financial intermediaries.

### What directly gave rise to financial innovation?

Financial innovation is the creation of **new products, new businesses or new processes** by financial institutions in response to **changes in technology, institutions, regulation and market constraints**. The direct causes fall into three categories:

- (1) **"Evasion of regulation" — the prime motive of financial innovation.** To circumvent regulatory requirements such as **capital adequacy**, **reserve requirements** and **permissible scope of business**, financial institutions devise instruments such as off-balance-sheet business, asset securitization and financial derivatives.
	Financial innovation often begins with the search for regulatory gaps — the very reason it is "innovation."
- (2) **Technological progress.** Computers, the internet, blockchain and other technologies lower **transaction costs** and **information costs**, making financial innovation possible.
	Lower information costs make it possible to price risk more finely, and thus to structure new products.
- (3) **Market demand.** Customer demand for **more diversified, customized and convenient** financial services drives innovation in products and services.
	Emerging demand for pension, insurance and wealth-management services is the driving force behind innovations in bank products.

The **"safe haven effect"** of financial innovation means that innovation typically **outpaces regulation**. The **Basel framework** and the **G-SIB surcharge** are regulation's response to financial innovation, but **regulatory arbitrage** persists.

Between 2020 and 2023, **crypto-assets and stablecoins** were a typical financial innovation — **blockchain** technology enabled **peer-to-peer transactions** that bypassed traditional intermediaries, and **disintermediation** challenged the **"deposit—loan" intermediary chain**. Yet **shadow stablecoins** (such as **USDT** and **USDC**) have in effect rebuilt a **new intermediary system**, producing a **"disintermediation—reintermediation"** cycle.

The **creative-destruction** nature of financial innovation means regulation must keep adjusting — **"regulatory sandboxes"** and **functional regulation** are the two main paths for responding to innovation.

The **Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA)** took effect in the European Union in 2024, the first international model of **functional regulation**.

The **systemic risk** of financial innovation makes the **"paradox of regulation and innovation"** conspicuous — **excessive regulation stifles innovation, while inadequate regulation amplifies systemic risk**.

## Synthetic essay

### Why did the central bank come into being? What functions does it perform?

- (1) The reasons the central bank came into being
	- ① The need for unified issue of banknotes.
	In the early stages of the development of banking, numerous banks were engaged in the issue of banknotes.
	But the following problems arose:
		- a. The numerous small banks had weak creditworthiness, and the banknotes they issued often could not be redeemed — particularly in a crisis, which easily provoked panic.
		- b. Many scattered small banks had credit operations confined to a region, so the banknotes they issued could circulate only within a limited area.

	- ② With banks multiplying and banking business expanding continuously, and debt and credit relationships becoming intricate and complex, if bill exchange and clearing could not be handled in a timely and reasonable manner it would damage the normal operation of banking business. A unified clearing centre was therefore objectively necessary.

	- ③ In the course of the business cycle, commercial banks often fell into the predicament of being unable to arrange funds flexibly, and sometimes went bankrupt as a result. An institution that could provide financial assistance to commercial banks when they ran short of funds and were caught in a run was therefore objectively needed.

	- ④ As in every other industry, competition in banking was fierce, and the disruption that bankruptcies and failures caused the economy was far more serious than the failure of an ordinary industrial or commercial firm. An institution to supervise and coordinate banks' operations was therefore objectively required.

- (2) The functions of the central bank

	- ① The bank of issue
	As the bank of issue means to monopolize the right to issue banknotes and to become the sole issuer of cash nationwide. Today the cash of virtually every country in the world is issued exclusively by its central bank. For example, the **Federal Reserve System** (the Fed) is the sole issuer of cash in the United States, and the **People's Bank of China** is the sole issuer of the **renminbi**.

	- ② The bank of banks
	The business inherent to banks — handling "deposits, loans and remittances" — is likewise a central bank's main line of business, except that the counterparties are not ordinary industrial or commercial enterprises but other financial institutions, above all commercial banks. Specifically it comprises:

	 - a. Concentrating reserve deposits. Typically the law requires commercial banks and related financial institutions to deposit a portion of their deposits with the central bank (the statutory reserve requirement) so as to protect depositors' interests and the safety of the entire banking system.

	- b. The lender of last resort. Around the middle of the nineteenth century, successive economic disturbances and financial crises made it clear to people that once a financial panic broke out it would spread into a full-scale crisis and affect the normal operation of the whole economy. An institution was therefore needed to act as the "lender of last resort," providing financial assistance to financial institutions in crisis.
		- first, supporting commercial banks and other financial institutions that have fallen into a shortage of funds, so as to prevent the spread of a bank run from ultimately bringing down the entire banking system;
		- second, by providing short-term financial accommodation to commercial banks, adjusting the scale of credit and the money supply and thereby transmitting and implementing the intent of macroeconomic control.

	- c. Organizing national clearing. The modern payment and clearing system has developed alongside the central banking system. Central banks in most countries organize the settlement of funds between commercial banks through their own "current account" system. For example, the **Federal Reserve System** organizes the settlement of funds between US commercial banks through the **Fedwire** clearing system, and the **People's Bank of China** organizes the settlement of funds between Chinese commercial banks through the **High Value Payment System (HVPS)** and the **Bulk Electronic Payments System (BEPS)**.

	- ③ The bank of the state

	As the bank of the state means that the central bank, on behalf of the state, implements fiscal and financial policies, manages receipts and payments of the treasury, and provides various financial services to the state. Specifically:
		- a. Managing the national treasury.
		- b. Handling the issuance of state bonds.
		- c. Providing credit support to the state finances, mainly through the following two methods:
				- lending directly to the state finances;
				- purchasing government bonds.
		- d. Safeguarding foreign exchange and gold reserves, and conducting foreign exchange and gold trading and management.
		- e. Formulating and implementing monetary policy.
		- f. Formulating financial management rules and regulations and supervising their implementation.

	In addition, as the bank of the state the central bank also represents the government in international financial organizations, attends international conferences, conducts international foreign exchange and gold transactions, arranges international borrowing and lending, and provides international financial intelligence.

There is one conspicuous gap in timing between the four reasons for the central bank's emergence and its three functions, and it is worth making explicit: reasons ① and ② had already held true in the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries, yet a genuine central bank did not appear until the end of the nineteenth century — **the central bank is not a product of historical inevitability but a product of institutional choice**. The contemporary forms of functions ② and ③ (such as the Federal Reserve's discount window and the People's Bank of China's payment and clearing system) were only completed in the twentieth century, while function ①, the monopoly of "unified issue," is being reassessed today through the concept of the **seigniorage**: the base money issued by a central bank is a **zero-interest liability**, and seigniorage is essentially the **time value of money creation**. This gap in timing is directly related to the dispute over **exogenous versus endogenous** money creation in money supply and demand theory (see the money supply theory in section 3 of [money supply and demand theory](/monetary/货币供求理论)).

## Limits and what follows

- **There is no once-and-for-all answer to "why intermediaries are needed."** The "money—savings intermediary" explanation that dominated before the 1960s was **re-examined after 2008** — the existence of **shadow banking** shows that the core function of financial intermediation (**maturity transformation**) need not be performed by a licensed bank, an insight that directly drove the formulation of the **G-SIB regulatory framework**.
- **The debate between separation and mixed-scope operations was reopened after 2008, but it did not return to 1933.** The crisis tested **universal banks** — both **Lehman Brothers** (separated business, but with excessive **proprietary trading**) and **Bear Stearns** (mixed-scope) fell at the same time, showing that the **scope of business** is not the determinant of risk; **leverage plus maturity mismatch** is.
- **The contemporary forms of most concepts in this chapter lie outside the regulatory framework.** In 2014 the Financial Stability Board gave shadow banking a **broad—narrow** definition, and in 2020 the Basel framework issued more detailed risk-weighting rules for **off-balance-sheet derivatives**. But **crypto-asset intermediation** (such as **DeFi**) is currently **not** covered by any formal Basel framework.
- **A main line in China's financial intermediary system is "structural reshuffling" rather than replacement of concepts.** The restoration and establishment of rural credit cooperatives from 1979, the founding of the Bank of China in 1984, the three policy banks in 1994, deposit insurance in 2003, and G-SIB additional supervision in 2013 — each step is **institutional evolution**, yet the core concept of the **"deposit—loan intermediary"** has not changed since the seventeenth century.
