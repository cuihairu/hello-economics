# Chapter 7. Monetary policy

> **The core question of this chapter**: what are the goals of monetary policy? What policy instruments are there? How does the transmission mechanism run? How is the effect of policy evaluated?

## The arc of this chapter

The four blocks of this chapter (goals and intermediate indicators, instruments, transmission channels, effects and coordination) are in fact the outcome of monetary policy being rebuilt again and again over the twentieth century, and the order of the rebuilding is exactly the reverse of their arrangement. The instruments took shape first: the Federal Reserve set up an open market committee in 1922 and concentrated deposit reserves in the Federal Reserve System in 1935, and the three weapons - required reserves, rediscounting and open market operations - each had their own antecedents before that (the lender-of-last-resort idea goes back to Bagehot's *Lombard Street* of 1873). In the 1950s-70s policy had to land through intermediate indicators: Friedman argued for targeting the money supply, Keynesians preferred targeting the interest rate, in 1970 the Fed switched to non-borrowed reserves as its near-term indicator, and in October 1979 Volcker went further and declared the monetary aggregates the target. What happened next decides why the last two concepts of this chapter exist - financial innovation destabilised the money demand function in the mid-1980s, the monetary aggregates could no longer serve as an anchor, and Kydland and Prescott (1977) and Barro and Gordon (1983) showed that the time inconsistency of discretion devalues itself, so the policy framework turned to "rules plus credibility": the Reserve Bank of New Zealand formally adopted inflation targeting from 1990, with Canada, Britain and Sweden following in turn; Taylor (1993) supplied a testable interest rate rule against which the Fed's actual operations could be judged loose or tight. The transmission mechanism section (the credit channel) is likewise a product of this period: Bernanke and Gertler and others found that if only the interest rate channel is told, the real effect of monetary policy is far larger than the models predicted, so bank lending and borrowers' balance sheets were added to the chain. As for how fiscal and monetary policy are paired, Meade and Tinbergen worked out the "targets and instruments" arithmetic in the 1950s, which Chapter 10 treats formally. The Chinese experience forms a line of its own: in 1984 the People's Bank began to exercise central bank functions exclusively, in 1998 the credit quota was abolished in favour of indirect regulation, for a long stretch afterwards $M_2$ and aggregate social financing served as intermediate targets, and after 2013 instruments such as the Standing Lending Facility (SLF) and the Medium-term Lending Facility (MLF) were created, moving gradually toward price-based regulation - the end of this chapter returns to that line.

## Core concepts

### Inflation targeting

Inflation targeting is a framework used for monetary policy decision-making, a monetary policy regime in which the central bank takes inflation directly as its target and announces that target to the public.
Under inflation targeting, the traditional monetary policy system has undergone a major change: no intermediate target is set between the policy instruments and the ultimate goal, and the basis for monetary policy decisions rests mainly on regular forecasts of inflation.
The government or central bank sets in advance the medium- and long-term inflation target for the country over the coming period, and the central bank, under the public's supervision, applies the corresponding monetary policy instruments so that the actual value of inflation matches the forecast target.

The reason this framework abolishes the intermediate target is that it swaps the anchor: instead of promising to "control some monetary aggregate," it promises directly a result the public can check. The nominal anchor moves from the quantity of money to inflation expectations, the precondition being that the central bank must have credibility - able to state a target, to deliver it, and to admit it when it fails. For that very reason inflation targeting is more like a contract with the public than a set of technical parameters; its successes and its failures both depend on whether central bank independence and fiscal discipline can hold that contract up.

### The monetary policy lag

The monetary policy lag is the time that elapses from the making of a policy to the obtaining of its main or full effect. The monetary policy lag is the main factor affecting the effectiveness of policy.
The monetary policy lag consists of two parts:
- The inside lag
	- The recognition lag
	- The action lag
- The outside lag

The inside lag can be further divided into the recognition lag and the action lag; the outside lag is also called the impact lag.

The lag is not a technical detail but a question of the legitimacy of policy. Friedman's objection (1961) rests precisely here: the outside lag is both long and unstable, and by the time a policy takes effect the economy may long since have turned of its own accord, so that "counter-cyclical operation" in practice often becomes a pro-cyclical disturbance, the stabiliser turning into a source of disturbance. This argument is the most important ground for the move toward rules after the 1970s (including the Taylor rule below).

### The Taylor rule

The Taylor rule is an operating method of adjusting the interest rate according to the relative changes of output and inflation.
The rule embodies the way the central bank's short-term interest rate instrument is adjusted according to the state of the economy.
Taylor held that, to keep the real short-term interest rate stable and the policy stance neutral, when the output gap is positive (negative) and the inflation gap exceeds (falls below) the target, the real interest rate should be raised (lowered).

The motive for proposing the rule was quite specific: Taylor (1993), recomputing the Fed's real interest rate with data from 1987-1993, found the operations of the Greenspan period looser than the rule indicated, and on that ground argued for constraining discretion with a rule. The formula was later made to serve double duty - both as a yardstick for judging whether policy is loose or tight, and as a proposal for replacing policy. It also has a clear point of failure: when inflation is below target and the output gap negative, the nominal interest rate the rule calls for may drop below zero, which cannot happen in reality (the effective lower bound on nominal rates); after 2008 central banks' rates ran below the values the Taylor rule recommended for a long time - precisely where the zero lower bound and the unconventional instruments appeared.

## Problems and analysis

### Why does monetary policy need intermediate indicators? What conditions must they satisfy?

In the application of monetary policy, the policy indicators must satisfy the following conditions:
- (1) The intermediate targets of monetary policy must be controllable by the monetary authorities.
- (2) There must be a stable relation between the policy indicators and the final goals.
- (3) Measurability
	- First, the central bank must be able to obtain accurate data on the relevant indicators in good time
	- Second, there must be a fairly clear definition, to facilitate analysis, observation and monitoring
- (4) The policy indicators must be subject to relatively little interference from external or non-policy factors.
- (5) Good adaptability to the economic system and the financial system.

Generally, in transactions on a free credit market the monetary authority can choose only one of the money supply and the interest rate as its policy indicator.
If the authority chooses the level of the interest rate as its policy indicator, the money supply becomes an endogenous variable, determined by relations inside the economic system;
if it chooses the money supply as its policy indicator, the interest rate becomes an endogenous variable. Which of the money supply or the interest rate is chosen as the policy indicator depends on the various sources of instability in the economy.
To maintain economic stability, the monetary authority should choose its policy indicator with care.

The constraint that "only one of the two can be chosen" is the key: the central bank cannot pin down both the interest rate and the quantity of money at once, because it controls only base money, while the interest rate and the quantity of money are determined together by one market. Which one to choose depends on which side the disturbance comes from - if the shocks lie mainly in money demand (payment technology, asset substitution), pegging the interest rate is more stable; if the shocks lie in goods and investment demand, pegging the quantity of money is more stable (Poole's 1970 instruments-choice analysis). After the money demand function destabilised in the 1980s, the countries abandoned the monetary aggregate anchor - precisely this judgement being overturned by reality (see "Limits and what follows" at the end of this chapter).

### Why do the expectations of microeconomic agents offset the effects of monetary policy?

- (1) The expectations of microeconomic agents are an important factor affecting the effectiveness of monetary policy, because the expectations of microeconomic agents have an offsetting effect on the monetary policy effect once implemented.
	This offsetting works as follows: out of concern for their own economic interests, microeconomic agents form rational expectations about monetary policy, and on the basis of those expectations they widely adopt courses of action favourable to their own economic interests, and these actions have an offsetting effect on monetary policy, so that monetary policy cannot produce the effect it expected.

- (2) Given the expectations of microeconomic agents, it would seem that monetary policy can take effect or achieve its expected result only when its orientation and strength are not, or not fully, known to the public.
	But in reality the public's expectations are not necessarily accurate every time, and turning expectations into actual actions (re-signing contracts, adjusting the timing of borrowing, rewriting wage clauses) also takes time. Thus monetary policy can still work, but the public's expectational behaviour discounts the policy effect.

- (3) A game-theoretic analysis of the offsetting effect.
	By the view of the rational expectations school, if the public is rational in its expectations, then only monetary policy not expected by the public can be effective. By the same token, if the government's expectations are rational, then only public behaviour not anticipated by the government will have an offsetting effect on monetary policy. So the process by which government and public formulate their actions according to their expectations can be explained by game theory, that is, there is a game between government and public.

The modern version of this game is the argument that "rules beat discretion": if the central bank can renege at any time, the public will price that possibility into wage and price contracts, inflation expectations will be pushed up, and output will gain nothing; conversely, if the central bank ties itself down institutionally (independent, with a clear target, and paying a price for breaking its word), the public's expectations will come down first, and policy can hold down inflation at smaller cost. This line of thought later grew into two institutional designs: legislatively confirmed central bank independence and the inflation targeting mentioned above - both trade "flexibility" away for "the public's trust."

## Synthetic essay

### What are the features of the three major monetary policy instruments? Why is open market operations the one most used in daily practice?

- (1) The reserve requirement policy

	- ① The reserve requirement policy refers to the policy measure by which the central bank, within the rights granted it by law, prescribes or adjusts the ratio of deposit reserves that commercial banks must deposit with the central bank, so as to change the money multiplier, thereby controlling the credit-creating capacity of commercial banks and indirectly controlling the supply of money in society.
	
	- ② The main contents of the reserve requirement policy are:
		- First, prescribing the ratio of deposit reserves.
		- Second, prescribing the kinds of assets that may serve as required deposit reserves.
		- Third, prescribing the base on which deposit reserves are computed.
		- Fourth, prescribing the range of adjustment of the deposit reserve ratio, and so on.
	
	- ③ The process by which the policy works is: when the central bank raises the required deposit reserve ratio,
		- on the one hand, the required reserves commercial banks must hand over to the central bank increase, their excess reserves fall, and their capacity to lend and create credit declines;
		- on the other hand, the rise in the required reserve ratio makes the money multiplier smaller, and so reduces the capacity of the whole commercial banking system to create credit and expand the scale of credit; the result is that credit in society tightens, the money supply shrinks, interest rates rise, and investment and social expenditure are curtailed accordingly. The process runs the other way in the opposite case.
	
	- ④ The reserve requirement policy produces strong effects on market interest rates, the money supply, public expectations and so on, which is at once its advantage and its drawback.

- (2) The rediscount policy

- ① The rediscount policy is the financial policy by which the central bank, by setting or adjusting the rediscount rate, intervenes in and influences market interest rates and the supply of and demand in the money market, thereby regulating the market supply of money.

- ② The rediscount policy includes two aspects:
	- One is the adjustment of the rediscount rate.
	- The other is prescribing which bills qualify for rediscounting with the central bank.

- ③ Its process is: when the central bank judges it necessary to tighten credit and reduce the market supply of money, it raises the rediscount rate above the market rate, which raises the cost to commercial banks of rediscounting with the central bank; commercial banks then reduce the amount they borrow or discount from the central bank, and their reserves contract. The commercial banks can then only curtail loans and investment to their customers, reducing the market supply of money, tightening credit, raising market interest rates, and correspondingly reducing society's demand for money. The process runs the other way in the opposite case.

- ④ The effects of applying the rediscount policy:
	- One, it can affect the funding costs and excess reserves of commercial banks, and so their financing decisions, changing their lending and investment behaviour;
	- Two, it can produce an announcement effect, influencing the expectations of commercial banks and of the public at large;
	- Three, it can decide to whom the discount window is opened, influencing the direction in which commercial banks deploy their funds, and can also stop commercial banks from using the discount window for arbitrage.

- ⑤ The defects of the rediscount policy:
	- One, the central bank is in a passive position when using this instrument to control the money supply;
	- Two, the rediscount rate can affect only the general level of interest rates, not the structure of interest rates;
	- Three, the rediscount policy lacks flexibility.

- (3) The open market policy

- ① The open market policy is the policy measure by which the central bank openly buys and sells various securities in the financial market, to control the money supply and influence the level of market interest rates.

- ② Its process is:
	- When funds are scarce in the financial market, the central bank buys securities through open market operations, which is in effect the injection of a sum of base money.
	- If this base money flows into the hands of the public, it directly increases the supply of money in society; if it flows into commercial banks, it increases their excess reserves and, through the working of the money multiplier, expands the scale of bank credit, multiplying the money supply in society. Conversely, when there is too much money in the financial market, the central bank can sell securities to reduce base money, so that the money supply falls and credit tightens.

- ③ Compared with the previous two policies, the advantages of the open market policy are very obvious: strong initiative; high flexibility; smooth, low-shock regulation; wide range of influence.

The three instruments differ enormously in how often they are actually used, the reason being a set of trade-offs: impact and initiative cannot be had together. A change in the required reserve ratio forces every bank in the system to adjust at once, with power enough to shake business expectations, so most countries treat it as an instrument of announcement rather than of daily use (China is the exception, adjusting frequently between 2004 and 2015, precisely because the base money injected through funds outstanding for foreign exchange was so large that a powerful hedging instrument was needed). The rediscount rate can only wait for banks to come to the door - the initiative lies with the borrower - and any adjustment affects the holders of already-discounted bills, so central banks usually leave it parked at the "lender-of-last-resort price." Open market operations can move billions in a day and be reversed in the same direction the next, and a mistake can be squared the next morning, which made them the daily instrument of the central banks of developed countries - the Fed from the mid-1920s turned from a passive seller into an active instrument of regulation (system-wide open market operations were established in 1922, an investment committee in 1923, and after 1953 market operations wholly replaced the discount window as the instrument of day-to-day regulation); this path explains the modern central bank's attachment to it.

### Besides the interest rate channel, through which credit channels does monetary policy affect the real economy?

Why look for channels beyond the interest rate? Because experience does not match. Bernanke and Blinder (1988) found that after a contraction of the money supply, the fall in industrial output far exceeded what the standard chain of "higher rates curb investment" could explain; at the same time bank credit fell noticeably faster than other forms of finance. That is, tightening does not only make money dearer, it makes it scarcer, even unobtainable - and those hit hardest are precisely the borrowers who can rely only on banks. This gap gave birth to a theory of the credit channel symmetrical to the money channel: information asymmetry makes "who cannot borrow" itself a consequence of policy. Research by Western scholars shows that in the credit market there are two basic channels of monetary transmission: the bank lending channel and the balance sheet channel.

- (1) The bank lending channel

The mechanism by which monetary policy is transmitted through the bank lending channel rests on the recognition that banks play a special role in the financial system, because they are especially well suited to dealing with particular types of borrowers, especially small firms, where the asymmetric information problem is especially acute. Large firms, of course, need not go through banks but can reach the credit market directly through stock and bond markets. A contractionary monetary policy that reduces bank reserves and bank deposits will have its effect through these borrowers. A fall in the money supply reduces bank deposits ( D ), which in turn reduces the loans banks extend. The fall in bank loans ( L ) reduces firms' investment, and so total output falls. This monetary policy effect can be expressed as:

$$M ↓→ D ↓→ L ↓→ I ↓→Y ↓$$

- (2) The balance sheet channel

The balance sheet exercises monetary transmission through firms' net worth, resting on the recognition that a very low net worth means that lenders in effect have very little security for their loans, and likewise that losses from adverse selection increase. A fall in net worth gives rise to the adverse selection problem, and so reduces lending as a source of funds for investment spending. A firm's very low net worth also enlarges the moral hazard problem, because it means the owners hold a lower equity stake in their firm, which stimulates them to invest in risky projects. Since engaging in high-risk undertakings makes it less likely that lenders recover their loans, the fall in firms' net worth leads to less lending and hence less investment spending.

Monetary policy can affect firms' balance sheets in several ways. A contractionary monetary policy ($M ↓$) causes security prices to fall, $P_e ↓$, which further lowers firms' net worth. With the increase in adverse selection and moral hazard problems, this leads to a fall in investment spending $(I ↓)$ and in aggregate demand ($Y ↓$). This yields the following scheme for the balance sheet channel of monetary transmission:

$$M ↓→ P_e ↓→\text{adverse selection}↑\text{ and moral hazard}↑→\text{loans } L ↓→ I ↓→Y ↓$$
A tightening policy that raises interest rates also worsens firms' balance sheets, because it reduces cash flow. This yields the following additional scheme for the balance sheet channel:

$$M ↓→ r ↑→\text{cash flow}↓→\text{moral hazard}↑\text{ and adverse selection}↑→\text{loans } L ↓→ I ↓→Y ↓$$
The credit channel is not confined to the spending of firms; it applies equally to consumer spending. A fall in bank lending caused by monetary tightening will necessarily reduce consumers' purchases of consumer durables and housing, for they have no other sources of credit. Likewise, a rise in interest rates worsens households' balance sheets, because their cash flow is affected in the opposite direction.

Another way of examining how the balance sheet works through consumers is the study of the liquidity effect on spending for consumer durables and housing. By the view of the liquidity effect, the balance sheet effect operates through its influence on consumers' desire to spend rather than on lenders' willingness to lend. In this model, if consumers expect that they are more likely to fall into financial distress, they will prefer to hold fewer illiquid assets such as consumer durables and housing and more liquid financial assets. The underlying reason is that if consumers need to sell their consumer durables and housing to raise money, they expect to suffer a heavy loss, because in a fire sale they will not obtain their full value. Financial assets such as stocks and bonds, by contrast, can much more easily be turned into cash at full market value.

This yields another mechanism of monetary transmission that works by linking money and asset values. When falling stock prices lower the value of financial assets, spending on consumer durables and housing will also fall, because consumers judge such durable goods financially unsafe and more exposed to financial loss. In schematic form:

$$M ↓→ P_e ↓→\text{financial assets}↓→\text{possibility of financial losses}↑→\text{spending on consumer durables and housing}↓→Y ↓$$

A rise in interest rates squeezes consumers' cash flow, and so depresses spending on consumer durables and housing - the illiquidity of these two kinds of assets themselves supplies another explanation. The fall in consumers' cash flow increases the possibility of financial loss, which reduces consumers' wish to hold consumer durables and housing, and this cuts spending in these areas and total output with it.

### Why must monetary and fiscal policy be coordinated? What modes of pairing are there?

- (1) The basis for coordinating fiscal policy and monetary policy

Fiscal policy and monetary policy are the two great policies of the government's macroeconomic regulation, both being policies of demand management. In the state's macroeconomic intervention in the economy the focal point is to move the market's aggregate supply and aggregate demand back into equilibrium, so as to achieve the goals of growth, employment, stability and equilibrium in the balance of payments. To move aggregate supply and aggregate demand from disequilibrium back to equilibrium, one may regulate aggregate supply, or aggregate demand, or both at once. As far as short-run equilibrium is concerned, the key is to regulate aggregate demand, which is easier to adjust in the short run, so that it reaches equilibrium with aggregate supply, which is comparatively hard to adjust in the short run. That is the meaning of demand management policy. Since the carrier of market demand is money, regulating market demand is regulating the money supply. In other words, the operation of demand management policy cannot do without adjusting the money supply - either increasing it or contracting it. It is so for monetary policy and equally so for fiscal policy. That is the basis on which the two should, and can, be coordinated. As for their difference, from the angle of regulating the money supply it lies only in this: one works through the banking system, the other through the fiscal system; one employs financial instruments, the other fiscal and tax instruments; one takes effect through the financial transmission mechanism, the other through the fiscal transmission mechanism.

One doubt about this account that "both are regulating the money supply" is worth preserving: is a fiscal expansion actually adding to demand, or merely moving purchasing power from the private sector to the government? If the extra issue of government bonds raises interest rates and private investment falls in step, the necessity of coordination has to be reassessed - this is precisely the old problem of the crowding-out effect in Chapter 11 of *Western Economics* (macroeconomic policy). This chapter, following the textbook's account, treats it as "two sides of the same coin," and readers should remember that this simplification is conditional.

- (2) The modes of pairing fiscal policy and monetary policy

- ① Before the 1930s the dominant economic theories all opposed excessive government intervention in the economy. Except in wartime, governments consistently followed the fiscal principle of balancing revenue and expenditure. In the monetary sphere the highest ideal was the once-existent gold standard. The great economic crisis of the 1930s and the "Keynesian revolution" brought forth policies of macroeconomic intervention. Since the financial system showed little capacity for expansion, fiscal policy was elevated to the first place among anti-crisis policies.

- ② Entering the 1940s, the long depression was for a time replaced by the prosperity the war brought. Insufficient effective demand was no longer the main contradiction; coping with huge budget deficits and severe inflation became instead the pressing demand. In this new situation the Keynesians went on to propose the "compensatory fiscal and monetary policy," that is, alternating contraction and expansion according to whether the economy ran hot or cold.

- ③ Samuelson, a representative of the post-Keynesian school, stressed the efficacy of "mixing tight and loose" between fiscal policy and monetary policy. The meaning is to weigh the most urgent problems of the economy, and according to the respective applicability of fiscal policy and monetary policy, adopt either a mix of loose fiscal policy with tight monetary policy, or tight fiscal policy with loose monetary policy. Of course, particular economic conditions may also require "double loose" or "double tight." Since the mid-1990s our country has, according to different economic conditions, put forward several successive guidelines for pairing fiscal and monetary policy. In a weak, depressed economy, if one wants expansionary macroeconomic policy to overcome insufficient demand and turn the economy warm, monetary policy is inferior to fiscal policy. In an over-cool economy, commercial banks lack the incentive to expand credit, firms lack the incentive to expand investment spending, and consumers equally lack the incentive to expand consumption spending. Not only are their behavioural intentions not necessarily aligned with the monetary authorities, they do not take orders from the monetary authorities either. So even if the central bank carries out an expansionary monetary policy, it is not easy to obtain an actual expansionary effect. Fiscal policy is different: expanded fiscal investment is a good opportunity for private firms to maintain and even expand their operations without bearing the investment risk themselves. Investment spending and the consumption spending it drives, together with the consumption spending directly drawn out by increased fiscal social security and social welfare expenditure, often plays the key role in turning back an economic downslide.

- ④ Yet in an overheated economy, if one wants contractionary macroeconomic policy to restrain excessive demand, so as to overcome inflation and false prosperity, fiscal policy is inferior to monetary policy. The central bank's role in restraining the growth of the money supply is strong and powerful:
	- First, it has many instruments it can employ;
	- Second, it can exert a strong and effective constraint on the commercial banks that create deposit money. Fiscal policy, if it is to contract, faces considerable difficulty. For all countries, fiscal expenditure has an extremely strong rigidity: it is hard not only to cut in absolute terms but even to cut the rate of increase. Adjustments of tax and expenditure policy must all pass through legislative procedures, and questions such as raising taxes or reducing welfare expenditure are hard to get approved.

At the same time, whether monetary policy or fiscal policy, whenever its positive and favourable side is used, there always accompany it inevitable negative side-effects. Using the two in combination may moderate those side-effects somewhat.

## Limits and what follows

- **Monetary aggregates as intermediate targets were falsified by reality.** In October 1979 Volcker turned to targeting non-borrowed reserves and the monetary aggregates; in the short run this did suppress inflation expectations, but financial innovation clearly destabilised the velocity of $M_1$ and $M_2$ and the money demand function in the mid-1980s, and after 1982 the Fed in effect abandoned the aggregate targets. By the 1990s the main central banks generally no longer took any monetary aggregate as an anchor, switching instead to a policy interest rate plus expectations management (the first concept of this chapter is precisely the institutionalisation of that turn). Today the IMF and the academic world still argue over "whether inflation targeting is to blame for the post-pandemic high inflation": one side holds that it put too much weight on output stability and delayed the 2021 tightening; the other, that the problem lay in the "transitory" judgement rather than in the framework itself.
- **The length of the transmission chain depends on the financial structure.** The credit channel operates markedly in bank-dominated economies - under Japan's "main bank" system and the bank-centred financing structures of continental Europe, tightening directly compresses the lending available; in the United States after the 1980s the expansion of securitisation and the corporate bond market let large firms bypass banks, and the bank lending channel weakened accordingly, while after 2008 the problems of "banks' reluctance to lend" and "failure of policy transmission" were exposed, so that quantitative easing (QE), negative interest rates and forward guidance were taken up as means of forcing the chain back together when the regular channels broke - their effects and side-effects still being assessed.
- **The tests of expectation offsetting and time inconsistency are not clean.** The Lucas critique (1976) declared that evaluating policy with historical regression coefficients is unreliable, and rational expectations yielded the strong proposition that "policy that is expected is ineffective"; yet in the actual data the short-run trade-off between inflation and output has always been there (because wage and price contracts are re-signed in stages - precisely the New Keynesian explanation). The verdict experience gives is moderate: expectations do weaken rather than cancel the force of policy, and credibility does lower the cost of stabilising inflation - which is why "central bank independence + a clear target" became the global mainstream configuration after the 1990s.
- **China's practice attaches a whole set of additional conditions to this chapter's framework.** After the credit quota controls were abolished in 1998 China turned to quantity-based regulation ($M_2$, the scale of credit); from 2003 to 2013, because funds outstanding for foreign exchange expanded continuously, the required reserve ratio and central bank bills were used at high frequency as hedges; from 2013 instruments such as the SLF, the MLF and the Pledged Supplementary Lending (PSL) were created; the deposit rate ceiling was freed in 2015, and the 2019 reform of the Loan Prime Rate (LPR) linked the policy rate to lending rates; in 2024 the central bank further made clear it would narrow the interest rate corridor (anchored on the 7-day reverse repo operating rate, extended 50 basis points to each side) and proposed gradually playing down quantity-based intermediate targets. In other words, this chapter's three-stage scheme of "instruments - intermediate indicators - ultimate goals" is in China a template being rewritten, not copied.
