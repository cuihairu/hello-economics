# Chapter 7. Monetary policy

> **The core question of this chapter**: what are the objectives of monetary policy? What instruments are there? How does the transmission mechanism work? And how are policy effects assessed?

## The arc of this chapter

The four blocks of this chapter (objectives and intermediate indicators, instruments, transmission channels, effects and coordination) are in fact the result of monetary policy being reconstructed again and again in the twentieth century, and the order of that reconstruction is exactly the reverse of their arrangement here. The instruments took shape first: the Federal Reserve set up its open market committee in 1922 and centralised reserve deposits in the Federal Reserve System in 1935, and before then the three weapons of required reserves, rediscounting and open market operations each already had a pedigree of their own (the lender-of-last-resort idea can be traced back to Bagehot's *Lombard Street* of 1873). In the 1950s–70s, policy had to land through intermediate indicators: Friedman advocated pegging the money supply, Keynesians leaned towards pegging the interest rate, in 1970 the Federal Reserve switched to non-borrowed reserves as its near-term indicator, and in October 1979 Volcker went further still and announced monetary aggregates as the target. What happened next explains why the latter two concepts of this chapter exist — financial innovation destabilised the money demand function in the mid-1980s, monetary aggregates could no longer serve as the anchor, and Kydland and Prescott (1977) together with Barro and Gordon (1983) proved that the time inconsistency of discretionary action depreciates itself, so the policy framework turned to “rules plus reputation”: the Reserve Bank of New Zealand formally adopted inflation targeting from 1990, with Canada, the United Kingdom and Sweden following in turn; Taylor (1993) then offered a testable interest rate rule against which to compare whether the Federal Reserve's actual operations were loose or tight. The section on the transmission mechanism (the credit channel) is likewise a product of this period: Bernanke, Gertler and others found that if only the interest rate channel is told, the actual effects of monetary policy are far larger than the models predict, so bank lending and the borrower's balance sheet were added into the chain. As for how fiscal and monetary policy are coordinated, Meade and Tinbergen in the 1950s supplied the “targets against instruments” arithmetic, and chapter 10 treats it formally. China's experience runs on a line of its own: in 1984 the People's Bank began to act solely as the central bank, in 1998 it abolished loan quantity ceilings and turned to indirect control, for a long stretch afterwards it took $M_2$ and aggregate financing to the real economy as intermediate targets, and after 2013 it created instruments such as the standing lending facility (SLF) and the medium-term lending facility (MLF), gradually transitioning towards price-based control — the end of this chapter returns to this line.

## Core concepts

### Inflation targeting

Inflation targeting is a framework for monetary policy decisions, a monetary policy regime in which the central bank takes inflation itself as the target and announces that target to the public.
Under inflation targeting the traditional monetary policy system changes substantially: no intermediate target is any longer set between the policy instruments and the final objective, and the basis for monetary policy decisions rests mainly on regular forecasts of inflation.
The government or the central bank fixes in advance, on the basis of forecasts, the medium- and long-term inflation target for the coming period, and the central bank, under public supervision, uses the corresponding monetary policy instruments to bring the actual value of inflation into line with the forecast target.

The reason this framework abolishes the intermediate target is that it has changed its anchor: it no longer promises to “control some monetary aggregate” but promises directly a result the public can check. The nominal anchor shifts from the quantity of money to inflation expectations, on the precondition that the central bank is credible — it can state a target, it can meet it, and it dares to admit when it cannot. Precisely for this reason, inflation targeting is more like a contract with the public than a technical parameter; its success and its failure both depend on whether central bank independence and fiscal discipline can hold this contract up.

### Time lags of monetary policy

The time lag of monetary policy is the time that elapses from the making of a policy to the obtaining of its main or its full effects. The time lag of monetary policy is a major factor affecting policy effectiveness.
The time lag of monetary policy consists of two parts:
- The inside lag
	- The recognition lag
	- The action lag
- The outside lag

The inside lag can be further divided into the recognition lag and the action lag; the outside lag is also called the impact lag.

The time lag is not a technical detail but a question of the legitimacy of policy. Friedman's (1961) objection was built precisely on this: the outside lag is both long and unstable, and by the time a policy takes effect the economy may long since have turned of its own accord, so “counter-cyclical operation” in practice often becomes a pro-cyclical disturbance, and the stabiliser turns into a source of disturbance. This argument is the single most important ground for the rule-based orientation after the 1970s (including the Taylor rule below).

### The Taylor rule

The Taylor rule is a method of operation that adjusts the interest rate according to the relative changes in output and inflation.
This rule embodies how the central bank's short-term interest rate instrument is adjusted according to the state of the economy.
Taylor held that, keeping the real short-term interest rate stable and the policy stance neutral, when the output gap is positive (negative) and the inflation gap exceeds (falls below) the target value, the real interest rate should be raised (lowered).

The motive for proposing the rule was quite concrete: using data for 1987–1993, Taylor (1993) recomputed the Federal Reserve's real interest rate and found that operations in the Greenspan era were looser than the rule indicated, and on that basis argued for constraining discretionary decisions with a rule. The formula was later used as a double instrument — at once a yardstick for judging whether policy is loose or tight and a proposal for an alternative policy. It also has a clear point of failure: when inflation is below target and the output gap is negative, the nominal interest rate the rule would require can fall below zero, which cannot be done in reality (the effective lower bound on nominal rates); that interest rates at the major central banks stayed below the Taylor rule's suggested values for a long time after 2008 is precisely where the zero lower bound and unconventional instruments made their appearance.

## Problems and analysis

### Why must monetary policy set intermediate indicators? What conditions should they satisfy?

In the conduct of monetary policy, a policy indicator must satisfy the following conditions:
- (1) The intermediate target of monetary policy must be controllable by the monetary authorities.
- (2) There must be a stable relationship between the policy indicator and the final objective.
- (3) Measurability
	- First, the central bank can obtain accurate data on the indicator in time
	- Second, it has a fairly clear definition, which makes analysis, observation and monitoring convenient
- (4) The policy indicator is less disturbed by external factors or non-policy factors.
- (5) It adapts well to the economic system and the financial system.

Generally, in free credit market transactions the monetary authorities can choose only one of the money supply and the interest rate as the policy indicator.
If the authorities choose the interest rate level as the policy indicator, then the money supply becomes an endogenous variable and will be determined by the relationships internal to the economic system;
if the money supply is chosen as the policy indicator, then the interest rate becomes the endogenous variable. Whether to choose the money supply or the interest rate as the policy indicator depends on the various sources of economic instability.
To maintain economic stability, the monetary authorities should choose the policy indicator with care.

The constraint of “only one of the two” is the key: a central bank cannot peg both the interest rate and the quantity of money at once, because it can control only the monetary base, while the interest rate and the quantity of money are determined by one and the same market. Which one to choose depends on which side the disturbances come from — if the shocks lie mainly in money demand (payment technology, asset substitution), pegging the interest rate is more stable; if the shocks lie in the demand for goods and investment, pegging the quantity of money is more stable (Poole's 1970 this-or-that analysis). That after the money demand function became unstable in the 1980s the various countries abandoned the monetary aggregate anchor is precisely the process of this judgement being overturned by reality (see “Limits and what follows” at the end of this chapter).

### Why do the expectations of micro agents offset the effects of monetary policy?

- (1) The expectations of micro agents are an important factor affecting the effectiveness of monetary policy, because those expectations have an offsetting effect on the effects of the monetary policy being implemented.
	This offsetting effect shows itself as follows: out of regard for their own economic interests, micro agents form reasonable expectations about monetary policy and, on the basis of those expectations, widely carry out countermeasures that favour their own economic interests, and these behaviours have an offsetting effect on monetary policy, so that the policy cannot deliver its intended effects.

- (2) Given the expectations of micro agents, it would seem that monetary policy can take effect or achieve its intended results only when its direction and strength are not, or not fully, known to the public.
	In reality, however, the public's expectations are not necessarily accurate every time, and turning expectations into actual action (renegotiating contracts, adjusting the timing of borrowing, rewriting wage clauses) also takes time. Monetary policy can therefore still work, but the public's expectational behaviour discounts the policy's effects.

- (3) A game-theoretic analysis of the offsetting effect.
	On the view of the rational expectations school, if the public holds rational expectations, then only monetary policy that the public has not anticipated can be effective. By the same token, if the government's expectations are rational, then only public behaviour the government has not anticipated will offset monetary policy. The process by which the government and the public set their actions according to expectations can therefore be explained by game theory: there exists a game between the government and the public.

The modern version of this game is the argument that “rules beat discretion”: if the central bank can renege at any time, the public will price that possibility into wage and price contracts, inflation expectations are raised and output gains nothing; conversely, if the central bank binds itself with institutions (independence, a clear objective, a price to pay for breaking its word), the public's expectations come down first and policy can hold down inflation at smaller cost. This line of thought later grew two institutional designs: legislatively confirmed central bank independence and the inflation targeting mentioned above — both trade “giving up flexibility” for “the public's trust”.

## Synthetic essay

### What is characteristic of each of the three instruments of monetary policy? Why is open market operations the one most used day to day?

- (1) The reserve requirement policy

	- ①The reserve requirement policy is a policy measure whereby the central bank, within the powers conferred on it by law, stipulates or adjusts the ratio of the deposits that commercial banks lodge with the central bank, so as to change the money multiplier and thereby control the commercial banks' capacity for credit creation and indirectly control the money supply of society.
	
	- ②The main contents of the reserve requirement policy are:
		- First, stipulating the ratio of reserve requirements.
		- Second, stipulating the kinds of assets that may serve as required reserves.
		- Third, stipulating the base on which reserve requirements are computed.
		- Fourth, stipulating the range within which the reserve requirement ratio may be adjusted, and so on.
	
	- ③The policy works as follows: when the central bank raises the required reserve ratio,
		- on the one hand it increases the required reserves that commercial banks must lodge with the central bank, reduces their excess reserves and lowers their capacity to make loans and create credit;
		- on the other hand, the rise in the required reserve ratio makes the money multiplier smaller, which lowers the capacity of the whole commercial banking system to create and expand credit; the result is a tightening of credit in society, a fall in the money supply, a rise in interest rates, and a corresponding contraction of investment and social spending. Run the other way, the process is reversed.
	
	- ④The reserve requirement policy exerts a strong influence on market interest rates, the money supply and public expectations, and this is at once its merit and its defect.

- (2) The rediscount policy

- ①The rediscount policy is a financial policy whereby the central bank fixes or adjusts the rediscount rate so as to intervene in and influence market interest rates and the supply and demand of the money market, and thereby regulate the money supply of the market.

- ②The rediscount policy has two sides:
	- One is adjusting the rediscount rate.
	- The other is stipulating which kinds of paper qualify to apply to the central bank for rediscounting.

- ③It works as follows: when the central bank judges it necessary to tighten credit and reduce the market money supply, it raises the rediscount rate above the market interest rate; this raises the cost to commercial banks of rediscounting with the central bank, so the banks borrow or discount less from the central bank and their reserves shrink. The commercial banks can then only contract their lending and investment to customers, which reduces the market money supply, tightens credit, raises market interest rates, and correspondingly reduces the demand for money. Run the other way, the process is reversed.

- ④The effects of using the rediscount policy:
	- One, it can affect the commercial banks' cost of funds and their excess reserves, and so affect their financing decisions and change their lending and investment behaviour;
	- Two, it can produce an announcement effect, and so influence the expectations of commercial banks and the general public;
	- Three, it can decide to whom the discount window is open, can influence the direction in which commercial banks employ their funds, and can also keep commercial banks from using the discount window for arbitrage.

- ⑤The defects of the rediscount policy:
	- One, the central bank is in a passive position when using this instrument to control the money supply;
	- Two, the rediscount rate can affect only the general level of interest rates, not the structure of rates;
	- Three, the rediscount policy lacks elasticity.

- (3) The open market policy

- ①The open market policy is a policy measure whereby the central bank openly buys and sells securities of various kinds on the financial market, so as to control the money supply and influence the level of market interest rates.

- ②It works as follows:
	- When funds are scarce on the financial market, the central bank buys securities through open market operations, which in effect injects a sum of base money.
	- If this base money flows into the hands of the public it directly increases the money supply of society; if it flows into the commercial banks it increases their excess reserves, and through the money multiplier the credit scale of the commercial banks expands and the money supply of society increases many times over. Conversely, when there is too much money on the financial market, the central bank can sell securities to reduce base money, reduce the money supply and tighten credit.

- ③Compared with the first two policies, the superiority of the open market policy is obvious: it is highly proactive, highly flexible, its effects on the economy are gentle and cause little shock, and its reach is wide.

How far apart the three instruments stand in actual frequency of use comes down to one set of trade-offs: impact and initiative do not come together. The moment the reserve requirement ratio moves, the whole banking system adjusts passively at once, with force enough to shake business expectations, so most countries treat it as a signalling instrument rather than a daily one (China is the exception: between 2004 and 2015 it adjusted frequently, precisely because the base money injected through purchases of foreign exchange was so large that a powerful sterilisation instrument was needed). The rediscount rate can only wait for banks to come to the window — the initiative lies with the borrower — and an adjustment affects the holders of already-discounted paper, so central banks usually leave it standing at the position of "the price of the lender of last resort". Open market operations can be done in hundreds of millions a day and be reversed the next, and a mistake can be squared away the following morning, which is why they became the daily instrument of the developed countries' central banks — the Federal Reserve turned from being a "passive seller" to an active instrument of control in the mid-1920s (building system-level open market operations in 1922, an investment committee in 1923, and after 1953 replacing the rediscount window entirely with market operations for daily control); this path explains the modern central bank's fondness for them.

### Beyond the interest rate channel, through which credit channels does monetary policy affect the real economy?

Why look for channels beyond the interest rate? Because the facts do not add up. Bernanke and Blinder (1988) found that after a contraction of the money supply, the fall in industrial output was far larger than the standard chain of “a rising interest rate suppresses investment” can explain; at the same time the fall in bank credit was distinctly faster than in other forms of financing. In other words, contraction does not merely make money dearer — it makes money scarcer and even unobtainable, and the hardest hit are precisely the borrowers who can rely on nothing but the banks. This gap gave rise to the theory of the credit channel, the counterpart of the money channel: asymmetric information makes “who cannot borrow” itself a consequence of policy. Research by Western scholars shows that two basic money transmission channels exist in the credit market, namely the bank lending channel and the balance sheet channel.

- (1) The bank lending channel

The mechanism by which monetary policy is transmitted through the bank lending channel rests on the recognition that banks play a special role in the financial system, because they are particularly well suited to handling certain types of borrowers, above all small firms, where the problem of asymmetric information is especially acute. Large firms, of course, can deal with the credit market directly through the stock and bond markets without going through banks. A contractionary monetary policy that reduces bank reserves and bank deposits will therefore have its effect through these borrowers. The money supply falls, which makes bank deposits (D) fall, which in turn makes the loans banks extend fall. The fall in bank loans (L) makes firms' investment fall, which makes aggregate output fall. This effect of monetary policy can be written as:

$$M ↓→ D ↓→ L ↓→ I ↓→Y ↓$$

- (2) The balance sheet channel

That the balance sheet transmits monetary policy through firms' net worth rests on the recognition that very low net worth means lenders in effect hold very little collateral for their loans, and likewise the losses from adverse selection grow. A fall in net worth gives rise to the adverse selection problem and so reduces the loans that serve as the source of funds for investment spending. Very low net worth also worsens the moral hazard problem, because it means the owners hold a lower equity stake in their firm's returns, which tempts them into risky projects. Because engaging in high-risk ventures makes it less likely that lenders get their money back, a fall in the firm's net worth leads to a fall in lending and hence in investment spending.

Monetary policy can affect firms' balance sheets in several ways. A contractionary policy ($M ↓$) drives securities prices down $P_e ↓$, which further lowers firms' net worth. With the problems of adverse selection and moral hazard multiplied, investment spending $(I ↓)$ and aggregate demand fall ($Y ↓$). This yields the following scheme of the balance sheet channel of monetary transmission:

$$M ↓→ P_e ↓→adverse selection↑and moral hazard↑→loans L ↓→ I ↓→Y ↓$$
A tightening that raises interest rates also worsens firms' balance sheets, because it cuts their cash flow. This gives the following additional scheme for the balance sheet channel:

$$M ↓→ r ↑→cash flow↓→moral hazard↑and adverse selection↑→loans L ↓→ I ↓→Y ↓$$
The credit channel does not act only on firms' spending; it applies equally to consumption spending. The fall in bank loans brought about by monetary contraction must bring a fall in consumers' purchases of durables and housing, for they have no other source of credit. Likewise, a rise in interest rates worsens the household's balance sheet, because its cash flow is affected in the opposite direction.

Another way of examining how the balance sheet works through consumers is the study of the liquidity effects on spending on consumer durables and housing. On the liquidity-effect view, the balance sheet effect works through its influence on consumers' desire to consume rather than on lenders' willingness to lend. In this model, if consumers expect that they are more likely to fall into financial distress, they will prefer to hold fewer illiquid assets such as consumer durables and housing, and more liquid financial assets. The underlying reason is that if consumers have to sell their durables and housing to raise money, they expect to suffer a large loss, because in a distress sale they will not obtain the full value. Financial assets such as stocks and bonds, by contrast, can more easily be turned into cash at their full market value.

This yields yet another transmission mechanism of monetary policy, one that links money to asset values. When falling stock prices lower the value of financial assets, consumption spending on durables and housing will also fall, because consumers judge such durables to be financially less safe and more exposed to financial loss. Written as a scheme:

$$M ↓→ P_e ↓→financial assets↓→likelihood of financial loss↑→durable goods spending and housing spending↓→Y ↓$$

That a rise in interest rates squeezes consumers' cash flow and so depresses spending on durables and housing — the illiquidity of these two classes of assets themselves supplies another explanation. The fall in consumers' cash flow increases the likelihood of financial loss, which lowers their desire to hold consumer durables and housing, and this reduces spending on these and with it aggregate output.

### Why must monetary policy and fiscal policy be coordinated? What patterns of coordination are there?

- (1) The basis for coordinating fiscal policy with monetary policy

Fiscal policy and monetary policy are the two great policies of the government's macroeconomic regulation, and both are policies of demand management. In state intervention in the economy, the focus of macroeconomic regulation is to push the market's aggregate supply and aggregate demand back towards equilibrium, so as to achieve the goals of growth, employment, stability and balance of payments equilibrium. To bring aggregate supply and aggregate demand from disequilibrium back to equilibrium, one may regulate aggregate supply, or regulate aggregate demand, or regulate both sides. For short-run equilibrium, the key is to regulate aggregate demand, which is easy to regulate in the short run, so that it reaches equilibrium with aggregate supply, which is relatively hard to regulate in the short run. Such is the meaning of demand management policy. Since the carrier of market demand is money, to regulate market demand is to regulate the money supply. In other words, the operation of demand management policy cannot be separated from the regulation of the money supply — either increasing it or reducing it. This holds for monetary policy, and it holds for fiscal policy too. Such is the basis on which the two should be, and can be, coordinated. As for their difference, from the angle of regulating the money supply it lies only in this: one works through the banking system and one through the fiscal system; one employs financial instruments and one employs fiscal and tax instruments; one is made effective by the financial transmission mechanism and one by the fiscal transmission mechanism.

There is, however, a question worth holding on to about this account of “both regulating the money supply”: is the expansion of fiscal policy really adding to demand, or is it merely moving purchasing power from the private sector to the government sector? If the newly issued government bonds push the interest rate up and private investment falls accordingly, the necessity of coordination has to be reappraised — this is exactly the old question of crowding out in chapter 11 of *Western Economics* (macroeconomic policy). This chapter follows the textbook in treating the two as “two sides of the same coin”; the reader should remember that this simplification is conditional.

- (2) The patterns of coordinating fiscal policy with monetary policy

- ①Before the 1930s, the dominant economic theories all advised against excessive government intervention in the economy. Apart from wartime, the governments of every country followed the fiscal principle of balanced budgets. In the monetary sphere, the gold standard that once existed was taken as the highest ideal. The Great Crisis of the 1930s and the “Keynesian revolution” produced macroeconomic intervention policy. Because the financial system was too weak to expand, fiscal policy was elevated to the first place among anti-crisis policies.

- ②Entering the 1940s, the long depression was for a time replaced by the prosperity brought by war. Insufficient effective demand was no longer the main contradiction; coping with huge budget deficits and severe inflation became instead the urgent demand. In this new situation the Keynesians went further and proposed “compensatory fiscal and monetary policy”, that is, alternately implementing contractionary and expansionary policies according to whether the economy ran hot or cold.

- ③Samuelson, a representative of the post-Keynesian school, stressed the efficacy of “mixing loose and tight” fiscal and monetary policy. Its meaning is to weigh the most pressing problem in the economy that needs attention and, according to the respective suitability of fiscal and monetary policy, to adopt either a loose fiscal policy with a tight monetary policy or a tight fiscal policy with a loose monetary policy. A particular economic situation may of course also call for “both loose” or “both tight”. Since the mid-1990s our country has, according to the changing economic situation, put forward several guidelines in turn for coordinating fiscal policy with monetary policy. When the economy is weak and depressed, to overcome insufficient demand through expansionary macroeconomic policy and to turn the economy warm, monetary policy is inferior to fiscal policy. In an economy that is too cold, commercial banks lack the incentive to expand credit business, firms lack the incentive to expand investment spending, and consumers equally lack the incentive to expand consumption spending. Not only need their behavioural intentions not coincide with those of the monetary authorities, but they do not take orders from them either. So even if the central bank carries out expansionary monetary policy, it is not easy to obtain expansionary results. Fiscal policy is different: expanded fiscal investment is a good opportunity for private firms to maintain or even expand their operations without bearing the investment risk themselves. Investment spending together with the consumption spending it drives, plus the consumption spending drawn out directly by increased fiscal social security and social welfare outlays, often plays the key role in turning round a slump in economic development.

- ④But when the economy is overheated, to suppress excessive demand through contractionary macroeconomic policy and thereby overcome inflation and false prosperity, fiscal policy is inferior to monetary policy. The central bank's role in restraining the growth of the money supply is strong and forceful:
	- First, it has many instruments at its disposal;
	- Second, it can exert a strong and forceful restraining effect on the commercial banks that create deposit money. Fiscal policy, by contrast, finds contraction quite difficult. For every country, fiscal outlays are extremely rigid: not only are they hard to cut in absolute terms, even compressing their growth rate is no easy matter. Adjustments to tax and spending policy must all pass through the legislative process, and questions such as raising taxes or cutting welfare spending are very hard to get approved.

At the same time, whether monetary policy or fiscal policy, using its active and favourable side is always accompanied by unavoidable negative side effects. Using them in coordination can weaken those side effects somewhat.

## Limits and what follows

- **The monetary aggregate as intermediate target was falsified by reality.** In October 1979 Volcker turned to targeting non-borrowed reserves and monetary aggregates, which did hold down inflation expectations in the short run, but financial innovation made the velocity of $M_1$ and $M_2$ and the money demand function visibly unstable in the mid-1980s, and after 1982 the Federal Reserve in effect abandoned aggregate targets. By the 1990s the major central banks generally no longer took some monetary aggregate as their anchor, replacing it with the policy rate plus expectation management (the first concept of this chapter is precisely the institutionalisation of this turn). Today the IMF and academia still debate whether inflation targeting is to blame for the post-pandemic high inflation: one school holds that it over-emphasised output stability and delayed the tightening of 2021, the other that the fault lay in the “transitory” judgement rather than in the framework itself.
- **The length of the transmission chain depends on the financial structure.** The credit channel works most visibly in bank-dominated economies — under Japan's “main bank” system and continental Europe's bank-based financing structure, contraction directly squeezes the loans available; whereas in the United States, after the 1980s brought the expansion of securitisation and the corporate bond market, large firms could bypass the banks and the bank lending channel weakened accordingly. After 2008 the problems of “banks reluctant to lend” and “policy transmission failure” were further exposed, so quantitative easing (QE), negative interest rates and forward guidance were taken as the means of forcing the chain back together when the conventional channels had snapped; their effects and side effects are still being assessed.
- **The tests of expectation offsetting and time inconsistency are not clean.** The Lucas critique (1976) declared it unreliable to evaluate policy with historical regression coefficients, and rational expectations further produced the strong proposition that “anticipated policy is ineffective”; yet in the actual data the short-run trade-off between inflation and output has always been present (because wage and price contracts are renegotiated in stages, which is exactly the new Keynesian explanation). The verdict experience delivers is a moderate one: expectations do weaken rather than annul the effectiveness of policy, and reputation does lower the cost of stabilising inflation — which is also why “central bank independence plus a clear objective” became the global mainstream configuration after the 1990s.
- **China's practice adds a whole set of further conditions to this chapter's framework.** After loan quantity control was abolished in 1998 the turn was to quantity-based regulation ($M_2$, the volume of credit); during 2003–2013, because of the continuous expansion of funds outstanding for foreign exchange, the reserve requirement ratio and central bank bills were used at high frequency for sterilisation; from 2013 instruments such as SLF, MLF and pledged supplementary lending (PSL) were created, the ceiling on deposit rates was lifted in 2015, and the 2019 loan prime rate (LPR) reform connected the policy rate to lending rates; in 2024 the central bank further explicitly narrowed the interest rate corridor (anchored on the 7-day reverse repo operation rate and extending 50 basis points to either side) and proposed gradually playing down quantity-based intermediate targets. In other words, the three-part scheme of this chapter — “instruments, intermediate indicators, final objectives” — is in China a template being rewritten rather than copied.
