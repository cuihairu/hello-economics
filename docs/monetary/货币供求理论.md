# Chapter 1. Money supply and demand theory

> **The core question of this chapter**: how is money defined and measured? How do commercial banks create money through deposits? Can the central bank control the money supply? How much output does an increase in the quantity of money buy?

## The arc of this chapter

The chapter's four questions look like accounting and ledger-keeping, but they correspond to an argument that has run for nearly two hundred years: is the quantity of money in circulation a quantity controlled by rules, or a quantity pulled out by the needs of the economy? The argument first took shape in England in the 1840s — before the Peel Bank Act of 1844, the Currency School held that banknote issue must contract and expand mechanically with the flows of gold, while the Banking School held that self-liquidating real bills needed no hard rule; the act as passed took a compromise, separating issue from banking, and the question "can the quantity of money be deliberately manipulated" has stayed in economics ever since. Fisher (*The Purchasing Power of Money*, 1911) first wrote it as a testable equation, $MV=PY$, treating velocity as a statistically measurable quantity; Marshall and Pigou at Cambridge (1917) asked instead "how large a cash balance are people willing to hold", and the same quantity relation became an act of asset holding — Keynes's early work (*Indian Currency and Finance*, 1913, *A Tract on Monetary Reform*, 1923) belongs to this tradition, and by the *General Theory* (1936) he had taken the motives for holding apart, so that money demand was no longer a stable ratio $k$. A third line came from the other side: Friedman (*A Restatement of the Quantity Theory of Money*, 1956) restated the quantity theory in the form of a demand function, and in *A Monetary History of the United States, 1867–1960* (1963, with Schwartz) argued from historical data that the money stock is the main cause of fluctuations; the claim was put to the test by Volcker in October 1979 and withdrawn from the facts when financial innovation destabilised the money demand function in the mid-1980s. Institutional change on the banking side shaped this chapter's answers just as much: Regulation Q in the United States from 1933 capped deposit rates, so banks set the scale of lending by the asset side, and regulatory-avoiding innovations (Eurodollars, money market funds) appeared in turn; deregulation began in 1980 and was complete by 1986; after 2008 the banking system held huge excess reserves, the textbook chain "reserves constrain deposit creation" stopped biting for a time, and the Bank of England (McLeay, Manning, Milionis and Zhu, 2014) revived "loans create deposits" on that ground. China's course forms a line of its own: in 1984 the People's Bank of China began acting solely as a central bank, and a layered money statistic followed — in 2001 securities firms' client margin was counted into $M_2$, in 2011 housing provident fund deposits and deposits of non-depository financial institutions were added, social financing was compiled from 2011 to supply the off-balance-sheet view, and in 2024 the bank announced the gradual de-emphasis of quantitative intermediate targets. So the chapter's four questions must be read in this order: first settle what "money" is statistically (core concepts), then settle who determines it (problems and analysis), and only then ask how much real effect it has (synthetic essay) — the third question is precisely the contemporary edition of the argument of 1844.

## Core concepts

### The layers of money

The layers of money refer to the strata into which each central bank divides money when fixing the statistical definition of the money supply, taking the liquidity of financial assets as the standard and acting according to its own policy purposes and needs. A rough classification:
- $M_{0}$: currency in circulation;
- $M_{1}$: $M_{0}$ + all demand deposits;
- $M_{2}$: $M_{1}$ + all time deposits.

The layering of money helps the central bank monitor macroeconomic performance and conduct monetary policy.

Note that these three lines are the greatest common divisor of national definitions, not any single country's actual one. China's current definitions are finer: $M_0$ is currency in circulation (excluding banks' vault cash), $M_1$ adds corporate demand deposits (personal demand deposits are not included — a point of difference from the US definition, and the reason the $M_1$–$M_2$ growth scissors in China are so often misread), and $M_2$ further adds corporate time deposits, personal deposits and deposits of non-depository financial institutions. The standard of division is not "good money versus bad money" but the cost of realisation: the smaller the price loss and the faster the speed with which an asset turns into purchasing power, the closer it stands to $M_0$. The layering must exist because policy needs an observable gauge — if only one number were reported, one could not distinguish "everyone moving money from time to demand" (a rise in willingness to pay, often ahead of an expansion of consumption and investment) from "the central bank has really issued more money", and the two mean completely different things for the economy. The "term-ification" of Japan in the 1990s and of China in the 2020s is read precisely off the $M_1/M_2$ structure, not the totals. At the same time, the boundary itself moves with innovation: bringing brokers' client margin into the measure in 2001 and provident fund deposits in 2011 were both ex-post recognitions that "assets not previously counted as money had come to behave like money"; money market funds (Yu'e Bao-type products) are by now nearly as liquid as demand deposits yet are still not counted in $M_2$ — the largest current dispute over the definition (see "Limits and what follows" at the end of this chapter).

### Base money

Base money, also called "high-powered money", is the sum of the currency issued by the central bank and commercial banks' reserve deposits at the central bank.
Base money appears directly as a liability of the central bank; it is created by the central bank's asset business, and it is the source of credit money.

The exact meaning of "source" must be read off the structure of the central bank's balance sheet:

| Assets | Liabilities |
| :----: | :----: |
| Loans to commercial banks (rediscounts, advances) | Currency (money in circulation) |
| Claims on government (treasury bond purchases, overdrafts to the treasury) | Reserve deposits of commercial banks |
| Foreign exchange and gold reserves | Treasury deposits |

A commercial bank has liabilities first (taking deposits) and asset deployment afterwards (making loans); the central bank is the other way round — assets determine liabilities. When it buys an asset (lending to a bank, buying foreign exchange, buying treasuries), the corresponding liability comes into being at the same moment; nobody deposits money with it first. This privilege arises from the monopoly of issue. The reason for standing "base money" up as a concept is to separate two things: which money the central bank creates directly (currency and reserves), and which money the banking system amplifies in between (deposits). The whole of Chapter 7's monetary policy operations work on the asset side of this table in essence — change what and how much the central bank buys, and base money on the liability side moves with it. China's history shows the point well: in the 1980s base money was injected through loans to banks (advances), in 2001–2014 through purchases of foreign exchange (FX purchases were for a time the largest channel), and after 2014, with FX purchases no longer growing, through the medium-term lending facility and reverse repos; the same table changed its main account three times in thirty years, and the operating philosophy of monetary policy changed with it.

### The money multiplier

Society's money takes three forms: currency $C$, issued and managed by the central bank; the reserves of commercial banks at the central bank $R$, comprising required reserves $RR$ set by the central bank and excess reserves $ER$ chosen by the banks themselves; and deposit money $D$. Hence

$$C+R=\text{Base money }(B)$$

$$C+D=\text{Money supply }(M)$$

$$M=mB,\quad m=\frac{M}{B}=\frac{C+D}{C+R}$$

Under the simplest assumptions (banks lend out everything beyond required reserves and the public leaks nothing into currency), with the central bank charging required reserves at a fixed proportion of deposits ($RR_r$), a primary deposit $R$ supports a total of deposits of

$$D=\frac{R}{RR_r}$$

A single round of expansion may be written $D=L+R$ (deposits equal loans plus reserves).

The true multiplier is smaller than this reciprocal formula, because there are three leaks in the denominator: the public holding currency (the currency–deposit ratio $c$), banks voluntarily holding excess reserves (the excess reserve ratio $e$), and the required reserve ratio itself. Writing all three in, one has $m=\dfrac{1+c}{c+e+rr}$. The reason the simple formula $1/rr$ must not be taken as reality: every term in its denominator is a behavioural variable, not a technical parameter of the central bank. $c$ depends on payment habits and interest rates, $e$ on the banks' judgement of liquidity risk and on whether the central bank pays interest on reserves; only $rr$ is set by law. This explains two large drifts of the multiplier in practice: in China the ratio of $M_2$ to base money was about 4 in the mid-2000s, then rose above 6 as the required reserve ratio was cut repeatedly and the use of cash declined; in the United States the multiplier was about 9 before 2008, but quantitative easing swelled reserves while deposits did not expand in proportion (banks reluctant to lend, the central bank paying interest on excess reserves), and the multiplier at one point fell to around 1 — the same concept of "multiplier" moving in opposite directions in two countries in the same decade, which shows it is a summary of behaviour, not a knob the central bank controls.

### Liquidity preference

In analysing the factors governing money demand, Keynes held that money demand is determined chiefly by the psychology of individuals in disposing of their income.
There are two forms of disposal, consumption and saving. Whether the saving part is held as cash money or as securities depends on the degree of people's preference for the liquidity of financial assets — liquidity preference.
Keynes held that the factors governing the degree of liquidity preference, and so money demand, are chiefly:
- The transactions motive
- The precautionary motive
- The speculative motive

"Preference" is easily read as irrational psychology, but it names a priceable service: money is the only asset that can be deployed without the cost of realisation, and holding it amounts to buying insurance for "being able to change one's mind at any time", with interest as the premium on that insurance. Seen as the purchase of a service, money demand becomes an optimisation problem, and the later advances all consist of adding constraints to that problem: Baumol (1952) and Tobin (1956) wrote the transactions motive as the choice of the number of trips to the bank, obtaining money demand proportional to the square root of income and inversely proportional to the square root of the interest rate (the square-root law), which showed that even transactions demand is rate-sensitive; Whalen (1966) proved that precautionary demand depends on the variance of unexpected outlays and the cost of holding; and Tobin (1958) replaced the corner solution of "all money or all bonds" with the portfolio choice of a risk-averse investor — the liquidity preference theory of Chapter 2 and the portfolio theory of Chapter 10 rest on the same microfoundation.

### Credit money

Credit money is money issued on credit and no longer convertible into metal, comprising currency and deposit money. Its value comes not from its material but from two supports: the credit and legal-tender standing of the issuer (the central bank and the treasury), and the capacity of the whole banking system to convert deposits into currency and settle by transfer. The reason the word "credit" must be made explicit: in the age of metallic money the quantity of money was decided by mines and trade surpluses, while in the age of credit money it is decided by the balance-sheet decisions of banks — every question in the latter half of this chapter (endogenous or exogenous, the multiplier, output effects) holds only under credit money. This is also the weight of the sentence that fiat money is issued as a monopoly by each country's central bank or monetary authority, with public confidence in it resting on the credit of the state — after 15 August 1971 no country in the world retained any obligation to redeem in gold, and confidence became the only anchor.

## Problems and analysis

### Can the central bank control the money supply like a tap?

The debate over the endogeneity and exogeneity of the money supply is in effect a discussion of whether the central bank can control the money supply entirely on its own.
- (1) Endogeneity means that the money supply is determined within an economic system by many factors and actors together.
	The central bank is only one of them, and therefore cannot determine the money supply alone. The intensity of micro agents' demand for currency, the state of the business cycle, commercial banks, the treasury and the balance of payments all influence the money supply.

- (2) Exogeneity means that the money supply is controlled independently by the central bank from outside the economic system.
	The reason is that, essentially, the modern monetary system is one of pure credit money: the central bank's asset deployment determines the scale of its liabilities and hence the quantity of base money, and provided the central bank is institutionally independent enough to be free of political interference, it controls the quantity of money at the source.

In fact, whether one looks at the basic models of the modern money supply or at the development of the theory, the money supply is to a considerable degree "endogenous", while the "exogeneity" theory rests on assumptions that are too strict.

This judgement needs three supplements, or it is easily read as the over-strong conclusion that "the central bank's word does not count". First, the true fulcrum of the exogeneity argument is not that the central bank can dictate the quantity of deposits but that it can dictate the **price** of reserves — a bank willing to pay a high enough price for reserves can always get them, and so the short-term rate is pinned; quantity is the outcome of that price constraint. Second, endogeneity gained a new argument after 2008: with the banking system holding massive excess reserves, the binding reserve constraint lapsed, and the Bank of England (McLeay et al., 2014) on that ground restated "loans create deposits" rather than deposits deriving loans; yet in the same period, 2020–2021, the US money stock surged because of direct fiscal transfers and was followed by inflation above 9% in 2022 — so the exogeneity proposition is very far from refuted. Third, China's experience supplies a mixed sample: during the expansion of FX purchases (2003–2013) whether the central bank sterilised actively determined $M_2$, after 2014 the channel of injection became endogenous (resting on banks' willingness to borrow from policy instruments), and after 2018 there were phases of abundant reserves but weak credit demand. The conclusion: the central bank controls conditions and costs, not the quantity itself; and this distinction is the precondition for understanding why Chapter 7's intermediate targets shifted from quantity to price.

### The same $MV=PY$: what did Fisher and Cambridge each see in it?

- (1) Definitions:
	- 1. Fisher's equation is:
		 $$MV=PY$$
		 , where $M$  is the quantity of money, $V$  the velocity of money, $P$  the price level, and $Y$  output.
		Rearranged:
		$$M=\frac{1}{\bar{V}}\cdot{P\bar{Y}}$$

		Fisher's equation says that the level of transactions generated by a given level of nominal income determines people's demand for money.

	- 2. The Cambridge equation is: $M_{d} = kPY$, where PY is nominal income, which can be held in many asset forms, money being one of them; money demand is thus the fraction k of nominal income, and the size of k depends on the opportunity cost of holding money — that is, on the expected returns of other financial assets.

- (2) What the two equations share
	- (i) The form is the same, k and V being reciprocals;
	- (ii) The conclusion is the same: both say the quantity of money determines the price level.

- (3) Where the two equations differ
	(i) The emphasis differs: Fisher's equation starts from money's function as a medium of exchange, while the Cambridge equation starts from money's function as a store of value and analyses it from the angle of individual asset choice.
	(ii) Fisher's equation ties money demand to the flow of expenditure, stressing the quantity and speed of monetary spending, while the Cambridge equation considers money demand from the angle of holding a stock of assets in monetary form, stressing the proportion of that stock to income. Hence Fisher's equation is called the cash transactions approach and the Cambridge equation the cash balances approach.
	(iii) The determinants of money demand emphasised differ. Fisher's equation proceeds from a macro angle, taking money demand as a function of income alone with no role for the interest rate; the Cambridge equation proceeds from a micro angle, treating money demand as the outcome of asset choice, which implies that the interest rate affects money demand — a view that profoundly influenced later research on money demand.

One further qualification: to say the two "share the conclusion that the quantity of money determines the price level" overstates the Cambridge equation. $M_d=kPY$ is only a demand condition; to reach "the quantity of money determines prices" one must add two assumptions — that the money supply is exogenously given, and that $k$ (or $V$) is stable. Fisher himself accepted both (that is what the bars over his $\bar V$ and $\bar Y$ mean); the Cambridge school wrote $k$ as a behavioural variable, which opened exactly the door through which "$k$ moves with interest rates and expectations" could pass — and Keynes walked through that door into the *General Theory*. In other words, the formal equivalence $k=1/V$ conceals the most consequential substantive disagreement: is the quantity theory an identity (in which case it says nothing) or an empirical proposition about behavioural stability (in which case the data can refute it)? What was refuted after velocity destabilised in the 1980s was precisely the latter.

### Why did Friedman advocate the "single rule"? Its theoretical basis and its territory

- (1) The single rule is the monetarist policy proposal that the money supply be guaranteed a long-run stable growth rate.
	Friedman held that the best choice for controlling the money supply is publicly to announce and long maintain a fixed rate of growth of the money supply, broadly matched to the rate of economic growth; and once the rate is fixed and announced, it should not be changed at will within the year or the quarter.

- (2) The theoretical basis of the monetarist "single rule"
	Following the method of demand analysis in microeconomics, taking income and the various opportunity costs of holding money as the main considerations, Friedman constructed a descriptive money demand function, which can be simplified to:

	$$\frac{M_{d}}{P} = f(Y_{p},(r_{b} - r_{m}),(r_{e} - r_{m}),({\pi}^{e}-r_{m}))$$

	where
	$\frac{M_{d}}{P}$ is the demand for real money balances,
	$Y_{p}$  is permanent income, and $r_{m}$ , $r_{b}$ , $r_{e}$  are the expected returns on money, bonds and equities respectively,
	${\pi}^{e}$  is the expected rate of inflation.

	From this money demand function Friedman concluded:

	(i) The influence of the interest rate on money demand is small, because when the rate changes, $r_{m}$  moves together with $r_{b}$ , $r_{e}$  and ${\pi}^{e}$ , the movements offsetting one another — that is, the opportunity-cost terms in the money demand function remain relatively stable.

	(ii) Since the interest rate matters little for money demand, the velocity of money is stable and predictable. Empirically it keeps a "positive consistency" with the level of income.

	(iii) Permanent income is the determinant of money demand. Permanent income means the average income expected over future years; it is a relatively stable variable, largely immune to short-run economic factors. Friedman therefore held the money demand function to be relatively stable as well, and on that ground arrived at the celebrated policy proposal of a "rule-governed money supply".

The logic of the three arguments is serial: only if money demand is stable is velocity predictable; only if velocity is predictable does controlling the quantity of money equal controlling nominal income; and only if that equality holds is a rule preferable to discretion. The fate of the proposal was therefore staked entirely on empirical test, and the test was not dignified: in October 1979 Volcker announced monetary aggregates as the operating target, between 1980 and 1982 the velocity of $M_1$ lurched violently, financial innovation (automatic transfer services, money market funds diverting deposits) broke the link between $M_1$ and nominal income, and after 1982 the Federal Reserve stopped enforcing aggregate targets strictly. Friedman's own position was scaled back accordingly — he later insisted that with a stable quasi-aggregate (an "M3B"-type definition including debt instruments, as he proposed) a rule would still work, but no central bank followed. What actually survived was the argumentative structure behind the rule, not its content: discretion depreciates itself through time inconsistency, and a credible commitment is worth more than flexibility — that thought changed vehicles and landed on the inflation targeting and the Taylor rule of Chapter 7.

## Synthetic essay

### How is money in the modern economy created and amplified many times over?

In the modern economy credit money is supplied and extended through the banking system with the central bank at its core, by way of credit expansion. The process of the supply and extension of credit money can therefore be revealed by a positive analysis of the banking system's credit mechanism. It shows itself in two connected processes.

- 1. The central bank's credit creation and the supply of base money

Since the central bank is a country's sole issuing institution, the increment of money in economic activity is issued in the first place by the central bank according to the needs of growth. The basic process is: when the actors in the economy need more money, demand for credit funds rises; when commercial banks' credit cannot meet that demand, the banks pledge to the central bank the assets their clients have [discounted](/glossary#贴现) or posted as collateral — bills of exchange, collateral certificates, foreign exchange — to obtain loans; and when the treasury runs a deficit it overdrafts from the central bank, or the central bank buys government bonds in the secondary market. The central bank's assets thus increase, the banks' deposits with it grow, and the banks can lend more on that basis while also adding to their reserve deposits at the central bank; in the end the total reserves of the banking system equal in amount the commercial banks' deposits with the central bank. Moreover, banks in operation must meet clients' withdrawals of currency, so their reserves at the central bank divide further into reserve deposits and currency in circulation, the sum of the two being base money.

The process of central bank issue can be put simply: the central bank's assets increase, and with them its liability — the supply of base money.

- 2. The commercial banks' credit expansion and the creation of derived deposits

While the central bank expands base money, the commercial banks expand their credit and create an increment of deposit money. The process may be summed up as: the banking system makes loans out of the primary deposits it has received, and through the alternating of deposit and loan within the system, creates derived deposits many times over.

In these two processes the banks' primary deposits originate in the central bank's new issue of base money, and the growth of base money is the basis on which the banks multiply credit money. When the banks' primary deposits exactly equal their reserve deposits at the central bank, their capacity for credit expansion is at its maximum. The process can be written:

$$M_{s}=KB$$

where $B$ is base money and $K$ the money multiplier expressing the multiple by which banks expand the quantity of money (in the rest of this section and in Chapter 7 the multiplier is also written $m$; the two are the same).

One point of this two-stage picture deserves testing on the spot: the textbook narrates "central bank injection first, bank derivation afterwards", while the accounting fact in banks runs the other way — when a commercial bank makes a loan it simultaneously writes a new deposit under the borrower's name; the asset (the loan) and the liability (the deposit) come into being in equal amounts, with no need to hold loanable funds beforehand. Reserves are needed only when a client withdraws currency, when settlement runs across banks, or when the legal requirement must be met. The two accounts do not contradict each other; they answer questions at different levels: at the micro level every deposit is indeed created by a loan, and at the macro level the expansion of the whole system is bounded by the quantity and the price of reserves (the interest rate) — the constraint comes from the central bank, but the trigger comes from demand. China's practice before 1998 pushed this constraint to an extreme: with a system of reserves fully remitted plus credit quotas, the multiplier was almost inert and banks "lent as much quota as they had"; only after 1998, with the shift to a two-tier reserve system and the abolition of quotas, did the multiplier really begin to work. Today's discussion of the phenomenon "banks have money but do not lend" turns on the same mechanism: the form of the constraint has changed, but every link between central bank injection and real-economy credit requires someone willing to sign and borrow.

### How did the theory of money demand evolve, step by step?

- (1) Along the line of the motives for holding money and the determinants of money demand, Western theories of money demand passed through the traditional quantity theory, the Keynesian theory of money demand, and the monetarist theory.

- (2) The early Western theory of money demand was the traditional quantity theory, chiefly studying the effect of the quantity of money on the price level and the value of money. The representative theories are:
	- Fisher's cash transactions approach
	- The Cambridge school's cash balances approach

	The two theories are basically the same in conclusion and in the form of the equation, both holding that the quantity of money determines the price level. But their starting points differ. The defect of the traditional quantity theory is that it did not analyse the effect of the quantity of money on output, nor did it examine in detail the factors of utility and cost affecting money holding.

- (3) Keynes's liquidity preference theory of money demand

	Following the line of cash-balance analysis, and likewise beginning from money as an asset, it points out that money demand is the quantity of money people are able and willing to hold over a given period, and that its size is governed by the psychology of liquidity preference. That preference springs from three motives:
	- the transactions motive
	- the precautionary motive
	- the speculative motive

	These three motives may be gathered into $L_1$ (transactions and precautionary) and $L_2$ (speculative); $L_1$ depends on income, rising with it, and $L_2$ depends on the market rate of interest, falling as it rises: $M=L_{1}(Y)+L_{2}(r)$. Because speculative demand is highly unstable in the short run, the market rate of interest fluctuates, which affects investment and through it output and growth. Keynes's theory repaired the traditional quantity theory's limitation that the quantity of money affects neither velocity nor output, but by over-stressing demand management it neglected the effect of changes in the money supply on prices. Moreover Keynes's theory is short-run: income and interest rates in it are short-run variables, and on that basis it concludes that money demand is unstable.

- (4) Developments of Keynes's theory of money demand.
	- (i) Baumol's correction of transactions demand, analysing deeply the relation between transactions demand and the interest rate (the square-root law: $M$ varies in proportion to $\sqrt{Y}$ and to $1/\sqrt{r}$).
	- (ii) Tobin's correction of speculative demand. Tobin discussed the optimal portfolio of a risk-averse investor under uncertain interest-rate expectations, and the effect of people's portfolio adjustments on speculative demand for money.
	- (iii) The neo-Cambridge school's development of Keynes's motive theory, expanding his three motives into seven, making them concrete, and adding the public-power motive — the expansionary money demand generated by the government's expansionary demand policy.

- (5) The monetarist modern quantity theory.
	The monetarist school led by Friedman took over Keynes's idea of money as an asset and the same method of analysing motives and determinants, and used it to rewrite the traditional quantity theory as a money demand function; at the same time it broadly affirmed the quantity theory's conclusion that changes in the quantity of money affect the price level. But it turned Keynes's short-run analysis into long-run analysis, proposed the variable of permanent income in place of Keynes's short-run income, held permanent income to be stable, and showed empirically that money demand is interest-inelastic. Its key conclusion follows: because the factors governing money demand are stable variables, money demand is a stable function. Given stable money demand, the central bank can set growth targets for the money supply objectively and accurately and conduct a stable monetary policy. Against Keynes's demand management, Friedman put the emphasis of management on the money supply, reconciling supply with demand by controlling the quantity of money so as to secure stable development and control inflation.

To read this lineage as "steady improvement" is to lose what is really interesting in it: each step did not patch a predecessor's hole but changed the object being interrogated. Fisher explained how much medium trade needs; Pigou and Keynes explained why a person is willing to leave money idle in hand; Tobin and Baumol wrote that willingness as a cost-bearing optimisation; Friedman then asked "is this willingness stable enough over the long run to serve as a policy anchor?" The fourth question still has no accepted answer — the instability of the 1980s showed that "stable" is conditional, and the experience of developed countries since the 2010s, that "the money demand function looks stable again, only stable in the opposite place from before" (at low rates the stock being highly insensitive to rates), shows it is still moving. This is why Chapter 2 on interest rate theory must be read together with this chapter: if money demand is unstable, interest rates are unstable; and if interest rates are unstable, the meaning of the quantity of money must be revalued.

### What is the relation between monetary equilibrium and market equilibrium?

Monetary equilibrium is the state in which money supply and money demand are broadly equal; market equilibrium is the state in which supply and demand in the goods market are equal.

- (1) The relation between monetary and market equilibrium shows itself as:
	- (i) Aggregate supply determines money demand, but the same aggregate supply can carry money demand that is too large or too small.
	- (ii) Money demand calls forth money supply, but again not in equal amount.
	- (iii) Money supply becomes the bearer of aggregate demand; likewise the same money supply can carry aggregate demand that is too large or too small.
	- (iv) Aggregate demand too large or too small exerts a great influence on aggregate supply:
		- with aggregate demand insufficient, aggregate supply cannot be fully realised;
		- with aggregate demand excessive, aggregate supply may under certain conditions be pushed up, but the gap is not necessarily thereby closed.
	- (v) Aggregate demand too large or too small can also be regulated by contractionary or expansionary policy, but controlling demand alone can hardly guarantee the goal of equilibrium.

- (2) If $M_{s}$ , $M_d$ , AS and AD stand for the supply and demand of money and the supply and demand of the market, their relation can be drawn as below. The actions all run both ways; the arrows mark only the dominant direction.

	![](../img/exercises/currency/1_1.png#pic_center)

	The figure shows that monetary equilibrium and market equilibrium are tightly linked, and monetary equilibrium helps market equilibrium to be achieved. But the two differ plainly: monetary equilibrium does not necessarily imply market equilibrium. The reasons are:

- (i) Market demand is borne by money, but not all of the money supply constitutes market demand.
	Money that satisfies the transactions demand and acts as a medium of circulation (including preparation for it) — money actually circulating — forms market demand; money that is actually not circulating, held as a store of value, does not constitute market demand, or is potential rather than current demand. The difference can be written:

	$$\text{Money supply}=\text{money actually circulating}+\text{money actually not circulating}$$

	$$\text{Market demand}＝\text{money actually circulating}\times\text{velocity of money}$$

- (ii) Market supply requires money to realise it, to clear it, and so generates a demand for money. But that demand for money is not the whole of money demand either. The means of storing wealth accumulated is not determined solely by market supply, or at least not solely by the current money supply — the money held as wealth is evidently in large part the accumulation of many years. The difference can be written:

	$$\text{Market supply}\div\text{velocity of money}=\text{demand for money actually circulating}$$

	$$\text{Money demand}＝\text{demand for money actually circulating}+\text{demand for money actually not circulating}$$

Put simply, the equilibrium of total market supply and demand corresponds one to one with the equilibrium of supply and demand for money in the state of actual circulation. But note also that money actually circulating and money actually not circulating can be, and in fact constantly are, converted into each other.

This passage is the most practically useful block in the chapter, because it gives the exact mechanism by which "aggregate data can lie". The distinction between circulating and non-circulating money is, in the contemporary Chinese context, "abundant money does not mean abundant credit": $M_2$ growth is not low and the banking system is flush with funds, yet firms and households will not borrow to spend; money idles in deposits and bills, and market equilibrium does not improve. Japan in the 1990s and China in 2015–2016 and 2023–2024 can both be read here — the long divergence between $M_2$ and the GDP gap and social financing growth, and the reason the central bank repeatedly reaches for phrases like "putting the existing stock to work" and "preventing funds idling in the system". In the other direction, China in 1988 and 1993 is an example: the moment money surged out of the "non-circulating" state, market equilibrium was broken by excessive aggregate demand. Watching money therefore means not only checking whether the total $M_s$ equals $M_d$ but seeing in which compartment it sits — which is the reason the layered money statistics of the first section exist, and the reason Chapter 3 looks at the structure of money before judging inflation.

### How much output does extra money buy? Output effects and the limits of expansion

In the argument over whether the quantity of money can push real output, the method now widely accepted analyses the state of potential and usable resources:

- (1) So long as the economic system contains resources actually available for expanded reproduction, and in fairly ample quantity, then increasing the money supply over a period can raise the level of real output without pushing up the general price level.

- (2) After the potential resources have been worked for a period and the money supply keeps growing, the economy may show both real output and the price level rising together.

- (3) When the potential resources are fully employed and the money supply keeps expanding, the system will show the general price level rising while real output stands still.

These three stages can be drawn as figure 1-2.

![](../img/exercises/currency/1_2.png#pic_center)

In figure 1-2 the horizontal axis measures the rate of output growth and the vertical axis the rate of price increase; the two turning points A and B mark the boundaries between the stages of different combinations of output growth and price inflation generated by monetary expansion.
- A is the critical point at which growth of the money supply has only real output effects and no price effect;
- B is the end of the stage in which money growth has both output and price effects, and the start of the stage in which it has only price effects and no output effect.
- Point B is in fact the limit of output growth over a period. Reaching it means the economy's actually usable resources are fully employed; not reaching it can be stated as a gap between actual and potential GNP. As a rule, the existence of a gap between actual and potential GNP describes the growth state of most countries. In other words, in real life the actual growth rate rarely reaches the degree at which all potential resources are employed, so some loss of potential GNP always exists. One important reason is that once monetary expansion passes point A, output growth is accompanied by rising prices, and the public's psychological tolerance of price increases and like factors weigh heavily on the choice of policy.

The skeleton of this diagram is in fact the three-segment aggregate supply curve (the Keynesian, intermediate and classical ranges of the AD-AS chapter of the *Western Economics* macro part); the difference is that here the axes are read as the output growth rate and the inflation rate, so it is naturally a dynamic diagram: the positions of the turning points are set by potential growth and resource constraints, while movement along the curve is set by the size of monetary expansion. Three limits deserve mention. First, the position of A is not fixed: if expansion is accompanied by supply-side reform (the tax cuts urged by the supply-siders in the 1970s, China's infrastructure and housing commodification after 1998, the export capacity released after WTO accession), potential output itself rises and the same monetary expansion can stay in the first segment longer — which is why "is money neutral?" cannot be settled with one cut empirically. Second, the public's tolerance of price increases is a real policy constraint, not just rhetoric: once inflation expectations lift, wages and contracts adjust pre-emptively and point B shifts left (the inflation inertia and expectations-augmented Phillips curve of Chapter 3). Third, a gap is not waste, because the tool that fills it is not the same instrument — where the gap arises from technological, institutional or allocative mismatch, expanding money can only raise prices rather than output, a distinction invoked repeatedly in the Chinese discussion of the "diminishing marginal effect of stimulus".

## Limits and what follows

- **The stability of the money demand function is the fulcrum of every policy conclusion in this chapter, and it collapsed in the critical decades.** Friedman's evidence came mainly from long-run US data for 1867–1960; in the early 1980s financial innovation destabilised the velocity of $M_1$ and $M_2$ abruptly, and countries abandoned the aggregate anchor one after another (see Chapter 7). The more fundamental methodological criticism came from Lucas (1976): the parameters of a demand function embed the public's past expectations about policy; once the policy rule changes, extrapolating from old parameters fails — the "Lucas critique" was no philosophical game, and after 1982 forecasting money demand from historical regressions did fail. The field then turned to more micro foundations (cash-in-advance and money search theory, New Keynesian models with payment constraints), but no new stable relation fit for operations emerged to replace the old function.
- **"$M_2$/GDP too high" and "$M_2$ no longer matters" are two faces of the same coin.** China's ratio of $M_2$ to GDP has long run markedly above the developed economies; the three common explanations each grasp one side: a bank-centred financing structure (with the same saving and investment, intermediated finance enlarges the money stock while direct finance does not), a high saving rate with thin social insurance, and the monetisation during financial deepening of assets previously outside the statistics (housing commodification and land sales in the 1990s both showed up as deposit growth). After 2017, as $M_2$ growth kept stepping down while nominal GDP did not slow in proportion, the central bank and researchers began to discuss openly the information content of the indicator; in 2024 the People's Bank of China announced the gradual de-emphasis of quantitative intermediate targets in favour of an anchor in the short-term policy rate. This is at once the institutional confirmation of this chapter's "endogeneity" judgement and the theme of Chapter 7.
- **An unexpected re-test in 2020–2022: the exogeneity proposition was not refuted.** US $M_2$ grew by about 25% between April 2020 and February 2021 (direct fiscal transfers to households, turned into deposits by the banking system with almost no friction), after which CPI inflation reached 9.1% year on year in June 2022, and from 2023 the money stock turned negative with the balance-sheet run-off. This sequence is awkward for pure endogeneity: when the injection of money is decided directly by fiscal and legal procedure, its relation to subsequent prices is almost an arithmetical replay of $MV=PY$. The contemporary consensus is therefore closer to a conditional judgement: in periods when banks are willing to lend and households willing to borrow, money is rather endogenous; in periods of fiscal dominance or direct transfers, money is rather exogenous — and the answer to the two-school dispute in this chapter's first section is "it depends on which door the money came in through".
- **Payment technology is rewriting this chapter's layers and multiplier.** The share of cash use has fallen very low in countries like Sweden, and mobile payment in China has made withdrawal almost never happen, so the term $c$ (currency leakage) keeps shrinking and the same base money supports a larger expansion of deposits — which explains the rise of China's multiplier after 2015. Money market funds (Yu'e Bao-type products at one point in 2018 approaching RMB 9 trillion) are nearly as liquid as demand deposits yet are not counted in $M_2$, so the indicator value of $M_1$ has visibly decayed. The digital yuan (e-CNY, piloted from the end of 2019 and widened after 2022), a direct central bank liability replacing $M_0$, is issued on a "central bank–operator" two-tier basis and pays no interest to holders; its design point is precisely to leave the reserve constraint untouched while making "non-circulating money" more traceable. Stablecoins (USDT and USDC with a combined market value at one point above USD 170 billion) pose another class of question: private liabilities issued by no central bank that yet perform part of the unit-of-account and settlement functions — do they count as "money"? This is in fact the same question as the argument of 1844 at the head of this chapter, only with the protagonist changed to a bookkeeping system that does without banks. The dispute over definitions will not end; it will simply be reopened at the next innovation.
