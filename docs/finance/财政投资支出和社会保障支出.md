# Chapter 3 · Fiscal investment spending and social security spending

> **The central question of this chapter**: a firm's investment is judged by its rate of return — on what is the government's investment judged? When fiscal money is short but the public projects must still go ahead, how does the government use "non-fiscal money" to do fiscal work? The risks of old age, illness and unemployment: why does market insurance under-provide them, so that the state has to step in?

## The arc of this chapter

This chapter takes the two blocks of fiscal expenditure that "directly change the fate of ordinary people" — investment and security — and the theoretical starting point of each is a comparison with the market. The investment question: private investment aims at profit, government investment at social returns; the objective function differs, so the standard for choosing projects differs — this chapter sets out three competing standards (saving capital, deepening capital, creating employment), and "which one the government should use right now" is essentially a question of policy goals, not a technical one. The second question is money: capital in the fiscal budget is limited, while infrastructure investment is large and long-cycled, and so there appeared financing methods sitting between the fiscal and the commercial — fiscal investment and financing, resting on national credit and used on a reimbursable basis (the prototype is Japan's systematised "Fiscal Investment and Loan Programme" of 1951; China's counterpart is the policy banks set up in 1994, the China Development Bank above all), and the BOT / concession approach, which hands construction and operation to private capital and takes the asset back at the end of the term (an invention of Britain's privatisation era in the 1980s; in China it started in the late 1980s with the Shenzhen Shajiao B power station, grew into the PPP wave after 2014, and contracted again in 2023 to focus on user-fee concessions). The answer to the security question is more direct: the social security system is not an extension of charity but a product of industrialisation — industrialisation stripped the risks of birth, age, sickness and death out of the hands of the family, the guild and the parish and turned them into social risks; the first systematic answers were the Bismarck government's Sickness Insurance Act (1883) and Old Age and Invalidity Insurance Act (1889), followed by Britain's old-age pension of 1908 and the National Insurance Act of 1911, and the Beveridge Report of 1942 wrote "the five giant evils on the road to the abolition of poverty" into the blueprint of the post-war welfare state. The theoretical ground economics supplies, and this chapter uses repeatedly, is: insurance markets fail under information asymmetry (adverse selection and moral hazard), and individual intertemporal decisions are systematically myopic, so compulsory, socialised insurance carries — beyond efficiency — a layer of paternalist justification. China's own social security story runs from the Labour Insurance Regulations of 1951 (work-unit security) to the socialisation reform of the 1990s; within it, old-age insurance established in 1997 the partially funded model of "combining social pooling with individual accounts", and the empty accounts, transition costs and national pooling that followed are the main thread this chapter's "Limitations and further work" has to explain.

## Core concepts

### The job-creation standard

The job-creation standard says the government should choose the projects that mobilise the largest amount of labour per unit of investment. It requires the government not only to expand the scale of fiscal investment extensively, to add employment opportunities, but also to give intensive priority to projects using labour-intensive technologies; and to consider not only the direct employment effect of the investment but also the indirect employment effect produced by the other investment the project draws along. The standard puts labour, as a factor of production, at the centre, and makes adding employment and reducing unemployment the goal of a fiscal investment project.

Only by setting it against the chapter's other two standards (minimising the capital–output ratio, maximising the capital–labour ratio) can its position be seen: both of those treat capital as the scarce factor, while the employment standard treats labour as the scarce factor — in a developing country with surplus labour and scarce capital, the two views produce almost opposite project lists. Its theoretical background carries a further weight of macro policy: unemployment is not only an output loss (Okun's rule of thumb, summarised in 1962, ties the unemployment rate to the output gap) but has social costs beyond output (crime, family breakdown, and the "hysteresis" by which skills erode under long-term unemployment), so writing employment into the investment standard amounts to admitting that fiscal investment carries social tasks beyond the output accounts. The boundary of the standard's applicability lies here too: screening projects in the name of employment sacrifices capital efficiency, and if this goes on for long, slower growth turns round and reduces employment — that is the internal tension of the "prop up projects" logic, which this chapter's synthetic essay returns to.

### Fiscal investment and financing

Fiscal investment and financing means raising funds through many channels on the basis of the state's credit and investing them, on a reimbursable basis, in fields of public character. It is a policy-based investment and financing: unlike ordinary fiscal investment, and unlike ordinary commercial investment, it is a new form of government investment lying between the two, marked by being reimbursable, public in character, non-profit-seeking, coordinated and flexible.

This "third financing channel" was designed for a dilemma: pure fiscal grant is hard-constrained by the size of the budget and cannot sustain large, long-term investment; pure commercial money demands profit and collateral and will not look at projects that run for decades, earn low returns but carry large social benefits (trunk railways, the regulation of great rivers, urban pipe networks). What fiscal investment and financing does is use the state's credit to put together money that neither route could raise or wait for — the sources of funds are typically low-cost public accumulations such as postal savings, pension surpluses and special-purpose funds (Japan's system, established in 1951, used postal savings and the national pension as its reservoirs, and its Fiscal Investment and Loan Programme was long called the "second budget", at one point approaching the general account budget in scale); the money is disbursed as low-interest long-term loans rather than grants, and the projects are picked by industrial policy. China's counterpart is the 1994 policy-finance reform: in that year the China Development Bank, the Export-Import Bank of China and the Agricultural Development Bank of China were founded as three policy banks; the CDB takes policy financial bonds as its main source of funds and directs long-term loans at infrastructure, basic industries and "two bases and one support" — the equity and debt structure of the high-speed rail network carries both its marks and those of the matching loans. The institutional risk comes from the same logic: when the boundary of state credit is blurred, policy loans slide easily into implicit guarantees for inefficient projects (the lesson of Japan's bubble-era Fiscal Investment and Loan Programme in the 1990s, and the controversy over the CDB's swelling commercial business after its 2008 restructuring), so the core question of any such mechanism is always where to draw the line between "public character" and "reimbursability".

### The build–operate–transfer (BOT) investment method

Under BOT the government, by inviting tenders, hands over a planned infrastructure project to a consortium or company, which sets up a project company to run it; for a fixed period set in the agreement, the project company services the debt and recovers the investment by operating the project; when the agreement expires, the property rights of the project are transferred to the government. Its greatest feature is that it encourages and attracts private investors (including foreign direct investors) to invest in infrastructure such as power stations, highways and energy development.

BOT answers the same question as fiscal investment and financing (where the money comes from) but in the opposite direction: the government does not borrow on its own credit, private capital puts up the money in exchange for a period of operating rights. Each of the three parties gets what it wants: the government obtains the facility without adding to its immediate debt; private capital earns user fees over the concession period; and prices and risks are fixed by contract. The term itself comes from the 1980s (in the same lineage as the privatisation of Turkish power stations and the Private Finance Initiative of Thatcher-era Britain); China's early landmark cases were the Shenzhen Shajiao B power station of the late 1980s (built with overseas capital on a BOOT basis) and the Guangxi Laibin B power station of the mid-1990s (the benchmark project of its day); in urban rail, Beijing Subway Line 4 (Beijing MTR, operating from 2009) was long the model of "private capital taking part in major infrastructure". China then scaled the idea up into the broader PPP (public–private partnership, explosive growth after 2014), and in doing so exposed the generic weakness of the model: if user charges fall short, "government payment / viability gap funding" becomes, in effect, debt with an equity mask, shifting the risk back onto the budget — from 2017–2018 there was a mass clean-up of non-standard PPP projects, and the new concession mechanism of 2023 explicitly focused on user-pays projects. Britain's audit verdict on the same class of tool agrees: the all-in capital cost of privately financed projects is higher than direct government borrowing, and the savings come mainly from management and incentives rather than from the financing itself (the 2012 UK review of PFI and the subsequent re-nationalisation of some projects). So the right way to read BOT is not as "magic that costs the government nothing" but as an intertemporal risk contract: the government's money has been exchanged for a premium paid to private capital for bearing construction and operating risk, and what decides whether the deal is worth it is who bears which risk and whether the pricing is fair.

### Social security

Social security means government programmes, financed by dedicated earmarked taxes, that provide basic livelihood support to the elderly, the unable to work, those who have lost the chance to work, the sick and the like. The social security system came into being alongside modern large-scale industry; the main economic reason for government provision is the failure of insurance markets. China's system is made up of four parts — social insurance, social relief, social welfare and social preferential treatment — of which social insurance is the core, consisting of the five items of old age, unemployment, medical care, work injury and maternity, and old-age insurance is in turn the most important.

Behind the four characters "insurance market failure" stand three sets of mechanisms which, taken together, explain why the market cannot insure the risks of birth, age, sickness and death, so that the government must. The first is adverse selection (Akerlof's 1970 "lemons" logic applied to insurance): those in poor health are the most eager to buy insurance, an insurer pricing at average risk loses its healthy customers, premiums rise again, more customers leave, and the market tends to collapse — the fact, in every country, that the very old and the chronically ill simply cannot buy commercial old-age or medical insurance. The second is moral hazard: full reimbursement breeds over-treatment, and unemployment benefits that run too long blunt the effort of job search — insurance changes the behaviour of the insured, and the changed behaviour in turn threatens the actuarial base. The third is individuals' intertemporal myopia: behavioural economics has repeatedly shown that the young systematically over-discount retirement savings forty years off, and old-age provision under a purely voluntary system is generally inadequate — which gives "compulsory enrolment" a paternalist reason. There are also considerations of distribution and macro stability: social security is one of the main channels of income redistribution, and unemployment benefit automatically expands spending in a downturn — by nature the automatic stabiliser that chapter 8 will discuss. China's institutional wording contains one technical detail easy to overlook: our social security takes the form of a "fee" rather than a "tax" (social insurance premiums), collected centrally by the tax authorities since 2019 — the choice between tax and fee is not a matter of naming: it bears on the coerciveness of collection, the level of pooling and the property rights over the individual account, and it is the institutional foreshadowing of the "empty account" debate taken up later.

## Problems and analysis

### By what standard does the government choose investment projects? — three standards and their own eras

Compared with private investment, fiscal investment seeks the maximum of social rather than private returns, and a large part of its funding comes from taxes raised gratuitously. For this reason the standard on which the government decides differs from that of the private sector, and which standard is finally adopted depends on the policy goals the government pursues in each period. There are three standards for fiscal investment at China's present stage:

- **The minimum capital–output ratio standard (the scarce-factor standard)**: choose the project with the largest output per unit of capital input. It answers the real condition of most developing countries, where capital is relatively scarce — even with the saving rate given, the capital–output ratio (the ICOR of development economics) is variable, and raising the turnover of capital magnifies output and growth.
- **The maximum capital–labour ratio standard**: choose the project that maximises marginal investment per head. The reasoning is that the higher the capital–labour ratio, the higher the technical composition of capital, the higher labour productivity and the faster growth — it requires the government to invest in capital-intensive projects.
- **The job-creation standard**: choose the project that mobilises the most labour per unit of investment, with both direct and indirect employment in view.

The three standards share one budget yet point to different project lists, and each has an era and a doctrine behind it. The first is the language of catch-up growth strategy: in the planning office's arithmetic, capital is the only binding constraint, and the lower the ICOR the faster the growth — this is essentially a Harrod–Domar growth view applied to the budget. The second lies behind the logic of heavy-industry priority: capital intensity means a higher organic composition and higher labour productivity, and China's industrialisation road of the 1950s was exactly this standard in practice (its cost — a capital-intensive heavy industry absorbed hardly any labour, and the state had to depress the cost of accumulation through the household registration and compulsory purchase systems — later became the object of the "comparative advantage strategy" reflection by Lin Yifu and others). The third turns the point of view from factor accumulation to the human being itself: when unemployment becomes the main contradiction, both "saving capital" and "deepening capital" give way to "putting labour to work". That the three coexist rather than supplant one another shows that the standard for fiscal investment decisions has never been a purely technical question — under every standard lies a value judgement about "what is scarcest at this moment", and the right way to read the dispute over standards is to reduce it to a dispute over policy goals.

### Where should fiscal investment in agriculture go? — infrastructure, ecology and technology

To solve the "three rural" problems (the countryside, agriculture, farmers), the government must increase its fiscal input into the rural areas and agriculture; with state investment limited, the key to raising investment efficiency is choosing the right priorities. The priorities of fiscal investment should include:

- **Rural and agricultural infrastructure**. Rural infrastructure covers basic rural education, health facilities, roads and the power grid; agricultural infrastructure is above all the comprehensive regulation of the great rivers and lakes, concentrating funds on large and medium water conservancy works with combined benefits, improving the conditions of agricultural production and raising overall productive capacity.
- **The agricultural ecological environment**. Raise spending on greening, soil and water conservation and shelterbelts, and step up investment in improving the agricultural ecological environment.
- **Agricultural R&D and technology extension**. The fundamental route of agricultural development lies in transforming traditional agriculture and raising labour productivity: support agricultural research institutions in carrying out research and push the development of new varieties and new techniques; raise spending on the extension of agricultural technique (labour-saving, yield-raising and efficiency-raising techniques such as improved seed, scientific fertiliser use, model-based cultivation, pest and disease control and water-saving irrigation); and increase education and training funds in the agricultural sector, so that most of the production labour force become "skilled workers".

The choice of these three priorities is not an arbitrary preference list; each corresponds to one form of market failure. Water conservancy and rural road networks are typical public goods or club goods: the investment is large, the cycle long, the benefits hard to split and charge for, and private capital cannot enter (large reservoirs and irrigation systems are precisely the mirror image of this chapter's earlier BOT discussion — projects that even a concession cannot price must be done by government). The ecological environment is an externality problem: the beneficiaries of shelterbelts and soil conservation lie across the whole basin and indeed the whole country, the builders collect no return, and only the treasury can internalise such a spillover. Agricultural research and extension carry both a public-good character and a positive externality — the core argument of Schultz's *Transforming Traditional Agriculture* of 1964 supports exactly this path: traditional agriculture is poor not because farmers are dull but because it lacks new factors of production at the right price; once usable technology exists (improved seed, fertiliser, irrigation systems), the return to agriculture can be very high. Hybrid rice (the three-line system completed in 1973) and the contribution of fiscal spending on improved seed and technique extension to every subsequent rise in grain output are this sentence's Chinese footnote; the continuing investment in high-standard farmland and in seed-industry revitalisation (the *Seed Industry Revitalisation Action Plan* of 2021) follows the same logic.

### How is the pension financed? — the intergenerational arithmetic of three models

By the mode of financing, old-age insurance falls into three models:

- **Fully funded**: benefits are paid from the interest earned on contributions accumulated in the past. Workers contribute over their working lives; the contributions are deposited in a government-managed fund that keeps accumulating and earning interest; the benefit received each year after retirement comes mainly from the interest income within it.
- **Pay-as-you-go**: benefits are paid out of current contributions — what is paid to today's retirees comes from the taxes (social security / payroll taxes) of the generation now working.
- **Partially funded**: a mixed system with the character of both — part of the social security tax pays today's benefits, and the remainder is invested in a social security trust fund to pay future benefits.

The three models divide over "who supports whom" and "with what", and each has its own weak point accordingly. The fully funded model is one's own savings supporting oneself — cleanest in intergenerational fairness, but it cannot withstand two things: inflation and capital-market swings wash away decades of accumulation (Chile privatised its public pension into individual accounts in 1981, and the subsequent fall in replacement rates and the protests that "what I saved for myself is not enough to retire on" are the instructional case for this risk), and it provides no intergenerational redistribution at all. The pay-as-you-go model makes demographic structure its actuarial base: with a stable dependency ratio it runs smoothly, but once ageing arrives the contributing generation shrinks and the recipient generation grows, and either tax rates rise or benefits fall — here is the arithmetic origin of the pension crises in many European countries. The partially funded model walks on two legs, but it introduces a subtler transition problem: the generation that switches from pay-as-you-go to a funded system must both go on supporting the retired (pooling contributions) and save for itself (individual account contributions), and the burden of double contribution must be borne somewhere as an implicit debt — precisely the reality China has faced ever since it established the "pooling plus accounts" model in 1997, taken up in this chapter's Limitations and further work.

### Since the "Tenth Five-Year Plan", what six things did finance do to raise input into the "three rural"?

Since the "Tenth Five-Year Plan" period (2001–2005), the finance authorities have taken successive measures to raise input into the "three rural"; the main measures were:

- **Deepening comprehensive rural reform centred on tax-and-fee reform**. From the reduction and exemption of the agricultural tax and the abolition of the special agricultural product tax (except tobacco) in 2004, to the complete abolition of the agricultural tax in 2006. The "imperial grain and state tax" of more than two thousand years ended there; on the Ministry of Finance's figures of the time, the policy eased farmers' burdens by about 125 billion yuan, about 140 yuan per head, and to cover the running gaps of townships and grassroots government the central budget arranged transfers of about 1,968.3 billion yuan across 2004–2006 (subject to official publication).
- **Implementing the "three subsidies" policy**. From 2004 the use structure of the grain risk fund was adjusted: direct subsidies to grain growers by planted area, plus improved-seed extension subsidies and farm-machinery purchase subsidies in parts of the country; in 2006 a comprehensive subsidy to grain growers was added after some farm input prices rose — cumulative subsidies over the three years of about 632.6 billion yuan.
- **Supporting the building of comprehensive agricultural productive capacity**. Raise investment in agricultural and rural infrastructure, ecological construction, technical progress and comprehensive development: in 2006 central "three rural" funds grew by about 14.2%, about 45.7% of the state capital-construction investment budget went to agriculture, and 10 billion yuan of newly added budgetary capital-construction investment went directly to improving rural production and living conditions; over 2003–2006 funds for the key forestry ecological projects totalled about 961.6 billion yuan, natural forest protection and afforestation funds about 326.7 billion yuan, and forest ecological benefit compensation about 8 billion yuan.
- **Establishing a funding guarantee mechanism for rural compulsory education**. From 2006, on the principle of "make each level's responsibility clear, share between centre and locality, raise fiscal input, lift the level of provision, implement step by step", rural compulsory education was brought fully under fiscal guarantee, with all tuition and fees waived for rural primary and secondary students in the western regions; over 2006–2010 the plan was to add about 218.2 billion yuan of new rural compulsory education funding in total, while also supporting experimental training bases for vocational education.
- **Raising input into rural basic medical care, public health and social security**. The pilot of the new rural cooperative medical scheme (launched in 2003) was extended to about 40% of the country's counties (cities, districts); the subsidy standard from both central and local finance rose from 10 to 20 yuan per person per year, with the centre arranging 4.73 billion yuan accordingly — a more-than-sevenfold increase; medical assistance was strengthened at the same time, easing poverty caused or renewed by illness.
- **Advancing the pilot of equity participation in comprehensive agricultural development investment**. From 2004, in Hebei, Henan and other provinces, state development funds were piloted in equity participation in operations, following the principles of "voluntary application, equal competition, support for the best; whoever invests owns, benefits and bears the risk; separation of government and enterprise, entrusted supervision, authorised operation; standardised operation, steady advance, timely exit" — the fiscal funds take shares but do not control, and are not the largest shareholder.

This list is worth reading item by item for its structure: the first two are "give money and lift burdens" (tax and fee exit, direct subsidy enter), the middle two are "make up basic public services" (compulsory education, the new rural cooperative medical scheme — extending to the countryside services urban residents had long enjoyed), and the last two are "change the way of investing" (project funds for ecology and productive capacity, equity participation using market mechanisms to lever social capital in). After 2013 this line was upgraded to the targeted poverty alleviation campaign (the central poverty alleviation and development conference of 2015, the 2020 declaration that absolute poverty had been eliminated) and the rural revitalisation strategy (proposed in 2017, the *Rural Revitalisation Promotion Law* in force in 2021), with the citizenisation of the rural migrant population and the equalisation of urban and rural basic public services becoming the new themes — but these six measures of the "Tenth Five-Year Plan" period are the turning point at which finance turned from "taking" to "giving", after which agricultural subsidies and rural public service spending entered a normal growth track (specific sizes subject to the final accounts published each year).

## Synthetic essay

### Which fiscal investment standard should China use at the present stage? — the case for the job-creation standard, and its boundary

The standards for fiscal investment decisions include the minimum capital–output ratio, the maximum capital–labour ratio and job creation. At China's present stage the job-creation standard should serve as an important standard of fiscal investment decisions, for these reasons:

- In theory, labour is the most important economic resource, and the existence of unemployment is an unpardonable waste of a strategic resource; more employment generally brings more output as well.
- China's labour resources are extremely abundant, and the government must consider the full use of this resource from a strategic, overall point of view; fiscal investment projects are an effective route to creating employment.
- Even leaving output aside, adding employment has an independent value of its own: mass unemployment breeds crime, social disorder and even more serious conflict, and the social benefit of adding employment far exceeds the significance of adding output.
- The job-creation standard also helps to improve income distribution: unemployment sinks families into poverty, and transfer-style redistribution is limited by political and social conditions; the fundamental measure for raising poor families' income is adding employment, and expanding fiscal investment (especially into backward regions) creates jobs for the poor directly — private investment decisions weigh rates of return, not job creation.

The common skeleton of these four reasons is: when labour is relatively abundant and capital relatively non-scarce, investing capital in labour is at once buying growth, stability and fairness. In policy practice, the standard gets invoked most readily in crisis years — the public works relief of the Great Depression (the work relief of America's WPA/CWA), and China's mass infrastructure investment in response to the 1998 Asian financial crisis and the 2008 global financial crisis, all objectively carried an employment-absorbing function; the "six stabilisations" and "six guarantees" of 2018–2019 placed employment at the front of macro policy, and the 2019 *Government Work Report* for the first time raised employment-first policy to the macro-policy level — that is this standard expressed in institutional language. But its boundary must be stated just as clearly: first, "more people" at project level is not "worthwhile" at the level of society — jobs bought with inefficient works are paid for by taxes and future growth, and Keynes's thought experiment of "burying banknotes and hiring men to dig them up" is a satire precisely because it counts demand but not assets. Second, the employment effect depends heavily on the mode of financing: government investment financed by deficit works in the short run, which connects with the fiscal balance question of chapter 8. Third, the more precise modern instrument is "support the person, not the project" — skills training, social insurance subsidies, public-interest jobs, and the special work-relief programmes (the national work-relief tasks and coverage expanded markedly in 2022) aim directly at the employability and opportunities of distressed groups, rather than letting the project itself create jobs in passing. So the complete statement should be: job creation is a social benefit that fiscal investment must count, but it should not be the only benefit.

### Why does finance put money into the "three rural"? — four necessities and three priorities

**(1) Necessity.** Speeding up rural urbanisation, agricultural industrialisation and the rise of farmers' incomes cannot land without more fiscal input, for four reasons:

- The rural population is numerous and development relatively backward, and the key to building a moderately prosperous society in all respects (worded today as common prosperity and Chinese-style modernisation) lies in the "three rural".
- Agriculture is the foundation of the national economy and the industry most in need of development: it supplies labour, food and raw materials to the non-agricultural sectors, widens the domestic market, and earns foreign exchange through agricultural exports.
- The agricultural sector's productivity is low and it can hardly accumulate enough on its own: constrained by natural conditions and low in value added, private or collective capital accumulates slowly; it not only fails to attract good resources but keeps losing its own (labour, funds) to higher-return industries — this is the root of the urban–rural investment imbalance, and the reason finance must step in.
- Many agricultural investment projects suit only government: large reservoirs and irrigation works involve large sums, long periods and wide ramifications, their benefits are hard to split, and the cost–benefit relation is not obvious — the public-good character decides the market will not supply them of its own accord.

**(2) Priorities.** Under the constraint of limited investment, three: rural and agricultural infrastructure (education, health, roads, the power grid; regulation of the great rivers and lakes, and large and medium water conservancy); the agricultural ecological environment (greening, soil and water conservation, shelterbelts); agricultural R&D and technology extension (new varieties and techniques, extension of agricultural technique, training of the labour force).

Of the four necessities, the third is the one most worth chewing on. It is not the moral claim of "agriculture is poor, so help agriculture", but a self-reinforcing vicious circle: low productivity → low return → outflow of factors (young and middle-aged labour and rural savings flow to the cities, and the long-run "reverse flow" of rural funds into the cities is a phenomenon repeatedly noted in the financial statistics) → productivity falls further behind. Fiscal input here works by breaking the circle — supply the infrastructure and research that have the strongest spillovers and that private capital will not enter, so that the private return to agriculture picks up first, and social capital will follow; Schultz's claim (the transformation of traditional agriculture rests on the supply of new factors of production and on price incentives) is logically isomorphic with this path. After the abolition of the agricultural tax in 2006 (see the previous question), this positioning was upgraded from "take less" to "give more, take less, enliven", and to the equalisation of urban and rural basic public services, and the continuing investment from 2021 in key counties for rural revitalisation and in high-standard farmland are extensions of the same framework.

## Limitations and further work

The two systems of this chapter — policy investment and financing, and social security — both went through a tug-of-war between "the arithmetic at design time" and "the demographic and technical change after launch", and each lesson is representative.

The partial funding of pensions (pooling plus accounts) is the textbook's "mixed beats pure" frustrated in reality. When the system was set up in 1997, the plan was to make the individual account a genuine accumulation; but at the transition, the "old" and "middle" cohorts already retired or near retirement had no contribution record, and their pensions had to be paid out of current contributions — when the pooling account could not cover its outgo it borrowed from the individual account funds, producing the long-standing "empty account operation" (the amounts booked to individual accounts exceed the actual assets). The responses of 1997 through the 2000s (pilots to make individual accounts real, the National Social Security Fund set up in 2000 as a strategic reserve, the merger of the pension schemes of government agencies and public institutions in 2015, the transfer of social insurance collection to the tax authorities after the 2018 national-local tax reorganisation, the launch of the third pillar of personal pensions in 2022, and the central adjustment of enterprise employee pension funds from 2019 moving to national pooling) — every step addressed this transition gap. International experience offers another set of options: Sweden's 1994 reform kept pay-as-you-go but embedded actuarial linkage through notional defined contribution (NDC) accounts, followed by Italy and Poland; the Chilean privatisation model was progressively corrected amid mass protest after the 2000s (a solidarity pension and maternity cover introduced in 2008). China's choice has been gradual repair — notional accounts were discussed but not adopted, and national pooling together with transferring state-owned capital to replenish the social security fund (the 2017 pilot plan) are the current main line. Judging any model comes down to the same arithmetic: the speed of ageing, the path of the dependency ratio, wage growth and fund returns decide more than the elegance of the institutional design (the national pooling of basic old-age insurance in force from 2023 and the gradual rise of the statutory retirement age — the relevant decision passed by the NPC Standing Committee in 2024 — are actuarial patches calibrated to the demographic variables; details subject to the formal documents).

The global experience of BOT/PPP has also gone round a bend. British official assessments after 2012 showed the all-in financing cost of the Private Finance Initiative (PFI) was significantly higher than government bond issue, that risk transfer was often overestimated and re-pricing often over budget; Japan's Fiscal Investment and Loan Programme swelled with the bubble in the 1990s and was sharply cut back in the 2000s; China's PPP expanded in 2014–2017, then went through a clean-up, and the new concession mechanism of 2023 explicitly "focuses on user-pays projects". The common conclusion of the three countries/phases is to put this chapter's intermediary tools back where they belong: they do not create free infrastructure, they swap "current fiscal expenditure" for "future contingent liabilities plus an efficiency contract"; the test of a BOT deal is not whether the money comes from a private pocket, but whether the risk pricing, the sufficiency of competition and the whole-life cost favour the public.

The meeting point of social security and fiscal investment grows clearer: the rising share of social security spending in China is transition costs and ageing laid on top of each other (the deficit discussion of chapter 8 will meet it again), and "investing in people" (health, education, care) is becoming a new analytical category in public finance — the UNDP and World Bank satellite accounting of the national accounts makes care spending explicit as investment, and behavioural economics (Thaler and Sunstein's *Nudge*, 2008) refines the design of "the paternalist reason for compulsory enrolment": default enrolment options, automatic escalation (the American automatic-enrolment IRA), and the linking of pension contributions to wage growth. At the border between public finance and individual behaviour, the chapter's two old questions — what should the state invest in, and how should security be designed — are being asked anew.
