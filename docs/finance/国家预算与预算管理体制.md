# Chapter 7 · Government budget and the budget management system

> **The central question of this chapter**: the state takes in and spends tens of trillions of yuan a year — what makes anyone believe it "collects by the plan and spends by the plan"? What rules must the budget's ledger obey, and who audits it? In dividing money and duties between centre and locality, why does almost the whole world converge on the same pattern — revenue tilted to the centre, expenditure tilted to the localities?

## The arc of this chapter

The birth of the budget system and the birth of representative politics are two sides of one thing: Magna Carta in 1215 established that "taxation requires consent", and the four hundred years of tug-of-war between parliament and crown were in essence a contest for "control of the purse" — Britain set up the Consolidated Fund in 1787 and formed the regular annual budget report in the early 19th century, and "the budget" thus passed from the crown's private account into a public ledger open to parliament; the municipal budget reform movement in America in the early 1900s and the Budget and Accounting Act of 1921 spread the executive budget system nationwide. This history explains the phrase in the budget concept most easily mistaken for form — "the government budget takes effect only after approval by the national legislature": the power of approval is not an administrative step but the legitimating device of the modern state; spending without the consent of the representative assembly is unlawful. China's path to a law-based budget compressed this history: the Budget Law was passed in 1994 (in force 1995), and the great revision of 2014 (in force 2015) established the whole-calibre budget — the "four budgets" (general public budget, government-managed funds budget, state-owned capital operations budget, social insurance funds budget) were written into law, together with budget disclosure, the cap management of local government borrowing, and the regularisation of transfer payments; the matching management reforms rolled out from around 2000 — departmental budgets (one budget per department), the separation of revenue and expenditure channels, centralised treasury receipt and payment, government procurement — sweeping the "off-the-books accounts" scattered across ministries into a single treasury. The other half of this chapter is the vertical relation: the budget management system handles the fiscal relations between centre and locality and among the levels of local government, and its modern form is the 1994 tax-sharing system — two faces of the same reform package as the tax system reform of the chapter on taxation. The theoretical groundwork had been laid decades earlier: Tiebout's 1956 "voting with the feet" model and Oates's 1972 *Fiscal Federalism* proved the efficiency case for local provision of local public goods, while centralisation is irreplaceable for externalities, redistribution and macro stability. The concepts section gives the basic frame of the budget and the system, the problems-and-analysis section handles the principles, the law, the basis for dividing revenue and expenditure and the features of the tiered tax-sharing system, and the ideas for improving the tax-sharing system in the synthetic essay were written in the late 1990s — several of those judgements were later checked, one by one, against the practice of reform, and that is laid out specially in the closing "Limitations and further work".

## Core concepts

### The government budget

The government budget is the state's basic plan of fiscal revenue and expenditure. In content, it requires the various fiscal revenue and expenditure plans to be entered, year by year, in prescribed forms; in procedure, it is the process of arranging the plan in advance, then raising and using fiscal funds according to it. The government budget takes effect only upon approval by the national legislature; once passed, all organs of state power and related units must carry it out strictly. Budgetary activity is bound by the budget law. The budget takes many forms and methods: single budget, double budget, zero-based budget, incremental budget, medium- and long-term budget, programme budget, action budget, national economic budget, full-employment budget, and so on. Generally, every level of government has its budget, and the government budget consists of the central budget and the local budgets.

The English word budget comes from the old French for "leather bag" — the British Chancellor carried the budget papers to parliament in one, and the etymology lets slip the essence of the budget: it is first of all an instrument of constraint, not a planning document. The budget's several faces are worth telling apart. The double budget separates current receipts and outlays from capital ones (so one can see whether the government is "living its life" or "buying assets", and it sets up a ledger for debt management); China trialled it from 1992 and the 1994 Budget Law confirmed the double-budget frame. Zero-based budgeting requires every spending item to be justified from zero each year (rather than added to or subtracted from last year's base — the arithmetic of the incremental budget); the Carter administration rolled it out federally in 1977, many countries have tried it in cycles, and from 2024 China has deepened zero-based budgeting pilots in central departments. The "full-employment budget" is the Keynesian-era drafting idea of estimating revenue at full-employment output in order to judge the fiscal stance (the cyclical budget of chapter 8 connects to it). The "four budgets" after China's 2014 Budget Law revision are the map to the real budget: the general public budget (tax-based), the government-managed funds budget (land transfer revenue-based), the state-owned capital operations budget (profits handed up by state-owned enterprises) and the social insurance funds budget — the four ledgers differ in nature, source and reallocation rules, and reading only the first misses nearly half of the government's true financial resources (land transfer revenue used to run at seventy or eighty percent of general public budget revenue, falling back in recent years; latest published figures govern).

### "Two-channel" management of revenue and expenditure

"Two-channel" management means that state organs, public institutions, social organisations and other organisations pay in full into the treasury, or a special fiscal account, the non-tax revenue of the government they collect by law under state rules, while their spending is arranged through budgets drawn by the finance department, with funds collected and disbursed through the treasury or special fiscal account.

The institution targets a very specific incentive trap: if a department can "collect its own fees and spend its own money", every administrative power gets developed into a fee — fees for approval, fees for inspection, fees for licences, and the motive of fee-collecting in turn reinforces the expansion of power, while money circulating outside the body escapes all supervision. The knife-work of the two channels is to cut the "collecting" and the "spending" of the same money into two processes blind to each other: revenue, whatever its size, is paid over in full to the treasury or special account (the collecting unit never touches the money), spending is arranged separately under the budget (decoupled from what the unit paid in), and the separation of fine-imposing from fine-collecting and of invoice from cash cuts further between "the one who writes the ticket" and "the one who takes the money". Together with the departmental budget reform (trials in central departments from 2000) and the centralised treasury receipt and payment system (pilots from 2001), it forms one combination whose aim is to gather all fiscal funds into "one vat": the principle of budget completeness (one of the five principles in the next section) thereby turns from a demand into a technical reality. The clean-up of "small treasuries" (the nationwide campaign of 2009) was the closing move on the same line. The institution also points at the general difficulty of non-tax revenue management: China's land transfer revenue, administrative and institutional fees and fines are enormous, and once they escape budget constraint, fiscal transparency and the business environment suffer together — the two channels are the base of that management bottom line.

### The government budget law

The government budget law is the body of legal norms for government budget management, the legal basis for organising and managing the state budget; its main task is to fix the relations of powers and duties, and of revenue and expenditure allocation, between the national legislature and the executive, between centre and locality, and between the consolidated budget and unit budgets.

What is special about the budget law is that it is "the law by which the government governs its own purse" — behind every administrative law stands the budget law, because any statutory function with no budget behind it is a bounced cheque. China's 1994 Budget Law embodied four guiding ideas: strengthen the legal binding force of the budget, making budget revenue and expenditure genuinely rigid statutory indicators; regularise budget management procedure and make budget powers clear; closely combine strengthening budget management with promoting reform and developing the economy; and combine the scientific soundness of the law with its feasibility. The great revision of 2014 pushed these four in new directions: budget completeness (all four budgets brought into law, and with that the concept of "off-budget funds" died); budget disclosure (the subjects, deadlines and content of disclosure written into law, turning "openness" from a principle into an enforceable institution); local borrowing with "the front door open and the back door blocked" (provincial bond issue within a State-Council-approved cap is legal, while no other form of borrowing is permitted — the legal base for the local debt governance of chapter 6); and the hardening of budget constraint (no level may set revenue collection targets for the collecting departments, and spending must rest on the approved budget). The Implementation Regulations of the Budget Law, revised in 2021, carried the rules on whole-calibre, transfers and debt down to an executable level. In comparative law, budget law converges everywhere: the approval and supervision powers of the representative assembly keep growing (the appropriations committee system of the US Congress, Brazil's budget transparency portal), and "controlling how the government spends" is the single theme of budget law.

### Budget adjustment

Budget adjustment is an important procedure in budget execution. It means that a budget already approved at any level, in the course of execution, needs — because of special circumstances — to raise expenditure or cut revenue, so that total expenditure exceeds total revenue or the amount of borrowing already authorised increases, partially changing the original budget.

The adjustment institution handles the contradiction between plan and change, and its design philosophy can be read off "what counts as an adjustment": spending increases, revenue shortfalls, added borrowing — any change that worsens the balance or the deficit must go back for approval (in China the standing committees of the people's congresses at each level), while over-collection and savings reallocated during execution are handled by the government within set rules and reported. Why must adjustment go through the legislature? Because the budget's rigidity is its entire value: if the government could move money at will, approval would be theatre. The 2023 case shows the point of the procedure best: in the fourth quarter of that year the central budget issued an additional 1 trillion yuan of national bonds and correspondingly raised the central deficit (the year's deficit ratio moving from 3% to about 3.8%), on precisely the Budget Law's adjustment procedure — proposed by the State Council, deliberated and approved by the NPC Standing Committee, and executed thereafter, with all the funds passed to localities through transfers (subject to official announcement). The case also shows the relation of the adjustment institution to "proactive fiscal policy": counter-cyclical top-ups are normal policy behaviour; what the institution requires is "a top-up must pass one more democratic procedure", not that no top-up be allowed. Adjacent to adjustment are the "contingency reserve" (set at 1–3% of expenditure, for sudden outlays) and the budget working balance — the institution trades small flexibility for large rigidity.

### Off-budget funds

Off-budget funds are funds that, under the state fiscal system, are not included in the government budget and that local finance departments and administrative and public institutions funded by the budget are allowed to collect and spend on their own; they are a kind of fiscal fund outside the government budget.

In China today this is a historical concept — from 1 January 2011, all revenue previously under off-budget management was brought into budget management, and "off-budget" was abolished as a management category. But its rise and fall is a condensed textbook of this chapter's whole institutional logic, worth retelling as a case. In the planned-economy era off-budget funds were an arrangement leaving localities and enterprises room to breathe; in the market reform they swelled: by the mid-1990s off-budget revenue was at one point on a par with budget revenue (around 1992 it stood at about nine-tenths of the budget total, subject to official historical data), and the consequence was the list that recurs in this section — opaque, hard to supervise, prone to corruption, chaotic fee-collecting. The governance path ran in sequence: the State Council's 1996 *Decision on Strengthening the Management of Off-Budget Funds* brought a considerable part of the fees into special fiscal accounts (the two channels); the departmental budget and centralised treasury reforms of the 2000s shrank their room; a 2010 Ministry of Finance document brought the whole into budget management; and the 2014 Budget Law revision finally sealed the category off in law with the "whole-calibre budget". The methodological lesson it left is written into the principle of budget completeness: the government's money knows no "inside" or "outside" — whatever is obtained by public authority belongs in the same ledger under the same scrutiny, or any "on-budget balance" may be an accounting game.

### The budget management system

The budget management system is the collective name for the institutions that handle the fiscal relations between centre and locality and among the levels of local government; it is the institutional and legal basis for drawing up, executing and settling the state budget and for budget supervision, and the leading link of the fiscal management system. To put the distributional relations between centre and locality in order, in line with the basic requirement of building a socialist market economy, China established after the 1994 fiscal reform a tax-sharing budget management system drawing on foreign experience. The system helps stabilise the budget revenue sources of centre and locality, clarifies the functions and powers of each level's budget, joins powers to duties, and mobilises the initiative of every level of government in budget management.

Reading the budget management system as "the Chinese scheme of fiscal federalism" needs two theoretical props and one historical coordinate. The props: first, Oates's decentralisation theorem (1972) — the more heterogeneous local preferences and the more local the benefit range of a public good, the more efficient is local provision, since the locality knows local preferences better and residents can "vote with their feet" (Tiebout 1956), imposing competitive discipline; second, the irreplaceability of the centre — income redistribution (the poor should not receive fewer public services for living in a poor province), cross-regional externalities (the regulation of great rivers), and macro stability can only be carried by the centre. The pattern the two synthesise into: expenditure tilted to the locality (local spending is over half of national fiscal spending in most countries, and China's local share is higher still, at about 85% — education, health and social security, the big items, all sit on the locality), revenue tilted to the centre (the centre concentrates enough money to hold the levers of redistribution and regulation). The historical coordinate is the 1994 tax-sharing system: taxes were split into central taxes (customs, consumption tax), local taxes (property tax, the former business tax, etc.) and shared taxes (VAT, income tax), with national and local tax bureaux set up separately (merged in 2018 into a unified tax service), and the central revenue share leapt from about 22% in 1993 to around 55%, where it has stayed (subject to official publication); "local spending covered by local revenue plus transfers" has been the stable structure of central-local fiscal relations for thirty years since, its friction points (land finance, local debt, transfer dependence) taken up later in this chapter and in chapter 8.

## Problems and analysis

### What rules must the budget obey? — where the five principles come from

The principles of the government budget are the guiding ideas the state follows in choosing the form and system of the budget, the policy for drawing up the fiscal revenue and expenditure plan; there are five:

- **Openness**: the budget and how it is executed must be published in some form, so that the people can see fiscal revenue and expenditure and hold them under supervision.
- **Reliability**: the figures of every revenue and expenditure item must be worked out by scientific methods on sufficient grounds and solid data — no assumed estimates, still less fabricated ones.
- **Completeness**: all fiscal revenue and expenditure that belongs in the government budget must be reflected in it — no false accounts, no separate off-budget budgets; revenue and expenditure the state permits off budget should also be reflected in the budget.
- **Unity**: every level's budget is a part of the government budget, and must use unified budget items, computed and entered on one basis and by one procedure.
- **Annuality**: drawing up and carrying out the budget must be bounded in time — the budgetary year, normally one year.

These five principles were born of 19th-century European parliamentary struggle against the royal purse (the standard version was systematised by the German scholar Neumark and others), and each answers one chronic disease of monarchical finance: openness against the secret ledger, completeness against "accounts behind the accounts" (the crown's "privileged revenues" and off-budget levies), unity against the departmental small treasuries, reliability against dressing up balance with debt and expected revenue, and annuality from the parliamentary tradition of annual authorisation — the legislature scrutinising once a year is the executive being tamed once a year. Each principle's Chinese career has its own record: budget disclosure entered the Budget Law in 2014 with deadlines and content requirements, and since 2015 central and local departmental budgets, final accounts and the "three public expenses" have been continuously published; completeness is carried by the whole-calibre four budgets and the abolition of off-budget funds; unity landed in the 2007 reform of government revenue and expenditure classification and the integrated budget management system (running nationwide from 2022, all national fiscal funds moving through one platform); annuality has a technical modern variant — the medium-term fiscal framework (three-year rolling plans drafted from 2015) and multi-year budgets, which do not abolish annuality but give the annual budget an intertemporal anchor. The principles also hold tensions (chasing within-year "reliability" sacrifices spending flexibility; "unity" means tolerating coarse items), and the history of budget technique is to a large degree the history of re-balancing among them.

### On what ground does the budget law bind the government itself? — four guiding ideas and one law

China's Budget Law embodies four guiding ideas:

- strengthen the legal binding force of the budget, making budget revenue and expenditure genuinely rigid statutory indicators;
- regularise budget management procedure and make budget powers clear;
- closely combine strengthening budget management with promoting reform and developing the economy;
- combine the scientific soundness of the law with its feasibility.

The four ideas answer one difficulty: how to make the maker of the rules (the government) bound by the rules too. The first is the core — "statutory rigidity" means a budget, once passed by the people's congress, has legal force, and change must pass a legal procedure (the budget adjustment institution of the previous section); the second fixes in statutory language the chain of powers — who drafts, who approves, who executes, who supervises — to block off-procedure flows of money; the third and fourth are a conscious legislative technique — the budget law cannot be a pile of idealist clauses, it must leave interfaces for reform (the "open the front door" clause on local debt was the lawful channel 2014 left open to a real debt problem), or it will be generally evaded and survive in name only. The test of budget rule-of-law lies not in the text but in a few relationships in execution: whether the people's congress review becomes substantive (from 2017 central budget review and supervision expanded towards expenditure budgets and policy, with audit results linked to congress supervision), whether over-collection and surplus are handled properly, and who gives way when a statutory expenditure conflicts with a leader's will. Budget performance management (the 2018 *Opinions on Fully Implementing Budget Performance Management*) added the last piece of this constraint: not only "spend by the budget" but "spend to effect, and answer for non-effect" — from compliance control to performance control is the direction in which budget-law thought is being extended in the 21st century.

### How are the money and the duties divided between centre and locality? — two basic grounds

There are two basic grounds for dividing revenue and expenditure:

- **The tiered character of social public needs**. On the expenditure side, levels are distinguished by benefit range (national needs to the centre, local needs to the locality, cross-regional ones shared); on the revenue side, there is a tiering of sources too. Judged by the outcome of central and local revenue and expenditure, the two sides must always match up — only matched revenue and expenditure keep fiscal balance — so the division of revenue is constrained by the division of expenditure, that is, by the benefit range of public needs; but local spending needs and local revenue capacity are not symmetric, so central transfers are needed to adjust, and the division of revenue must follow further standards besides.
- **The relation between centralisation and decentralisation**. The tiering of public needs is the general standard of a tiered budget system in a market economy, but actual divisions of revenue and expenditure differ greatly between countries, depending mainly on the centralisation–decentralisation relation settled by the political system and national conditions.

The essence of the first ground is "benefit range decides where the expenditure sits" — the beneficiaries of national defence are the whole people, so it goes to the centre; the beneficiaries of a street lamp are the residents of one block, so it goes to the locality; this "matching principle" comes from Oates's decentralisation theorem: whoever's information advantage covers the benefit range should provide. But the textbook's second half is where Chinese fiscal reality centres: once expenditure is divided by benefit range, revenue cannot be divided on the same boundary (tax bases are mobile, tax sources are very unequal across regions), and so the inevitable structure is "expenditure on the locality, revenue centralised, the gap filled by transfers" — which also foreshadows the synthetic essay's judgement that "perfecting transfers is the key to perfecting the tax-sharing system". The second ground is the reminder that fiscal decentralisation is never only an efficiency question. The difference between the French-type centralisation and the German and American-type decentralisations goes back to unitary versus federal polities and historical tradition (Germany's "tax union" tradition, the independent taxing powers of American states, are both constitutional legacies), and China's pattern of "centre-led, with regard to the locality" corresponds to the political architecture of a unitary state — so in comparing countries' fiscal systems, revenue–expenditure ratios are the surface, and the constitutional arrangement the root.

### What does a tiered, tax-sharing budget system look like? — five basic features

The tiered tax-sharing budget system is the system generally practised in market economies; its main features are regularisation, legalisation, long-term relative stability, and local budgets constituting genuine first-level budget entities. Its basic content includes:

- one level of government, one budget entity; each level's budget relatively independent, seeking its own balance;
- on the premise of a clear boundary for government functions under the market economy, the duties (functional responsibilities) of each level of government are divided, and on that basis the expenditure duties (fiscal powers) of each level's budget;
- the division of revenue implements the tax-sharing system: in proportion the centre holds the dominant share to guarantee regulatory power; in method, either by tax type, or by splitting the rate of one tax, or by shared proceeds;
- the budget adjustment institution — the transfer payment system — takes two forms, vertical adjustment (the classic being the central grant to localities) and horizontal adjustment (between regions);
- each country's system is formed over the long run by its own political and economic institutions and historical tradition, and is stable as a whole, while the centralisation–decentralisation relation and its adjustment methods can be tuned frequently.

The five features form one self-consistent system, and reading them means catching their order of dependence: first feature 2 (division of duties), then feature 3 (revenue assigned to duties), then feature 4 (transfers filling the remaining gap) — a system with the order reversed will grind on friction. Feature 1's "seeking its own balance" is often misread as "local governments may not run deficits"; the exact meaning is that each level's budget answers for itself and the centre does not stand behind local debt — China's post-2014 local debt principle of "whose child, whose parent carries it" is precisely this item in Chinese, while the actual central handling of local hidden debt (swap, debt relief support) shows the credibility of the "no bail-out" promise is itself a dynamic question in the system's evolution. Feature 5 is the historical-materialist reminder: American states still keep independent sales and income tax systems, shared taxes dominate in Germany, and neither can be simply transplanted — the "sharing" of the tax-sharing system obeys polity and tradition, not some optimal parameter. Every stroke of China's concrete split (consumption tax to the centre, to suppress the incentive distortion of localities fostering tobacco and liquor industry; VAT shared, fifty-fifty from 2016, to balance incentives; income taxes shared sixty-forty from 2002) is a repeated calibration of "incentive compatibility".

## Synthetic essay

### How should the tax-sharing system be improved further? — four lines of thought and their later testing

The basic lines of thought for improving China's tax-sharing system were four:

**(1) Clarify further the scope of each level's functional duties and each budget entity's expenditure responsibilities.** This includes: making concrete the principle of the government–market relationship (the transformation of government functions); fixing the division of functional duties among levels of government in legal form, with a clear division of labour conducted by law; and dividing expenditure responsibilities in line with functions and fiscal powers, without forcing exact matching on every specific matter.

**(2) Regularise the division of revenue.** The tax-sharing system is closely bound to the tax system; once the tax system is adjusted, the division of revenue must follow — dividing revenue by tax type and dividing it by administrative affiliation have entirely different effects; corporate income tax was at the time still divided by affiliation, so a move to sharing was the inevitable choice; taxes whose base is spread nationwide or with strong regulatory function should go to the centre, and the then assignment of individual income tax to the locality was only a stopgap; completing the local tax system should be an important goal of tax system reform.

**(3) Adjust the relation between centralisation and decentralisation.** On the expenditure side, most items are carried out by the locality, close to the grassroots and familiar with residents' preferences, so the local expenditure share should be large; on the revenue side, to even out horizontal imbalances and carry macro policy, the centre's revenue should be dominant — the common pattern of tiered budget systems everywhere.

**(4) Perfecting the transfer payment system is the key to perfecting the tax-sharing system.** The basic route: define the target model of transfers, approach it by fine adjustment of both increments and stock, and push forward the establishment and completion of the transfer system below the provincial level.

What gives these four lines their special value is the testing record of the twenty-plus years after they were written. The two concrete judgements in item (2) both came true: from 2002 corporate income tax moved to a 60:40 central–local sharing (the distortion of division by affiliation — central enterprises paying where they sat, local enterprises to the locality — ended there), individual income tax moved the same year to the same sixty-forty sharing, and the "stopgap" judgement was adopted verbatim; the fixed-asset investment direction adjustment tax was suspended in 2000 and formally abolished in 2013. Item (1) took a more winding road but not a different direction: the State Council's 2016 *Guiding Opinions on Advancing the Reform of Dividing Fiscal Powers and Expenditure Responsibilities between Central and Local Governments* put forward, for the first time systematically, the concept of fiscal powers and the principles of their division; the 2018 list of common fiscal powers in basic public services landed; the 2022 *Guiding Opinions on Further Advancing Fiscal System Reform below the Provincial Level* pushed the same logic below the province — "matching powers with expenditure responsibilities" passed from textbook proposition into the language of reform documents. The transfers of item (4) are now the largest single item in central–local relations: central transfers to localities have passed 10 trillion yuan (crossing 10 trillion in 2023, subject to latest publication), with general transfers (equalisation-type, formula-based) rising in share and special transfers being cleaned up and consolidated, and the below-province system advancing in step. That a late-1990s list of reform ideas was taken up, item by item, by later practice shows that "perfecting" the tax-sharing system is not a completed event but a continuing adjustment on a decadal scale.

## Limitations and further work

The frame of this chapter keeps evolving in three directions, each "an old question on a new scale".

First, **the deep-water zone of powers and expenditure responsibilities**. The reforms since 2016 distilled "fiscal powers" out of the vague notion of "duties" (who decides, who executes, who pays, who supervises, defined separately) and divide them by three principles — externality, informational complexity, incentive compatibility; but the big common powers in education, health and social security still run on many transitional arrangements of "centre and locality sharing in proportion", and regional differences of standard (in the same compulsory education, per-student funding differs between east, centre and west) leave "equalisation of basic public services" a work in progress. Theoretically this is the sinification of the Tiebout–Oates frame: population moving across regions (citizenisation) puts the "local preferences" model out of true — whether the public services of the migrant population are supplied by the receiving place or the registered place is the frontier case of fiscal decentralisation theory in contemporary China.

Second, **the triangle of transfers, land finance and local debt**. After the tax-sharing system concentrated revenue, localities filled the gap with land transfer revenue (the government-managed funds budget), which is the public-finance explanation of "land finance" since the 2000s (chapter 8 and chapter 6 both hang their debt questions on this line); after the long-cycle turn of real estate, land transfer revenue contracted (falling markedly from 2022, subject to latest publication), the offsetting pressure on transfers and debt-relief policy rose sharply, and the 2023 debt-relief package and the 2024 local debt swap are the latest moves in that adjustment. The next agenda of the budget system — moving the consumption tax collection point back and assigning it to localities, regularising non-tax revenue, cultivating a local tax system — are all attempts to re-balance the same gap.

Third, **the frontier of budget technique: from the paper ledger to integrated systems and performance budgeting**. The integrated budget management system (running nationwide from 2022) puts drafting, execution, accounting and final accounts on one data base, and the principles of "unity" and "completeness" gained technical enforcement for the first time; the reform of government comprehensive financial reporting (accrual-based government financial reports, drafted in full trial from 2019) gave government, for the first time, a financial portrait like a corporate balance sheet — the future gaps of local hidden debt, state-owned assets and the social insurance funds can now be "seen whole". Performance budgeting (2018's full implementation of budget performance management) and international programme rating (America's PART, Korea's self-assessment) converge from different roads: the object of budget scrutiny is moving from "how much was spent" to "what result was bought". These technical evolutions will not replace the chapter's political logic — the purse controlled by the representative assembly, revenue–expenditure division following the assignment of duties, centralisation and decentralisation calibrated to national conditions — but they leave an auditable data trace of each run of that logic. The next stretch of budget history will most likely be written on the twin tracks of "political principle + data technique".
