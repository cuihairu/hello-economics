# Chapter 6 · Government debt theory and management

> **The central question of this chapter**: why does the government borrow — what essential difference is there between borrowing and raising taxes, and between borrowing and issuing money? Is there a "red line" for the scale of government debt, and by what indicator is it measured? How do the market mechanisms from issuance to circulation run, and what do they mean for the financial system as a whole?

## The arc of this chapter

Modern government debt's qualification comes from an institutional revolution rather than from a technical invention. States have borrowed since ancient times (medieval Genoa and Venice already had the germ of public debt), but the national credit of "borrow and be able not to repay" is the product of the English "financial revolution" of the late seventeenth century: after the Glorious Revolution of 1688 Parliament controlled the budget, and in 1694 the Bank of England was founded to finance the war against France, so that government bonds for the first time had the double insurance of a "Parliamentary guarantee plus market circulation" — North and Weingast's classic study of 1989 attributes the sharp fall in Britain's borrowing costs over the following half-century to this mechanism of credible commitment, and the government bond market from then on became an amplifier of state capacity. Theoretical controversy then unfolded for more than two hundred years: Ricardo between 1817 and 1820 put forward an "equivalence proposition" — whether to tax first or to issue debt first (the debt being repaid by future taxes) is equivalent for the taxpayer, and the doctrine that public debt is harmless has nothing to do with him; in the Keynesian era public debt was redefined as a tool of macroeconomic stabilisation (internal debt being "merely what we owe ourselves"), and Buchanan's *The Public Finance of Public Debt* (1958) pointed out head-on that the taxpayers of the future are not "ourselves" but another generation, and that the real burden of debt is an intergenerational transfer — this controversy is still a living question of public finance (chapter 8 on fiscal deficits and Ricardian equivalence will meet it again). China's modern government debt history has three key nodes: the resumption of debt issuance in 1981 (after more than twenty years of "neither internal nor external debt"), the extra issuance of 1998 that opened proactive fiscal policy (in response to the Asian financial crisis), and after 2008 the local government bonds and the problem of implicit debt (dealt with at the end of this chapter). The structure of this chapter is therefore: the concepts section first sets up "how the scale is measured" (the limit of government debt) and "how the market runs" (the two markets of issuance and of circulation), and problems and analysis handle the three great functions, the triple burden and the function of the market — these three questions corresponding respectively to government debt's "use, cost and mechanism".

## Core concepts

### The limit of government debt

The limit of government debt is the maximum amount or the moderate scale of a country's debt, comprising mainly three aspects: the total accumulated stock of debt over the years; the total debt issued in the current year; and the total debt maturing in the current year requiring repayment of principal and interest. Controlling the total stock is an important link in preventing a debt crisis, while controlling the amount issued in the current year and the amount maturing for repayment is often more important. The scale of government debt is constrained by the subscribers' ability to bear it. The indicators for examining the scale of government debt are: first, the ratio of the current year's interest payments to the current year's central fiscal revenue (expressing the government's capacity to repay); second, the ratio of the current year's debt issue to central fiscal expenditure (the debt dependency ratio, which can indirectly express the capacity to repay). In addition, the direction, structure and efficiency of the use of government debt also affect its bearability, which is an endogenous factor.

Why is it hard to have one unified answer for the "moderate scale"? Because government debt is not ordinary corporate indebtedness — the government is not liquidated in bankruptcy, and it borrows against the future cash flow of its own taxing right, so the key variable is "repayment capacity" rather than "asset collateral". The shared logic of the two textbook indicators is to compare the flow of the debt (interest, issuance) with the flow of fiscal revenue or expenditure and so measure the finance's dependence on the debt; the more commonly used stock indicators are the debt balance as a share of GDP (the debt ratio) and the current year's repayment of principal and interest as a share of current revenue (the repayment ratio). The EU's Maastricht Treaty set the deficit ratio at 3% and the debt ratio at 60% as reference values for member states, and these have since been widely borrowed by countries (including in China's budget practice) as "safety lines" — but the two cracks in this line must immediately be added: the 60% was a political compromise at the European average level of the 1990s, not an economic derivation; and it is sensitive to growth, so the same debt ratio means something entirely different in a high-growth and a low-growth economy (the relation of r and g at the end of this chapter). China has managed central government debt on a balance basis since 2006 (the NPC approves an annual balance ceiling, and the finance ministry issues flexibly within the ceiling), and the debt balance as a share of GDP has long been below most developed economies (recently about a quarter, per the latest releases), but the figure is noticeably higher when local government explicit debt and implicit debt are observed together — the real-world version of "the limit of government debt" is therefore "whole-calibre government debt management". Beyond the indicators, the textbook's last sentence is what the seasoned fiscal expert really cares about: the use to which the debt is put decides whether it is a burden — borrowing to build a high-speed rail line whose returns cover its costs, and borrowing to fund recurring expenditure, are two wholly different debts.

### The primary market for government debt

The primary market for government debt is the place where government bonds are issued, also called the primary or first market in government debt, and it is the initial link of government debt trading. Transactions in the primary market are generally between the government and securities underwriting institutions (banks, financial institutions and securities brokers), and usually the underwriting institution buys the entire issue at once.

The core question of the primary market is not "where to sell" but "how to price" — the government's cost of financing is directly tied to the competitiveness of the pricing mechanism. Both historical evolution and institutional choice revolve around this point: in the early period countries relied on syndicate underwriting (the syndicate negotiating the price and covering the unsold portion), and after the 1990s marketised auction became the mainstream — and the dispute between the Dutch auction (a single price, with those bidding lower winning at the same price) and the American auction (multiple prices, each bidder pricing and closing at its own) is itself a body of learning (the US Treasury ran a comparative experiment in the 1990s and returned to the single price, on the grounds that under multiple prices bidders bid conservatively because of the "winner's curse", which actually raises the cost of financing). China's issuance system evolved along the same path: syndicate underwriting was trialled in 1991, full auction issuance began in 1996, and the system of primary dealers in government bonds was established; as for instruments, book-entry bonds (issued by marketised auction, tradable on a market) and savings bonds (certificated or electronic, for individuals, not tradable) are distinguished. The political economy of the primary market is also not to be ignored: it is at the same time the infrastructure for the central bank's open market operations — without a deep primary market, the central bank's buying and selling of government bonds (open market operations) would lack sufficient eligible instruments; in 2024 the People's Bank of China began buying and selling government bonds in the secondary market to fill out its policy toolkit, and its precondition is still the continuous supply of standardised bonds from the issuance market. This is also why countries treat primary-market discipline (the primary dealer system, market-maker obligations) as part of the financial infrastructure.

### The secondary market for government debt

The secondary market for government debt, also called the second market in government debt, is the market in which issued bonds are bought, sold, transferred and circulated. Government debt traders entering the secondary market for spot transactions must go through securities dealing institutions; transactions comprise both those between underwriting institutions and subscribers and those among bond holders themselves, and also those between holders and the government (repos). It divides into exchange transactions and over-the-counter transactions: transactions carried out in the trading hall of a designated exchange are exchange transactions, and the rest are over-the-counter.

The function of the secondary market is often underrated as merely "convenient for resale"; in fact it is the pricing hub of the whole financial system. The first layer of function is liquidity itself: only when holders can cash in at any time without a discount can government bonds be allocated by banks, funds and insurance companies as a "quasi-money" asset — liquidity turns the bond from an IOU into the core collateral of the financial system (collateral in the repo market, margin in derivatives). The second layer is the pricing benchmark: the continuously traded government bond yields of each maturity form a yield curve, which is the starting point of pricing for all fixed-income assets — loans, corporate bonds and mortgage rates are all built on government bond yields plus a risk premium; China's 10-year government bond yield has been included since 2016 in the reference basket of rates for the SDR, and the international recognition of the government bond yield curve as "the anchor of the risk-free rate" follows from this. The third layer is the policy channel: the central bank's open market operations and the finance ministry's cross-period adjustment of financing costs (the timing of refinancing the old with the new) both depend on the depth of the secondary market. China's secondary market structure has one feature differing from that of most countries: the interbank bond market (established in 1997, an over-the-counter market among institutions) accounts for an overwhelming majority of the transaction volume, with the exchange market second — this pattern arises from commercial banks being the largest holders of government bonds, and it has given rise to the long-running discussion of "market segmentation and poor interest rate transmission" (the long persistence of cross-market spreads on the same bond being its manifestation).

## Problems and analysis

### What use is government debt for? — three functions and their boundaries

The functions of government debt comprise three aspects:

- **Making up the fiscal deficit**. This is the main motive for the emergence of government debt, and it is the common practice of modern states. Making up a fiscal deficit by issuing government bonds generally does not affect economic development and has smaller side effects, because: issuing bonds is only a temporary transfer of the right to use part of social funds, and generally does not cause inflation; the subscription of bonds follows the principle of voluntariness and draws on funds that are largely idle and floating in the movement of social funds. But this function cannot be absolutised: an excessively large fiscal deficit piles up a mountain of debt and ultimately leads to a vicious cycle of fiscal revenue and expenditure; social idle funds are limited, and if the state concentrates too much it erodes the necessary funds of economic subjects, lowering the levels of investment and consumption.
- **Raising construction funds**. When the government issues public debt with a definite purpose and use, the funds go to economic construction; even debt issued to make up a deficit without a specific prescribed use may still be productive in itself — fiscal deficits are often caused by over-expenditure on capital spending.
- **Regulating the economy**. Government debt is a redistribution of GDP, reflecting the reallocation of social resources: funds used for investment or for consumption change the proportion between social accumulation and consumption; and the scale of government debt issuance itself has a macroeconomic stabilising effect.

Each of the three functions has a modern footnote that the textbook did not put in the table. The counterpoint for "making up the deficit" is the other two ways of financing a deficit: overdraft at the central bank or issuing money (direct inflation — China's 1994 Budget Law explicitly forbade the central bank from directly subscribing to or underwriting government bonds, and the 2009 amendment continued this) and temporary tax increases (high political cost, long lags, and running counter to the stabilisation principle of taxation precisely in a recession) — government debt became the first choice precisely because it is voluntary, marketised and tradable in the secondary market, converting immediate fiscal pressure into a cross-period financing arrangement. "Raising construction funds" has its most literal application history in China: special construction bonds from 1987, extra long-term construction bonds in 1998 for infrastructure investment, 1 trillion yuan of anti-epidemic special bonds in 2020, and ultra-long special bonds from 2024 supporting major national strategies (scale and use per official documents) — the tradition of "project bonds" with a definite use gives China's government debt a relatively clear ledger for its "endogenous factor" (the efficiency of use). "Regulating the economy" flips government debt from a passive financing tool into an active policy tool: issue more in a recession and expand expenditure (proactive fiscal policy), compress in an overheating, and the counter-cyclical swing of the debt scale itself becomes part of demand management; commercial banks' allocation of government bonds further makes debt the junction between monetary and fiscal policy — the dispute over "the monetisation of the fiscal deficit" (detailed in chapter 8) takes place at exactly this junction.

### Who bears the burden of government debt? — three viewpoints and a theoretical controversy

The burden of government debt can be analysed from three aspects:

- **The creditor's burden**. Subscribing to government bonds is a transfer of the subscriber's right to use income, temporary though it is, and it will affect their economic behaviour — so the issue of government debt must take account of the subscribers' actual ability to bear it.
- **The debtor's burden**. Government borrowing is repayable, and principal and interest must be repaid at maturity; the borrowing obtained an economic gain, while the repayment appears as an expenditure, so the process of borrowing is the process in which the debt burden forms — the government must consider its repayment capacity and act within its means.
- **The taxpayer's burden**. Whatever the direction and efficiency of the use of the debt, the source of revenue for repayment is ultimately taxation: when the government can no longer keep refinancing the old with the new, principal and interest are finally repaid by taxes.

The ranking of the three viewpoints is itself the answer to the question — the lightest burden falls on the creditors (voluntary transaction, with interest compensation), the heaviest on the taxpayers (passive, cross-generational, and under the "refinancing the old with the new" pattern deferred into a future too remote to notice). Written as two sides of a theoretical controversy it is clearer: the Keynesian tradition says internal debt is "what we owe ourselves", the debt's interest is only an internal transfer payment, and the real burden lies only in external debt; Buchanan's public-choice critique is head-on — the phrase "ourselves" conceals the sequence: those who benefit when the debt is issued are the present generation (enjoying more public services, paying less tax), while those who suffer at repayment are future taxpayers, and these are not the same group, so the real economic meaning of government debt is transferring the cost of public expenditure to the next generation. Modern public finance has given this controversy an adjudicating quantification with two tools: first, "generational accounting" (Kotlikoff and others), which discounts all future taxes and transfers by age group and computes directly the difference in net tax burden borne by different generations; and second, the empirical test of Ricardian equivalence (Barro theorised it in 1974: rational people save the money saved by a tax cut to hedge the future tax burden, so debt and taxes become equivalent) — country data support "partial equivalence": households do indeed respond to an expected tax burden, but households under liquidity constraints do not, so debt financing still has an expansionary effect in the short run and still constitutes an intergenerational burden in the long run. External debt is a separate matter: repaying principal and interest to non-residents is a real outflow of resources, and the debt crises of Argentina and Greece are both cases of external debt constraint.

### What use is the government debt market for? — more than the government's funding channel

The government debt market generally has two functions:

- **Realising the issuance and redemption of government debt**. The state can complete the tasks of issuance and redemption in the government debt market by way of fixed-return sales and by public auction.
- **Regulating the movement of social funds**. The direct and indirect transactions among the underwriting institutions, subscribers, holders and securities brokers are all processes of redistributing social funds, meeting the supply and demand for funds and making the allocation more reasonable; when the government participates directly in government debt trading (selling or repurchasing bonds at a certain price), it can guide the flow of funds and enliven the securities market.

The "government participating directly" in the second function is the key to understanding modern macrofinance. When the government (through the central bank) buys government bonds in the secondary market, it injects base money into the banking system and pushes down long-term rates — this is the prototype of the quantitative easing mechanism: the several rounds of QE by the Fed after 2008 and the Bank of Japan's large-scale purchases of government bonds pushed the sentence "the government debt market is the hub of fund allocation" to an extreme form; conversely, government selling or non-renewal at maturity is a tightening of liquidity. The government debt market thereby becomes the docking point of fiscal and monetary policy: the finance ministry decides how much debt to issue (the fiscal stance), the central bank decides how much to buy (the monetary stance), and together they decide the shape of the yield curve — the joint effect of the US Treasury's issuance structure and the Fed's balance sheet reduction on long-term rates in the 2020s is the contemporary case of this docking point. The textbook's last sentence adds the reality for developing countries: the government debt market is often also the "infrastructure" of the whole securities market — the risk-free rate, repo collateral, the custody and settlement system for bonds are usually established first in the government debt market and only then spill over into corporate bonds and equities; China's interbank custody and settlement system (the Central Depository & Clearing Company and the Shanghai Clearing House) grew along exactly this path.

## Limitations and further work

Government debt theory was reopened under three new conditions.

First, **the relative size of the interest rate and the growth rate (r against g) became the core parameter of debt sustainability**. The condition Dornbusch gave in 1944 — for the debt ratio to be stable, the growth rate must not be below the interest rate — was long taken to mean "growth faster than interest", because developed economies saw a secular decline in rates from the 1980s; Blanchard's presidential address of 2019 therefore put forward a counter-intuitive judgement: when r is below g for long, rolling the debt (refinancing the old with the new) may be close to a "free lunch" in generational-accounting terms, and the fiscal cost of government debt is over-estimated. After 2022 the major central banks hiked aggressively, r again approached and even exceeded g, and the premise of the "free lunch" wavered, with interest payments as a share of fiscal revenue rising quickly in the United States and Europe (US federal interest outlays having already surpassed military spending, around 2024, per the latest releases) — the optimistic window for debt sustainability is a product of the interest rate environment, not an inevitability of institutions.

Second, **local debt and implicit debt became China's main battlefield**. Central government debt itself is sound, and the problem is concentrated in the local governments: the new Budget Law of 2015 permitted local borrowing (making it explicit, with ceiling management), but the financing-vehicle debt accumulated before that and new forms of implicit guarantees kept expanding, and from the late 2010s the centre has continuously worked at resolving it (swap bonds, the "lifetime accountability" of the 2017 financial work conference, the 2023 package for debt resolution and special refinancing bonds). The theoretical points agree with this chapter: the problem has never been the "debt" itself, but "whose debt, at what rate, and is there a matching cash flow" — making implicit debt explicit, pressing the cost down, and matching use with assets is the direct application of the textbook framework.

Third, **the challenge of modern monetary theory (MMT)**. MMT, emerging at the end of the 2010s, argues that for sovereign currency states the debt constraint is not the budget but inflation — as long as inflation is controllable, the government can and should use "central bank purchase of government bonds" to finance full employment. Mainstream economics' rebuttal is concentrated on two points: the inflation constraint is precisely the least certain constraint (the global inflation of 2021–2023 gave this rebuttal empirical backing), and outsourcing fiscal discipline to the central bank's judgement erodes central bank independence (a core concern in the traditional positions of the Bundesbank and the ECB). The corresponding discussion in Chinese scholarship is "the monetisation of the fiscal deficit" (the debate during the 2020 epidemic: fiscal scholar Liu Shangxi proposed that the central bank directly subscribe to special bonds, opposed by the monetary authorities and by most economists), and its result continued the isolation principle established by the 1995 Central Bank Law — the central bank may not directly subscribe to or underwrite government bonds, and may only buy and sell them in the secondary market. This controversy has no winner, but it asked the old questions of Ricardo, Keynes and Buchanan again in new terms: what is the relation between the government's debt capacity and its monetary capacity, who supervises it, and where is the line. This chapter's three indicators for the "limit of government debt" remain the starting point one cannot go round when answering them.
