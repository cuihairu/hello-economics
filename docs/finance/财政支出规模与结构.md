# Chapter 2 · The scale and structure of fiscal expenditure

> **The central question of this chapter**: where does the government's ever-growing spending come from, and where does it go? Why has fiscal expenditure kept rising in almost every market economy? Government spending has no bottom line of profit — so by what measure do we judge whether an outlay was worth it?

## The arc of this chapter

Fiscal expenditure has two dimensions of observation — scale (its share of total output) and structure (the distribution of money across uses) — and this chapter answers one overall question for each. The first observer of the question of scale was the German economist Wagner: watching Germany industrialise in the second half of the 19th century, he noticed that the higher per-capita income rose, the more the relative scale of fiscal expenditure expanded, and around 1883 he put this forward as the "law of the expanding activity of the state", later known as Wagner's law. That law stimulated more than a century of empirical research — over the 20th century the government spending share of GDP in the main developed economies rose from a general level below 10% to around 40%, confirming Wagner in direction — but the explanation of "why it grows" unfolded into four competing theories: demand-side social evolution (Wagner), crisis-driven ratcheting (Peacock and Wiseman, 1961, who proposed the displacement effect from British data for 1890–1955), the succession of development stages (Musgrave and Rostow), and the supply-side bureaucrat's budget impulse (Niskanen, 1971). The question of structure ties directly to the functions of the state: the expenditure structure is the ledger projection of what the state does, and China's shift from "dominated by economic construction spending" toward a rising share of social, cultural and educational spending and administrative spending is itself a record of the migration of the government's functional centre of gravity. The third question — benefit — comes from a fundamental difference between public and private spending: a firm's spending is judged by the single yardstick of profit, the government's is not. This question was taken seriously as early as 1844: the French engineer Dupuit, evaluating bridge projects, argued that the benefit of a public work should be measured by the sum of what users would be willing to pay, not by the tolls actually collected — the origin of the idea of consumer surplus and the starting point of cost-benefit analysis; afterwards the minimum-cost method and public pricing were developed to handle, respectively, the cases where "benefit cannot be monetised" and where "benefit shows up in the price". The three threads are arranged in this chapter as follows: the concepts section first sets up the structural foundation of the economic classification of expenditure (purchasing versus transfer), then moves to the three benefit-evaluation tools; problems and analysis and the synthetic essay handle the classification and the theories of scale growth; the debate over "will the scale keep growing" is left to limitations and further work at the end of the chapter.

## Core concepts

### Purchasing expenditure

Classified by economic character, fiscal expenditure divides into purchasing expenditure and transfer expenditure. Purchasing expenditure is the government's outlay on goods and services, covering purchases needed for day-to-day administration and for state investment; it embodies the government's market-based redistribution activity. The larger the share of purchasing expenditure, the more direct the impact of fiscal activity on production and employment, and the greater the volume of resources allocated through the budget; fiscal activity dominated by purchasing expenditure carries out the resource-allocation function more strongly.

This classification is the foundation of the whole chapter because it translates the function theory of chapter 1 into line items of spending: purchasing expenditure (the workers hired to build a road, the steel bought, the salaries of civil servants, the equipment procured) corresponds to the resource-allocation function — like a market participant, the government spends money to obtain goods and services, directly consuming and mobilising social resources; transfer expenditure (pensions, subsidies, interest) corresponds to the income-distribution function — the government takes in with one hand and gives out with the other, consuming no resources itself. The macro implications of the two differ: purchasing expenditure enters aggregate demand directly and pulls production and employment — it is the "accelerator" of fiscal policy; transfer expenditure only changes whose wallet the money sits in, and its aggregate-demand effect must pass through the intermediate link of the beneficiaries' propensity to consume (a well-known paradox in economics holds that the immediate demand pull of a transfer can actually be weaker than that of an equal amount of purchasing expenditure — Keynes half-joked that burying banknotes in abandoned mines and hiring people to dig them up would still create employment, and this transmission chain is exactly what he had in mind). To read the "character" of a country's finances, look first at the ratio of the two: China at the peak of infrastructure construction, or a wartime economy with soaring military budgets, is overwhelmingly purchasing expenditure; welfare-state Sweden, or an ageing society with heavy pension outlays, is more than half transfer expenditure. The long-run movement of this share is therefore ledger evidence for judging the evolution of the government's role.

### Transfer expenditure

Transfer expenditure is the gratuitous, unilateral transfer of government funds — mainly subsidies, donations and interest on debt — embodying the government's non-market redistribution activity. The larger its share, the more direct fiscal activity's impact on income distribution; fiscal activity dominated by transfers carries out the income-distribution function more strongly.

Why must transfer expenditure be "gratuitous and unilateral"? Because what it buys is not a commodity but a change in the pattern of income itself. That gives it two analytical properties wholly unlike purchasing expenditure. First, its effect on income distribution is direct and computable: lay out the beneficiary distribution of pensions, minimum-living allowances and the various subsidies, and you have the itemised account of the fiscal "robbing the rich to aid the poor" effect — which is why the drop in the Gini coefficient before and after taxes and transfers is significantly larger in high-transfer welfare states. Second, its economic effects are contested: subsidies change behaviour — a price subsidy depresses the subsidised good's relative price and can induce over-consumption or arbitrage (the historical grain-and-oil subsidies that turned into ration coupons; more recently, farm-machinery purchase subsidies in some regions that triggered "subsidy farming" with paper transactions), and the interaction of transfers and taxes can also alter labour supply (the "dependency trap" debate in high-welfare countries). So the evaluation of transfer expenditure never asks "how much was spent" but "did it reach the target group, and did it change behaviour it should not have changed" — precisely why it is harder to evaluate than purchasing expenditure, and the theme chapter 3 develops for social security spending. Debt interest is the most easily overlooked transfer: it buys nothing at all, being purely the distributional obligation that past deficits left to the present, and its scale rises with interest rates and the debt stock — US federal interest outlays have surpassed the military budget (around 2024, subject to the latest release), which is the entry point for seeing how the government debt problem (chapter 6) and the fiscal balance problem (chapter 8) interlock.

### The government procurement system

The government procurement system is a control regime under which the central government, local governments and other entities prescribed by law purchase goods, works or services from society in legally prescribed ways, with supervision and management over the procurement process. It is marked by openness, impartiality and competitiveness, of which fair competition is the cornerstone. The system covers the drafting and approval of procurement plans, the management and supervision of procurement procedures, and the audit and inspection of procurement results.

The impetus for raising "the government buying things" into a statutory system comes from the agency problem discussed in chapter 1: the procurement official spends taxpayers' money; buying dear or buying wrong costs them nothing directly, while profit may be had from suppliers — hence collusion, kickbacks and "the connected bidder wins". The system's answer is to replace competition in connections with competition in procedure: open tendering places the procurement decision among suppliers' competing bids, and who supplies at lower price and higher quality is decided by rules rather than by favour. Its institutional history is long — Britain's Office of Works, set up in 1782 to centralise government office purchasing, is the starting point of the modern procurement system; the United States issued unified armed services procurement regulations in 1949; the pivotal international rule was the GATT Tokyo Round's Agreement on Government Procurement (1979), which brought the field under international trade discipline. China piloted the system in Shenzhen and elsewhere in 1996, passed the Government Procurement Law in 2002 (in force 1 January 2003), and in 2007 formally applied to join the WTO Agreement on Government Procurement, with negotiations continuing. The mechanism by which the system raises efficiency can be seen on three levels: for the finance department it hardens the budget constraint and lowers transaction costs; for the procuring entity, competitive bidding forces best-of-the-best; and for the relationship between procuring entity and suppliers, open competition refashions the old "collusive game" into a "prisoner's dilemma game" — under one-shot or weakly repeated open tendering, reporting a rival's deal with an official becomes the dominant strategy, and the cost of sustaining corruption shoots up. Of course the game-theoretic warning applies too: wherever the supplier group is stable enough and the game repeated often enough, collusion can re-form (bid-rigging and companion bidding are its forms) — which is why countries keep amending the law (China's 2014 amendment introduced competitive consultation and strengthened e-procurement).

### Cost-benefit analysis

Cost-benefit analysis is a method for government investment decisions: for a construction goal the government has set, propose several alternative schemes, enumerate in detail all expected costs and all expected benefits of each, and choose the optimal government investment project through comparison. It contains two processes: the government defines the candidate projects and schemes; and the schemes and projects are then selected. The key to applying it is how to determine a project's benefits, costs and discount rate; the general principle is to choose the project with the largest net social benefit for a given outlay. It is used mostly to evaluate government investment projects.

The provenance of this method is entirely unlike the private investor's ledger, and worth spelling out. A firm's arithmetic need only compare cash flows, because prices already tag everything with social value; the trouble with public works is that most of their output never passes through a market — the time a bridge saves, the losses a flood embankment avoids, have no ready-made price. Dupuit's 1844 paper "On the measurement of the utility of public works" was written precisely to solve this evaluation problem: he argued that benefit should be measured by the maximum users "would be willing to pay", even if the government charges nothing — the gap between willingness to pay and actual payment (consumer surplus) is the work's social benefit. The idea was institutionalised in the 20th century: the US Flood Control Act of 1936 required that a federal water project be undertaken only if "benefits exceed costs", and cost-benefit analysis became a statutory procedure, spreading after the war to transport, water, health and environmental projects. Two joints of the method decide the credibility of its results. One is the boundary of benefits and costs: whether spillover benefits count, whether side-effects count as costs — where that line is drawn often decides a project's life or death. The other is the discount rate: for projects spanning decades, distant benefits are extremely sensitive to discounting — the economics of climate change pushed this to the limit: the Stern 2006 report used a low discount rate to conclude for immediate large-scale emission cuts, while Nordhaus, using a market-based rate, derived a far gentler path; the divergence between the two calculations lies not in the data but in the choice of an ethical parameter. The preconditions of cost-benefit analysis are: benefits can be monetised, costs can be enumerated, and the discount rate can be defended; if any one fails, the conclusion becomes "gut feeling with decimal points".

### The minimum-cost method

The minimum-cost method generally does not measure the social benefit of candidate spending projects in monetary terms; it computes only the tangible cost of each candidate and takes the lowest cost as the criterion of choice. In application one first draws up several alternatives, computes and ranks their tangible costs, and leaves the choice to the decision-maker. The method suits the expenditure evaluation of public institutions.

This method was designed for the dead zone of cost-benefit analysis: compulsory education, the defence command system, epidemic control — benefits that either never enter a market or whose monetisation immediately provokes ethical protest ("what is a human life worth?"). Since no credible number can be produced for benefit, step back, fix the premise that "the effect must be identical", and compare only on cost: two staffing arrangements for the same teaching goal, two vaccination schemes for the same epidemic-control goal — take the cheaper. Two rigid boundaries follow for its scope: the effect must be homogeneous or the difference negligible (comparing a bridge with a tunnel, whose effects differ enormously, by cost alone is self-deception); and the difference in benefit must truly be impossible to monetise (if benefits can in fact be valued, bypassing them throws away information). In practice its commonest form is the "goal-means" checklist: a political process or a superior department fixes the goal and the quality standard, then professionals compress the cost with engineering and operations-research methods — like-for-like price comparison in military procurement, like-effect scheme comparison in public health, coverage-cost computation for school placement, all run on this logic. Together with cost-benefit analysis it covers the main scenarios of government project evaluation: priceable projects take the former, unpriceable but comparable-effect projects the latter; where neither applies (say, basic human-rights guarantees), evaluation can only resort to political procedure and social consensus — not a failure of the analytical toolkit, but a reminder that the final arbiter of fiscal decisions was always public choice.

### Public pricing

Public pricing is the government's setting of prices for goods and services produced by public enterprises, or its regulation of private-sector pricing. It has two aspects: pure public pricing, where the government directly sets prices in natural-monopoly industries; and regulated pricing (price controls), where the government sets prices in competitive regulated industries such as finance, agriculture, education and health. Its aim is not only to raise the allocative efficiency of society's resources overall, but above all to see that these goods and services are used most effectively and that the efficiency of fiscal expenditure rises. Public pricing includes average-cost pricing, two-part pricing and load pricing.

Why can water, electricity grids and gas not escape government pricing? Because they are natural monopolies: the fixed cost of laying pipe and wire is enormous and the marginal cost tiny; two companies duplicating networks is economically absurd, so the market has a single supplier, and monopoly pricing without competitive discipline would push prices far above cost — the price mechanism does not fail here for "lack of signals" but because "the signals can be manipulated". Government pricing therefore substitutes for competition, and the pricing formula is itself a set of economic trade-offs. Price at marginal cost (theoretically the most efficient) and a firm whose marginal cost lies below average cost necessarily runs a loss, filled by fiscal subsidy — the efficiency goal collides head-on with the fiscal burden. Average-cost pricing (price = average cost, the firm breaks even) gives up part of the efficiency in exchange for self-financing. Two-part pricing (a fixed monthly charge plus a volume charge) is the masterstroke of compromise: the fixed fee recovers fixed costs, the volume price near marginal cost recovers variable costs, combining recovery with efficiency — widely adopted for telephone, water and gas; but it has a distributional side-effect, the fixed fee being regressive for low-income households, and many cities accordingly waive or reduce the basic charge for welfare recipients. Load pricing handles the time structure of demand: the grid's peak-hour marginal cost is met by expensive peaking plants, so peak-off-peak differentials push part of demand into the trough, equivalent to avoided investment (China rolled out residential tiered electricity pricing nationwide from 2012, with commercial peak-valley pricing earlier — both applications of load pricing). The practical difficulty of public pricing is information: to set a price the government must know the firm's true cost, and cost information sits precisely in the hands of the priced firm — the literature on regulatory capture (Stigler's classic 1971 paper deriving regulation from industry demand) reminds us that every "reasonable cost" presented at a pricing hearing is the outcome of interested parties contending inside the procedure.

## Problems and analysis

### Where does the government's money go? — Classification by state function and the change it records

Classification by expenditure category is classification by the function of the state. The state's functions comprise economic administration and social administration, and fiscal expenditure accordingly divides into economic-administration spending and social-administration spending. Following this division of state functions, China has traditionally grouped fiscal expenditure into five categories: economic construction spending; social, cultural and educational spending; national defence spending; administrative management spending; and other spending.

The value of classifying by state function is that it is the government's "ledger self-portrait": a time-series analysis of one country's expenditure structure reveals the evolution of its functional centre of gravity; a cross-country comparison of expenditure structures at the same period reveals the differences between states' functions. In China, economic construction spending was long the largest item; since reform and opening its share has kept falling while the shares of social-cultural-educational and administrative spending have risen — behind the numbers is the functional shift of "the government withdrawing from producer back to provider of public goods" (the mechanism of this shift recurs in chapter 8 on fiscal balance, in the context of the 1994 tax-sharing reform). One definitional note: in 2007 China reformed its government receipts and expenditures classification to a new system running "functional classification" alongside "economic classification"; the old five categories have since served mainly historical comparison. When reading the statistical yearbooks, mind the joint between the old and new classifications, or the time series will show a break.

### Why does government procurement both save money and restrain corruption? — The benefit mechanism on three levels

The government procurement system selects suppliers mainly through open tendering and bidding, buying goods or services for government departments and affiliated bodies from domestic and foreign markets; it is marked by openness, impartiality and competitiveness, with fair competition as its cornerstone. It raises the efficiency of fiscal expenditure on three levels:

- From the finance department's own standpoint: it strengthens expenditure management and hardens the budget constraint, lowering transaction costs in an open, fair and just competitive environment, and raising the efficiency of the use of fiscal funds.
- From the procuring entity's standpoint (the agent of the government department): competitive bidding for the best-of-the-best means the entity will economise as far as possible and raise the quality of the goods, works and services bought.
- From the standpoint of the relationship between the finance department's agents and suppliers: tendering and bidding converts the "collusive game" between the two sides into a "prisoner's dilemma game", sharply reducing conspiracy and corruption, and avoiding the outcome where the suppliers and the procuring entity are the biggest winners and the state the biggest loser.

The third level deserves the most development, because it is game theory applied directly to institutional design. The premise of a corrupt transaction is that both parties can lock in a common interest free from the threat of third-party denunciation; open tendering changes the payoff structure — the more suppliers in the bidding, the lower a single supplier's gain from conspiring with an official and the higher the risk of being reported by a rival. The institution's "anti-conspiracy" power rests not on moral exhortation but on making denunciation the dominant strategy. This also explains the institution's boundary: where suppliers are few, the industry closed, and dealings repeated, collusive tendering (bid-rigging, companion bidding) still returns, which is why expanding the supplier pool, electronic audit trails and random drawing of evaluation experts have become a continuing arms race. The conclusion across the three levels: government procurement raises not the efficiency of some step in the process but the game structure of the entire "spending public money" link.

## Synthetic essay

### Problems of China's fiscal expenditure structure and directions for adjustment

**(1) The current state.** The expenditure structure is the share of each category in total expenditure (the composition of spending); it bears directly on the degree to which the government mobilises social resources, and its influence on the operation of a market economy may exceed that of the scale of spending. The current state and movement of a country's expenditure structure show what the government's priority functions are and where they are heading. Government functions reduce to two broad classes, economic administration and social administration, with spending forming economic-administration expenditure and social-administration expenditure correspondingly. With the establishment of the socialist market economy, the economic-administration function gradually weakened and the social-administration function strengthened, and the share of economic construction spending kept declining. Two causes drove its decline: falling working-capital outlays and falling capital-construction outlays. Within the growth of social-administration spending, reasonable and unreasonable elements coexist: the growth of social-cultural-educational spending is reasonable; the growth of administrative management spending is not entirely so; and within administrative spending the structure has shifted unreasonably — personal salaries growing faster than operating expenses.

**(2) Adjustment.** Whether an expenditure structure is reasonable can be judged from at least two aspects: the stage of economic development the country occupies and the main policy goals the government pursues at that stage; and the relative growth rates among the spending items. China is in the primary stage of socialism, with economic growth the principal goal; from the fiscal standpoint the main policy instrument for sustainable growth is fiscal investment — yet while the scale of fiscal investment has grown, its share has kept falling, the share of consumption spending is excessive and grows far faster than production spending, and this will suppress growth. The route of optimisation advances on both fronts: define the scope of spending rationally by the priority order of social public needs; and, in line with current economic policy goals, gradually straighten the ratio of production spending to consumption spending within incremental outlays. The adjustment focuses on restraining excessively fast growth of consumption spending, the main measures being, on the premise of a clear definition of government functions, streamlining institutions, controlling the personnel of administrative organs, and cutting consumption expenditure.

The skeleton of this essay — "function determines structure, structure is evidence of function" — has stood the test of time better than its concrete policy advice. Indeed each of its diagnoses got a response in later reform: the 2007 receipts-and-expenditures classification reform made "economic classification" able to monitor personnel costs and operating costs separately; from 2012 central departments published the "three public consumptions", exposing administrative consumption spending to public oversight for the first time; the institutional reforms from 2013 with the "reduce only, never increase" staffing pledge correspond directly to the prescription of "streamline institutions, control personnel"; and the 2018 Opinions on the full implementation of budget performance management turned "benefit" from an essay topic into a hard constraint inside the budget process. What this passage leaves behind that outlasts its conclusions is a method: any judgment about expenditure structure must first ask "what stage is this country at, and what is the government's task at this moment", and then ask "do the relative growth rates of the items match the task" — discussing shares without the stage, or numbers without function, is idle motion.

### Why does fiscal expenditure spend more and more? — Four theoretical explanations

The growth of fiscal expenditure looks like a law of economic development in market economies. The share of GDP taken by fiscal expenditure in socialist countries trended upward before reform and fell during the reform period — because the government was trying hard to escape over-packaging and over-centralisation; once the economic system moves onto the market track and reaches a certain stage, the decline should stop and turn upward again. Theoretical explanations of the growth trend fall mainly into four kinds.

**(1) The expanding state-activity theory (Wagner's law).** As per-capita income rises, the relative scale of fiscal expenditure rises with it. Wagner held that modern industrial development produces social progress, social progress necessarily enlarges state activity, and he sorted the growth factors into political and economic: the political factor is that industrialisation complicates market relations, increasing the need for commercial law and contracts, requiring judicial and public-security organisations and more resources for legal infrastructure; the economic factor is that industrialisation drives urbanisation, and dense populations generate congestion and other externalities that call for government management and regulation.

**(2) The gradient-increment theory (the inner-and-outer factors theory).** The British economists Peacock and Wiseman distinguished inner and outer factors of public expenditure growth, holding the outer factor the main reason spending outpaces GDP. In normal conditions economic development raises incomes, tax receipts at unchanged rates rise with them, and government spending grows near-linearly with GDP (the inner factor); once an external shock such as war arrives, the government is forced to raise tax rates and the public accepts it, tolerance rising in crisis, while public spending "displaces" private spending and its share leaps; after the crisis spending does not fall back — postwar there is always a mass of government debt to service, and the spending level stays long on a new step.

**(3) The development-stage theory.** Musgrave and Rostow explained expenditure growth by development stage: in the early stage government investment takes a high share of total investment, providing the social infrastructure of roads, transport, sanitation, legal order and human capital; in the middle stage government investment continues but only as a supplement to private investment; once the economy matures, public spending shifts from infrastructure to education, health and welfare services, and this part grows much faster than other spending and than GDP, swelling the scale of expenditure.

**(4) The bureaucratic-behaviour theory.** Niskanen kept the rational-economic-man assumption and argued that bureaucrats aim to maximise the size of their agency — the bigger the agency, the bigger the power — so fiscal expenditure keeps expanding, even beyond the level needed for the optimal output of public goods. Bureaucracies typically monopolise public services and hold specialised information, enabling them to persuade politicians that the social return of their output level is high, thereby maximising the budget; and because transaction costs are high, the appropriating bodies find it hard to control bureaucratic behaviour. Bureaucracies expand the budget in two ways: persuading the government that their chosen output level is necessary; and using inefficient production techniques to raise the inputs needed for a given output (bigger budgets, fringe benefits, job security, lighter workloads). Bureaucratic behaviour thus inflates the scale of spending from both the output and the input ends.

The four theories in fact explain different objects, and only read as complements rather than rivals do they become usable: Wagner answers "why does it rise in the long run" (demand expands with development); Peacock and Wiseman answer "why does it rise in steps" (crisis lifts the public's tax tolerance and the spending baseline onto a step and locks them there); the stage theory answers "why the content of the rise changes" (from rails to dams to pensions); Niskanen answers "why the supply side fans the flames" (every department has an incentive to grow). The data mark out each theory's territory: the rise of OECD government spending from about 10% of GDP to around 40% over the 20th century confirms Wagner's long-run direction; the leap in spending during the two world wars and its failure to fall back after are the standard cases of the displacement effect; the rising share of health and pension spending in ageing societies is the stage theory's contemporary edition; and the fact that after the 1980s the spending share stopped rising and even fell in some countries shows that bureaucratic-expansion theory explains only supply-side pressure, with the true landing point still decided by taxpayer-side constraints — each theory governs one stretch, which is exactly why the public finance textbook keeps them all.

## Limitations and further work

The theories of expenditure growth and their policy discussion have left several debates still without a verdict.

The debate over scale turned in the opposite direction in the 1980s. Prodded by stagflation and high tax burdens, the supply-side and public-choice schools drove a worldwide "austerity experiment": British privatisation, American tax cuts, the reconstruction of New Zealand's government departments (the New Public Management movement) — the government spending share stopped rising in several countries, state enterprises withdrew on a large scale, and contracting out of public services became normal. Evaluating these experiments became a research field in itself: privatisation generally improved performance in competitive industries, while in natural monopolies the regulatory cage had to be built before the enterprise was let out; the managerial assessment introduced by New Public Management has been shown by research since the 2000s to induce gaming of indicators and displacement of goals. The later consensus leans toward "the size of government is not itself the problem; the quality of spending and the mode of financing are" — the Nordic countries sustain the highest levels of government trust and innovation at spending shares near 50%, showing that behind the correlation Wagner's law describes, institutional quality is the key moderating variable.

Rules-based constraint is the most durable legacy of this debate. The 1992 Maastricht Treaty set the euro area reference values of 3% deficit and 60% debt; Germany wrote a "debt brake" into its Basic Law in 2009; Switzerland and Chile introduced structural-balance rules; China revised the Budget Law in 2014 and put local debt under quota management (detailed in chapter 8) — fiscal expenditure shifted from "determined by demand and the political process" to "framed by rules", an institutional counter to the Peacock-Wiseman ratchet: since post-crisis spending will not come down, install the limiter on the step before the crisis.

The boundary of benefit-evaluation methods is also being redrawn. Cost-benefit analysis has no final answer to the ethical dispute over "how to price life, health, ecology" (the fairness protests over the 9/11 compensation fund converting awards by victims' income; the dispute over the climate discount rate are both cases), but the method keeps extending: health economics uses the quality-adjusted life year (QALY) to give medical interventions a common measure of output; environmental economics uses shadow prices to tag carbon emissions and ecological services; more important, randomised controlled trials have converted evaluation from "forecast before" to "verify after", giving the performance of fiscal expenditure for the first time an evidentiary standard isomorphic to medicine's. For China this line has landed inside the institutions of budget performance management: "money spent must be asked for results; no results, accountability" has been written into central documents since 2018, so the three evaluation tools of this chapter are no longer study-room methods but paperwork that must be produced inside the budget process.
