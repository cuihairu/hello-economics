# Chapter 5 · Tax systems

> **The central question of this chapter**: why does a country's tax system look the way it does — which taxes are collected, and which class of tax is the main body? Is the income tax levied 'classified' or 'comprehensively', and what is the difference? On what ground did the VAT become, in a few decades, the most important tax in the world? And in the 1994 Chinese tax reform — what was changed, and why was the change unavoidable?

## The arc of this chapter

The tax system is the concretisation of the principles of taxation (the previous chapter's principles and optimal tax theory), and "concretisation" must answer one strategic question: within the large family of taxes, who becomes the main body. This question has received different historical answers in countries at different income levels: industrialised countries generally make the income tax dominant (the income tax generally accounts for over half of total tax revenue), while developing countries generally make the commodity tax dominant — the reasons are unfolded in this chapter's section on "the determinants of the tax structure", with the one-sentence advance warning that "the income tax needs administrative capacity and a credit system, while the commodity tax can be collected by catching the circulation stage". China's 1994 tax reform was the outbreak of this general question at a particular point in time: the background on the eve of the reform was the continuing slide of the "two shares" — fiscal revenue's share of GDP falling from about 31% in 1978 to about 12.6% in 1993, and central fiscal revenue's share of the national total at only about 22% (per official releases) — so the fiscal system could neither support the public functions nor support macroeconomic regulation. After the Fourteenth CPC Congress established the goal of a socialist market economy in 1992, the 1994 reform rolled out three things at once — a circulation-tax system centred on the VAT, the unified domestic and foreign-capital income taxes and the unified individual income tax (the unification of the foreign-related tax system), and the shared-revenue fiscal management system that is the yin and yang of the tax system (central taxes, local taxes and shared taxes set apart, with the national and local tax bureaus as two separate agencies). The content of this chapter is arranged as "overall — elements — cases": first the two overall concepts of tax-system types and of tax structure (including the single tax system, which exists only in theory), then the three modes of levying the income tax (classified, comprehensive, classified-comprehensive — China's 2018 individual income tax reform is precisely the case of moving from "classified" to "classified-comprehensive"), then a separate dissection of the VAT, the most important invention of twentieth-century tax technology (France, 1954, by Maurice Lauré), and finally the 1994 reform as the closing — it built the skeletal structure of the tax system that has run for the following thirty years, and its subsequent deepening (the 2008 unification of the domestic and foreign-capital corporate income taxes, the 2016 completion of business-tax-to-VAT, the 2018 comprehensive reform of the individual income tax, and the legislation for each tax under the principle of statutory taxation) is placed in "Limitations and further work" at the end of the chapter.

## Core concepts

### Tax-system types

Tax-system types are the forms of tax system produced by classifying the tax system by certain standards. By the standard of the overall design of the tax system, they divide into the single tax system and the composite tax system: where only one kind of tax is levied within a given tax jurisdiction, it is the single tax system, and where two or more kinds of tax are levied simultaneously, it is the composite tax system. The single tax system exists only in theory; all countries of the world generally adopt the composite tax system.

The single tax system has had serious versions in the history of theory: the Physiocrats advocated a single land tax (in their eyes only land produces "net product", and all tax burdens ultimately fall on land — Henry George's single tax on land values in *Progress and Poverty*, 1879, is the American version of this line of thought), and afterwards designs for a single income tax and a single consumption tax also appeared. Their shared theoretical motive was simplicity and neutrality (one tax, no repetition, no distortion), and their shared cause of failure was the same: no single tax base can cover all sources of revenue, so a single tax system either collects too little or presses an excessive burden on a single stage and thereby creates distortion and unfairness — which is exactly another statement of chapter 4's conclusion that the composite system's direct and indirect taxes complement each other. One terminological confusion should be avoided: the "single tax system" (levying only one kind of tax) and the "single tax" of the 1980s and after (the flat tax — Hall and Rabushka's 1985 single-rate income tax proposal, first adopted by Estonia in 1994 and by Russia in 2001 with a 13% single-rate personal income tax) are not the same thing — the latter concerns the structure of rates, the former the number of tax kinds; flat-tax countries still have a composite tax system. China's reality is a composite system: taxes on goods and services (the VAT, the consumption tax), income taxes, property and behavioural taxes, and taxes of specific purposes exist side by side, and the tax net complements itself according to the logic of "universal collection plus specific adjustment".

### Tax-system structure and tax-system pattern

The tax structure is the overall layout and general structure of a country's tax system; it is the coordinated, mutually supplementing whole system, with primary and secondary parts, composed of tax classes, tax kinds, tax-system elements and the levels of collection and management, formed by the state according to the economic conditions and development requirements of the time under a given tax system. The question of tax structure is the fundamental strategic question of tax design, and a sound tax structure is the primary precondition for the fulfilment of taxation's functions, determining the scope and the depth of taxation's effects. The tax pattern is which class of tax serves as the main tax kind within a country's tax structure; the main tax kind determines the overall function of the tax system.

It is truer to understand "structure" as a tax net: the size of the mesh (how broad the base), the thickness of the cord (how high the rate) and the position of the main line (the main tax kind) together decide what this net can catch and what it lets slip. The reason the main tax kind is the "overall function" switch is that it decides from whom taxation mainly collects and at what behavioural cost: an income-tax-dominated system means the tax is mainly levied on "earned income", and its progressive structure makes it bear the redistribution function naturally; a commodity-tax-dominated system means the tax is mainly levied on "consumption spending", with broad coverage and easy collection but regressivity. Long-run World Bank and IMF statistics show the systematic divergence of main tax kinds between developed and developing countries, and the explanatory variables are precisely the five that follow. China's indirect-tax structure dominated by the VAT since 1994 remains so after business-tax-to-VAT from 2013 (the VAT plus the consumption tax accounting for about forty percent of tax revenue, the individual income tax for about eight to nine percent, per the latest releases) — this structure is rational in terms of "efficiency and administrative feasibility", and the criticism that its "redistributive function is weak" arises from it as well; the debate over the comprehensive reform of the individual income tax and over legislation for a real estate tax is essentially all a debate about "whether the main structure should tilt toward direct taxes".

### The classified income tax

A classified income tax divides the taxpayer's various taxable incomes into several classes, and incomes of different classes (or of different sources) are subject to different rates and levied separately. For instance, dividing taxable income into salaries and wages, service fees, interest income, property rental income, business income and so on, prescribing different rates for each and levying by rate. It generally uses proportional rates and is collected by the method of withholding at source; its advantages are the simplicity of collection, the easy control of the tax source, and effective prevention of tax evasion, and its disadvantage is that it cannot levy by the principle of ability to pay. This tax system was first created in Britain (the schedules of the income tax of 1803), but countries practising a purely classified income tax are now few.

The classified income tax was born of the constraints of administrative technology: Britain at the beginning of the nineteenth century had neither a national declaration system nor linked bank accounts, and could only "catch the source" — withholding by the payer (withholding at source), one schedule and one rate per class of income, at the lowest administrative cost. Its fairness defect can be put with a single sharp sentence: the object of taxation by ability is "the person's total capacity to pay", while the object of the classified tax is "each item of income" — with the same annual income of 100,000 yuan, a person living entirely on wages and a person on 40,000 of wages plus 60,000 of rent pay entirely different amounts, while the latter's total income is the same or even more comfortable (property income costs no labour). The world history of the classified tax is a history of moving toward the comprehensive system: Britain itself progressively comprehensively reformed over the following two hundred years, and countries that today retain a purely classified system are few. China's individual income tax before the 2018 amendment was for a long time a typical classified system (eleven taxable items, each with its own rate and expense deduction), and the background of its reform is set out in this chapter's "Modes of levying the income tax" and in the further work at the end of the chapter.

### The comprehensive income tax

A comprehensive income tax collects the taxpayer's various taxable incomes (wages, interest, dividends, property income and so on) comprehensively, generally uses progressive rates, and is collected by the declaration method. Its advantage is that it can tax by ability and equalise the burden; but it requires taxpayers with strong tax awareness and a high degree of compliance, and a collecting authority with advanced administrative means and high working efficiency, otherwise tax evasion and avoidance are likely to arise. The comprehensive income tax appeared first in Germany (the Prussian tax reform of the late nineteenth century) and is now generally adopted by countries of the world.

The core mechanism of the comprehensive system is to take the "year" as the unit of taxation: all incomes are aggregated, deductions applied uniformly, and payment made by declaration at progressive rates — only aggregation makes the true capacity to pay comparable, and only declaration makes progression possible. These two requirements tie its feasibility to two social conditions: the development of the credit and payment system (income leaves a trace, while cash transactions escape the net) and the digitised capacity of tax administration (cross-checking of declarations). The comprehensive declaration system that developed in the United States after the Sixteenth Amendment to the Constitution established the federal income tax in 1913 (withholding plus an annual settlement) is the most complete twentieth-century form of this institution; and its costs are well known — extremely high compliance costs (the average US taxpayer spends a number of tens of hours a year on filing returns) coexisting with room for evasion. The trade-off between the comprehensive and the classified system is therefore never an abstract choice between "fair and unfair", but an engineering balance between "the gain in fairness" and "administrative cost and compliance risk" — which is precisely why China's reform did not jump to a purely comprehensive system in one step but adopted the transitional scheme of "combining comprehensiveness with classification".

### Tax reform

Tax reform is the process of advancing social welfare through marginal changes in tax design and in the tax structure. It has many forms: changes in tax rates, in tax brackets, in the threshold for levy or in the amount exempted, and changes in the tax base; the introduction of new taxes and the abolition of old ones; changes in the combination of taxes.

The words most worth chewing in this definition are "marginal" and "process". Marginal means that reform rarely means starting from scratch — the slogan "broad base, low rate" of the US Tax Reform Act of 1986 (the Reagan era pressing the top rate from 50% down to 28% while cancelling a great many preferences to widen the base) became the world model precisely because it was a marginal adjustment on top of the existing structure rather than a new stove; process means that reform has path dependence, and the skeleton laid down by China's 1994 tax reform is still having layers added on top of it to this day (see the synthetic essay and the further work at the end of the chapter). Why does the tax system need continuous reform? Because all three of its foundations are moving: the economic structure moves (the rise of the services share makes the old taxes based on industry invalid — the direct motive for business-tax-to-VAT), the distributional pattern moves (widening income gaps raise the importance of progressive instruments), and technology moves (the digital economy's capacity to shift profits to low-tax places forced the OECD/G20 "two-pillar" international tax reform of 2021). Judging whether a reform "advances social welfare" ultimately returns to chapter 4's standard: the net of the efficiency loss (excess burden) against the gain in fairness.

## Problems and analysis

### What determines a country's tax structure? — five factors and a convergence line

The main factors determining the tax structure can be summarised as the level of economic development, the capacity of collection and administration, the structure of fiscal expenditure, the goals of tax policy, and the demonstration effect of neighbouring countries:

- **Level of economic development**. The higher the per-capita income, the higher the shares of income tax and of social security tax revenue in total tax revenue — which largely explains why industrialised countries make the income tax dominant and most developing countries make the commodity tax dominant.
- **Capacity of collection and administration**. The more complete the accounting system, the more advanced the administrative means and the higher the degree of honest compliance, the higher the share of income tax; one important reason for the high share of commodity taxes in developing countries is that commodity taxation is easier to administer than income taxation.
- **Structure of fiscal expenditure**. Where taxes are earmarked, if a certain expenditure is large and takes a high share, the share of the revenue of the tax financing it rises too — the most typical case being the correspondence between social security tax and social security expenditure.
- **Goals of tax policy**. Indirect taxes favour economic growth more than income taxes (they dampen savings and investment less), and income taxes favour fair distribution more than indirect taxes — developing countries that make growth the first goal generally make indirect taxes dominant, and developed countries that make fairness the focus generally make the income tax and the social security tax dominant.
- **The demonstration effect of neighbouring countries**. The tax structure is influenced by surrounding countries, and this effect works through the movement of capital, labour and goods between neighbouring countries — rate competition and the imitation of tax systems are both its expressions.

Taken together, the five factors give an explanatory framework that reduces the "tax structure" from a question of preference to a question of constraint: making the income tax dominant requires three conditions to hold at once — per-capita income high enough (there is something to tax), a collection and credit system strong enough (it can be collected), and the fairness goal taking precedence enough (it is worth paying the administrative cost for); making the commodity tax dominant is then the rational choice under constraint — the circulation stage is the only tax base in a developing country that is stably observable and capable of withholding. And the trend line must also be seen: as countries develop and tax administration digitises, tax structures show convergence — the shares of goods and services taxes are rising in developed countries (European countries joining the VAT club, the GST being rolled out in several Asian countries), and the income tax share is slowly rising in developing countries. The convergence is not a coincidence but the effect of the hardest of the five variables (the level of economic development).

### In what way is the income tax levied? — classified, comprehensive, and classified-comprehensive

The income tax is the general name for taxes levied on income as the taxable object, and the modes of levying divide into three:

- **The classified income tax**: various taxable incomes are divided into several classes, incomes of different classes are subject to different rates and levied separately, generally with proportional rates and withholding at source. Its advantages are simplicity of collection, easy control of the tax source and prevention of evasion; its disadvantage is that it cannot levy by the principle of ability to pay. First created in Britain.
- **The comprehensive income tax**: various taxable incomes are collected comprehensively, generally with progressive rates and the declaration method. Its advantages are taxation by ability and equalisation of the burden; its disadvantage is that it demands a high level of tax awareness, compliance and administrative means, otherwise evasion is likely to arise. First appeared in Germany.
- **The classified-comprehensive income tax**: a concurrent system combining the two — first levied by classification, then the part of the individual's total annual income above the prescribed amount is aggregated comprehensively and taxed at progressive rates. It can embody taxation by ability while keeping collection simple.

The three modes are three marks on a "administrative cost — precision of fairness" spectrum: the classified system at the most frugal end (the institutional legacy of Britain in 1803), the comprehensive system at the highest precision (the American annual-return system), and the classified-comprehensive system in the middle. The change in China's position is itself a reform history: from the imposition of the individual income tax in 1980 the classified model was practised; the 2018 amendment merged the four labour items — salaries and wages, service fees, author's remuneration and royalties — into "comprehensive income", assessed annually and subject to the 3%–45% excess-progressive schedule, while the remaining items (interest and dividends, property rental, property transfer, incidental income and so on) are still levied separately — this is precisely the Chinese form of the classified-comprehensive system. The intention in choosing those four rather than all incomes is exact: the taxpayers of labour income are mainly wage-earners, whose income is most easily traced through withholding at source (the administrative risk of comprehensiveness is smallest), and only after comprehensiveness can the expense deductions (the special additional deductions) and the progressive rates be applied per "person" rather than per "item" — the part with the greatest gain in fairness is exactly the part with the least administrative cost, and the order of the reform's choice follows from this.

### Why did the VAT become the largest tax? — five advantages and one shared principle

The VAT is the tax levied on the value added within the value of a commodity, and it is the most important tax in China's present system. Its advantages are:

- it **avoids double taxation** in the repeated levying on a single commodity;
- it is **levied at every stage**, with each firm's newly created value as the base, so that affiliated firms supervise one another in tax payment, reducing or eliminating evasion;
- the levy adapts to the stages of commodity circulation, but **the amount of tax is unaffected by the number of stages**;
- the **mergers and divisions of firms do not affect the VAT amount**, guaranteeing the stability of revenue;
- **a zero rate can be applied to exports**, with all taxes already paid domestically refunded in one instalment, encouraging an outward-looking economy more than an ordinary turnover tax.

The five advantages share one technical principle: taxation by the credit method of "output tax less input tax", swapping the base from "the whole amount of turnover" to "the value added". Traditional turnover taxes (the business tax, the turnover tax) levy the whole amount at every stage, and the finer the division of labour, the heavier the double taxation — the same commodity produced by one general-purpose factory and by three specialised factories bear entirely different burdens, so taxation conversely punishes the division of labour and rewards the "all-in-one, both large and small" firm, which is exactly the economic reason it was finally eliminated. The credit method's two by-products are especially ingenious: the first is "self-enforcement" (Casanegra's famous line was that the VAT is almost "automatically collected") — the buyer, in order to claim a credit, must demand a lawful invoice from the seller, so every firm on the supply chain is a supervisor of its upstream, with the flow of invoices and the flow of money locked to each other, and fraud requires the collusion of the whole chain, so the cost rises steeply; the second is the thorough export refund under the "destination principle" — a zero rate at the export stage means the commodity enters the international market at a price excluding tax, without violating WTO rules, which is essentially different from export subsidies. The VAT was designed and implemented by the French tax official Maurice Lauré in 1954, and today some 170 economies use it (including those running it in the form of a goods and services tax), the speed of adoption being without precedent in tax history. China imported it as a pilot in 1979, formally legislated and trialled it in 1984, made it the largest tax in 1994, turned it from production-type to consumption-type in 2009 (allowing a credit for the input tax on equipment), and in business-tax-to-VAT from 2012 to 2016 brought construction, real estate, finance and consumer services all under it — the completion of "broad base, full chain" makes it the work closest in reality to chapter 4's principle of "tax neutrality".

## Synthetic essay

### The 1994 tax reform: background, principles and content

Tax reform is the process of advancing social welfare through marginal changes in tax design and in the tax structure. The overall guiding thought of the 1994 tax reform was: unify the tax law, equalise the tax burden, simplify the tax system, rationalise the division of power, straighten out distribution relations, and establish a tax system meeting the requirements of the socialist market economy.

**(1) Basic principles**

- Strengthen the macroeconomic regulatory function of taxation: adjust the layout of taxes, set reasonable rates, straighten out the distribution relations among the state, firms and individuals, and raise moderately the share of fiscal revenue in GDP; divide central taxes and local taxes reasonably, creating the conditions for straightening out the distribution relation between centre and locality.
- Equalise the tax burden and promote fair competition: unify the corporate income tax, perfect the circulation taxes, change the practice of setting tax kinds and rates according to different forms of ownership and to different regions, so that the tax burdens of firms of all kinds are roughly equal.
- Give play to taxation's regulatory effect on personal income and on regional development, keeping the distribution of personal income at reasonable gaps, and promoting coordinated regional development and the realisation of common prosperity.
- Implement the state's industrial policy, favouring a reasonable adjustment of the economic structure, and promoting the sustained, rapid and healthy development of the national economy.
- Simplify and standardise the tax system: abolish taxes that no longer fit economic development, merge duplicated taxes, and impose necessary new taxes.
- Keep the overall level of the tax burden unchanged: neither increasing firms' overall burden nor reducing the state's fiscal revenue — but structural changes in the interests of localities, departments, firms and individuals are unavoidable, and tax reform is itself an adjustment of interest relations and of the distribution pattern.
- Strictly control preferences and exemptions: beyond the exemptions and reductions listed in the tax law and the tax regulations, no tax may be reduced or exempted at will in execution.

**(2) Main content**

- Centred on rolling out a standardised VAT, set up the consumption tax and the business tax correspondingly, and establish a new circulation-tax system; stop levying the old unified industrial and commercial tax on foreign-capital firms and apply the new circulation-tax system to them uniformly.
- Impose a unified corporate income tax on domestic-capital firms, abolishing the old taxes on state-owned enterprise income, the state-owned enterprise adjustment tax, the collective enterprise income tax and the private enterprise income tax; state-owned enterprises no longer practise the contracted fixed-contribution system for income tax.
- Unify the individual income tax: abolish the old personal income adjustment tax and the urban-and-rural individual business-households income tax, and apply the amended Individual Income Tax Law uniformly to personal income and to the operating income of individual business households.
- Adjust, merge and abolish other taxes: adjust the resource tax, the urban maintenance and construction tax and the urban land use tax; abolish the market transaction tax, the livestock transaction tax, the special tax on oil burning, the bonus tax and the wage adjustment tax; impose the land appreciation tax; merge the salt tax into the resource tax.

The deeper background of this reform deserves to be read under the heading "why unify the tax law". Before the reform the tax system was a patchwork cut by ownership and by institutional identity (state-owned, collective, private and foreign-capital each had its own set), and the differences in rates were themselves differences in policy — which conflicted directly with the "equal competition" that a market economy requires; more urgent was the unsustainability of finance: under the contracted system central fiscal revenue was eroded by negotiation with the localities, and the "two shares" slid year after year to an untenable point. The 1994 package was therefore "tax system plus regime": unification and simplification on the tax side, and the shared-revenue system on the institutional side (paired with the division of tax kinds, with the national and local tax bureaus set apart as two agencies, and the centre directly holding 75% of the VAT and all of the consumption tax — choosing the VAT, the largest tax, as the main shared tax is precisely the institutional point here). The effects of the reform are plain in the statistical curves of the following decade: the share of fiscal revenue in GDP stopped falling and recovered, the centre's share rose to about 55% (per official releases), and macroeconomic regulation thereby got a financial lever; and the single rule of "strictly controlling preferences" ended the local tax competition of racing to grant exemptions to attract investment. The problems that emerged in operation (the dual track of domestic and foreign-capital corporate income taxes, the production-type VAT's dampening of investment, the business tax's double taxation, the insufficient progression of the classified individual income tax) also set out the list of subsequent reforms — the 2008 unification of the domestic and foreign-capital corporate income taxes (25%), the 2009 VAT transformation, the 2016 full completion of business-tax-to-VAT, the 2018 comprehensive reform of the individual income tax — all of them continuations of the same reform.

## Limitations and further work

The thirty-year continuation of the 1994 framework and its new frontier can be read as "three unfinished things".

The first unfinished thing is **the share of direct taxes and redistribution**. China's present tax system has limited overall progressivity: the high share of indirect taxes such as the VAT gives it a generally regressive tendency, the individual income tax covers a narrow population (the proportion of people paying it is limited), and the property tax is absent (the legislation for a real estate tax and the extension of the pilot have been under discussion for years; in 2021 the NPC Standing Committee authorised the State Council to carry out a real estate tax reform pilot in certain regions for a term of five years, though the actual pace of launch slowed — per official documents). The common-prosperity agenda (the tenth meeting of the Central Financial and Economic Affairs Commission, 2021) put "raising the share of direct taxes" back on the agenda, and the questions of the inheritance tax and the gift tax that arose in the discussion of the third distribution have entered the public view with it — the direction is clear (broaden the base, lower the turnover taxes, raise the direct taxes), and the pace is constrained by administrative capacity and by infrastructure such as asset registration.

The second unfinished thing is **statutory taxation and the legislation for each tax**. The 2015 amendment of the Legislation Law made explicit that "the establishment of tax kinds, the determination of tax rates and the administration and collection of taxes, and other basic tax systems" can only be established by law; before that, only 3 of the 18 tax kinds had a legal basis, and afterwards the environmental protection tax (2016), the tobacco leaf tax, the vessel tonnage tax, the vehicle purchase tax, the cultivated land occupation tax, the resource tax (2020), the deed tax, the urban maintenance and construction tax (2021) and the customs duty (2024) successively completed legislation, and the VAT Law (passed in 2024, in force from 2026) has taken the main taxes largely out of the "era of regulations". This is the institutional landing of chapter 4's principle of "fixity", and its meaning lies in predictability — for the taxpayer and for finance alike.

The third unfinished thing is **the digital economy and international taxation**. Digital giants can book profits wherever there is a network, and the traditional rule of "tax where there is physical presence" has failed: France in 2019 was first among the major economies to impose a digital services tax, and the OECD/G20 "two-pillar" plan (2021, with consensus among more than 140 jurisdictions) tries to rebuild the rules with "pillar one" (redistributing the right to tax a part of residual profits to market countries) and "pillar two" (a 15% global minimum corporate tax), while the pace of national legislation differs (per the latest developments). If this global agreement lands, it will be the largest reconstruction since the international tax rules of the 1920s — and its origin is, at a deep level, the same as the origin of China's 1994 tax reform: the tax system must keep up with the movement of the economic structure.
