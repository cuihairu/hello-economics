# Public finance

This group of subjects is not about reciting revenue and expenditure concepts; it answers one overarching question:

**Why a market economy still needs government finance, and how government accomplishes public governance through spending, taxation, debt and the budget.**

Compressed into a chain of questions, the whole course falls into roughly three layers:

1. Functions of public finance: why government exists at all, and why finance is legitimate.
2. Revenue and expenditure: how money is spent, how it is raised, and why borrowing is needed on top of that.
3. Fiscal constraints: how the budget, the deficit and sustainability keep finance in check.

## 1. The questions this group keeps coming back to

- Why public finance is still needed under a market economy.
- Why fiscal spending keeps expanding.
- How taxation embodies the balance between equity and efficiency.
- Why the state cannot rely on taxation alone and must also issue government debt.
- Why the budget and the deficit are the core of modern fiscal management.

## 2. How the theories were born and how they were tested

- **Pigouvian taxes and carbon pricing**: Pigou argued in 1920 that externalities could be corrected with a tax. That idea lay dormant for half a century before becoming reality at scale: the EU Emissions Trading System (from 2005) and the carbon taxes of individual countries are the largest experiments in "Pigouvian taxation" to date, and the US experience with sulfur-trading from the 1990 Clean Air Act Amendments was earlier still — observed emissions fell significantly, which is the theory's best evidence.
- **Public goods and free-rider experiments**: the non-excludability of a public good leads to under-supply. Public-goods games (Marwell & Ames 1981 and others) document free-riding behaviour systematically, but also find that nobody free-rides completely — people's conditional willingness to cooperate turns out to revise the pure-selfishness assumption.
- **Where the Laffer curve has its limits**: "tax rates too high collect less revenue" is valid in principle (both extreme tax rates collect nothing), but the supply-side prediction of the 1980s — that tax cuts would pay for themselves — was refuted by the path of the US deficit. The strong version fails; the weak version shows up in the data: people at the very top marginal rates (such as Sweden's wealthiest) genuinely relocate to avoid taxation. One curve, two halves of a different fate — a textbook case of "the theory being right does not make the policy right".
- **Debt burden and Ricardian equivalence**: should government borrow, or tax? Barro revived Ricardian equivalence in 1974 (debt is future taxation, and rational households will save against it). The empirical tests largely reject the strong form — consumption does respond to tax cuts and refunds — which means the assumption of intertemporal optimisation holds only approximately.
