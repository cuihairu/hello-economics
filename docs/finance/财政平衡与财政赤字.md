# Chapter 8 · Fiscal balance and fiscal deficit

> **The central question of this chapter**: is "the books must balance" an inherent virtue of sound fiscal stewardship, or an old dogma that macro goals can legitimately set aside? Under what conditions is a deficit harmless, and under what conditions fatal? Why does China's official deficit ratio stay perennially "around 3%" while the "broad deficit" estimated by international institutions is much larger?

## The arc of this chapter

A major rupture in the history of the idea of "fiscal balance" happened in 1936. For two centuries before, the classical consensus was that the government should live within its income like a household — Smith's argument against public debt (turning productive capital into unproductive spending) took balance as the norm, and annual balance was written into the constitutions and budget laws of many countries. The Great Depression smashed the creed: America, with unemployment at 25%, that kept chasing a balanced budget (it even raised taxes in 1932 to balance) could only deepen the slump. Keynes's *General Theory* of 1936 gave the opposite prescription — when the private appetite to save is too high, a government deficit is the necessary channel for mobilising idle resources; Lerner in 1943 pushed the logic to its extreme with "functional finance": the only criterion by which to judge government receipts and outlays is their effect on aggregate output, and balance is not a goal at all. But practice over the following decades swung the pendulum back to the middle: deficits are useful in a slump and dangerous in an overheating, so the conceptual tools emerged that reconciled Keynes with the classics — cyclical balance (making up losses with surpluses within one economic cycle), America's "full-employment budget" of the 1960s (revenue and expenditure estimated at potential output, isolating the structural deficit), and the EU's Maastricht Treaty of the 1990s writing 3% deficit and 60% debt into hard numbers (chapter 6 told its cracks). This chapter lays those theoretical tensions out in the concepts and problems sections and ends on a reading of Chinese practice: in the planned economy "balance of revenue and expenditure, with a slight surplus" was the iron rule of fiscal work (no deficit in most years 1950–1978); from 1979 deficits appeared year after year, the 1998 Asian financial crisis launched the first "proactive fiscal policy", 2008 brought the four-trillion package, 2020's pandemic lifted the deficit ratio to about 3.6%, and 2023's extra 1 trillion yuan of national bonds temporarily raised it to about 3.8% (all subject to official publication) — the deficit passed from "an unavoidable accident" to "a routine policy tool". At the same time, reading China's deficit numbers requires first understanding the calibre: the official deficit counts only the general public budget, central deficits are covered by national bonds and local ones by local general bonds, while the government-managed funds budget (special bonds among them), transfers-in and carry-overs are excluded from the deficit — so a low "deficit ratio" and a large "fiscal expansion" can coexist, which is precisely the calibre premise behind the synthetic essay's "factors constraining the scale of the deficit" today.

## Core concepts

### Fiscal balance

Fiscal balance means that state fiscal revenue and expenditure are roughly equal in amount. It has a static and a dynamic sense: static balance means achieving balance within one fiscal year; dynamic balance means offsetting the gains and losses of different fiscal years, surpluses against deficits, so that overall balance is reached over a longer period. The comparison of revenue and expenditure has three outcomes — revenue above expenditure gives a surplus, expenditure above revenue a deficit, and revenue equal to expenditure balance; in actual operation revenue and expenditure happen to be exactly equal almost never, so "balance" in practice can only mean "basically balanced or roughly balanced": a slight surplus and a slight deficit are both forms of balance.

Why is exact equality accidental and imbalance necessary? Because revenue and expenditure are two variables decided independently: revenue fluctuates with the economic cycle, prices and collection efficiency, while expenditure is set by legal commitments (social security, debt interest), project schedules and emergencies — two separately moving series being equal every period is near-impossible by chance. So the question that actually matters to policy is never "is it balanced?" but "what is the scale, direction and cause of the imbalance": cyclical imbalance (a slump cutting revenue) and structural imbalance (tax elasticity too low, or expenditure commitments beyond revenue capacity) mean entirely different things, the first self-corrects, the second rolls forward and accumulates into a debt problem (chapter 6's debt dynamics are the extension of this chapter's concepts). Understanding balance also needs a whole-economy view and a tiered view: fiscal balance is only a part of the overall balance of total supply and demand in the national economy, and must be assessed in the whole; the central and local budgets must be examined separately — in China for a long time the central budget ran a deficit (covered by national bonds) while the local budget by law could not show one (after the 2014 Budget Law revision, local general bonds became the lawful means of covering a local deficit), and the consolidated and the tiered pictures are not the same.

### Budget deficit

A budget deficit is the difference, appearing in the final accounts, in which the total planned expenditure of a fiscal year exceeds regular revenue. The concept carries two meanings: first, a deficit shows not only in the result of execution, the government leaves a gap deliberately and with a plan when it arranges the budget; second, such a planned deficit may be active — deliberately carrying out expansionary fiscal policy — or passive — expenditure is strongly rigid (social security, education and debt interest resist compression) and a gap is forced when revenue will not rise.

The institutional implication of the concept of a "planned deficit" deserves expansion: once the deficit is written into the budget and approved by the legislature, borrowing to cover it gains a lawful channel, and the size of the deficit itself becomes a political variable open to debate and to accountability — the deficit ratio in China's annual government budget report is exactly such an object of debate. Opposite to the budget deficit is the deficit that "runs into" during execution (over-collection falling short, or spending slipping out of control), which is the real signal of governance failure. Chinese budget practice has one further technical point: what fills the difference in the final accounts. The central deficit is covered by issuing national bonds, the local deficit by local general bonds, and both count inside the deficit calibre; whereas local special bonds go into the government-managed funds budget and are repaid from project revenue, not counted in the deficit — so "the actual expansionary force of fiscal policy" exceeds "the force the deficit ratio shows", which is the concrete shape of the calibre question raised at the head of this chapter. The analogous distinction internationally is between the aggregate deficit and the structural deficit (measured after stripping the economic cycle): a widening deficit in a slump year does not necessarily mean a turn to expansion, and if the structural deficit does not rise no "new" stimulus has been added — Germany's 2009 debt brake wrote "the structural deficit shall not exceed 0.35% of GDP" directly into the constitution, the strongest institutional form the concept has taken.

### Deficit dependency and deficit ratio

Two indicators measure the scale of a fiscal deficit. Deficit dependency is the deficit as a share of fiscal expenditure, showing what proportion of a country's total spending in a year was realised by relying on deficit spending; the deficit ratio is the deficit as a share of GDP, showing what share of social resources a country mobilised by deficit in a given year.

The two indicators divide the risk view in two. Deficit dependency is "the finance's own view": it measures the degree to which expenditure depends on borrowing — the higher the dependency, the more sensitive the fiscal system to the bond market (a rise in rates, a cold issue would cut the funding chain of expenditure straight through), and China's central government debt dependency in the late 1990s ran high at one point (a good part of central spending was met by bonds), which was exactly the real motive behind further deepening of the tax-sharing system and raising the central share of revenue. The deficit ratio is "the national economy's view": it weighs the deficit against the whole economy, and only against growth, the saving rate and interest rates does it mean anything — the same deficit ratio is absorbed easily in a high-growth, high-saving economy and rolls into a debt crisis in a stagnant one. The deficit ratio is the common language of international comparison (the Maastricht 3% reference line is set on it), but the calibre must be unified before comparing: some countries include the social insurance fund in the deficit, others do not; China's official deficit excludes special bonds and quasi-fiscal activity, and the "extended deficit" estimated on the IMF's calibre is significantly higher (the figures change year by year, subject to the IMF's fiscal monitor for the year). The way to use both indicators is to multiply them and check: deficit ratio = deficit dependency × expenditure as a share of GDP, and any oddity in either indicator is worth asking what happened to numerator and denominator.

## Problems and analysis

### Why can fiscal balance not be understood absolutely?

The four angles from which to open this out:

**(1) The goal is "broad balance", not exact equality.** As in the concepts section, revenue and expenditure being exactly equal is accidental, and a slight surplus and a slight deficit are both forms of balance; deliberately chasing exact year-by-year balance is in fact harmful — taxing harder and cutting spending to patch balance in a year of falling revenue magnifies economic fluctuation (the lesson of America in the 1930s), which is the first loosening of the balance view.

**(2) A dynamic view is needed; do not stop at the static.** Static balance requires each year to stand on its own; dynamic balance allows making up losses with gains and settling accounts over several years. Its institutionalised form is the "cyclical budget": allow deficits in a slump, make them up with surpluses in a boom, and be roughly balanced within a cycle. It fits the facts of economic fluctuation better than annual balance, and is more disciplined than perpetual deficit — but execution has a famous political-economy problem: expanding deficits in a slump is easy, accumulating surpluses in a boom is hard (politicians are always glad to spend the money in good years), and Germany's debt brake and Switzerland's cyclical adjustment rule are disciplinary patches against exactly this "clockwise malfunction".

**(3) A whole-economy view is needed; do not treat finance as finance alone.** Fiscal balance has no intrinsic value; its value depends on its contribution to the balance of total supply and demand in the national economy — opened out in the next question.

**(4) It can be examined by tiers.** The central and local budgets being balanced separately and each answering for itself is the bookkeeping basis of a tiered fiscal system (chapter 7's "seek its own balance"); meanwhile the overall balance on a consolidated calibre is the entry point of macro analysis — both sets of books have to be readable.

### What is the relation of fiscal balance to the balance of total social supply and demand?

Three sentences can summarise it, each worth expansion:

**(1) Fiscal balance is part of the balance of total supply and demand.** The national income identity (expenditure approach) decomposes GDP into consumption, investment, government purchases and net exports, and fiscal revenue and expenditure enter this identity through the government's saving gap: when private saving exceeds investment (S > I), a government gap or an external gap must absorb it, or output falls. The same identity gives a famous corollary — the "twin deficits hypothesis": in the 1980s America's fiscal deficit widened alongside its current account deficit, the mechanism being that once the government gap absorbs domestic saving, the residual gap is filled by foreign saving (the current account deficit). Finance and foreign trade are therefore not two separate skins.

**(2) Fiscal balance is a means of realising the balance of total supply and demand, not the end.** The goal is a roughly balanced total supply and demand (price stability + full employment + external balance), and fiscal balance is only the local tool for reaching it — so "sacrificing" fiscal balance for the aggregate (deficits in a slump) is the rational choice, and sacrificing the aggregate for fiscal balance (astringency deepening the slump) is putting the cart before the horse. This is the core proposition of functional finance, and the deep meaning behind this chapter's title setting "balance" and "deficit" side by side.

**(3) Fiscal revenue and expenditure are the lever on total demand that the government holds in its own hand.** Consumption, saving, investment and external trade are millions of dispersed decisions realised by individuals and firms through the market, which the government cannot directly command; fiscal revenue and expenditure, by contrast, are the government's own acts — a deficit adds to total demand directly (the multiplier), a surplus compresses it directly. Finance is therefore the only demand-management instrument in macro control that is "held in the government's own hand", and its costs and limits (crowding out, lags, debt accumulation) are the theme of the synthetic essay.

### What are the main causes of fiscal imbalance?

Fiscal revenue and expenditure being exactly equal is accidental; imbalance is necessary. The causes fall into four classes:

**(1) External shocks.** International events that greatly affect the country's national income but that the country cannot influence: changes in import and export prices (imported shocks of the oil crisis type), changes in the cost of external debt (global rate hikes raising debt-service spending), and changes in foreign aid and external demand. They disturb both the revenue and the expenditure side, and no single country can guard against them — the shock of 2022's global inflation and major central bank rate hikes on the finances of all debtors is the nearest case.

**(2) An inelastic tax system.** A tax system whose revenue elasticity is below one is called inelastic: the economy grows while the revenue share of tax falls relatively, yet expenditure grows rigidly, and the gap widens year by year. China's fiscal contract system before 1994 is the textbook case (chapter 5's "two shares" in decline): localities contracted a fixed remittance base to the centre, much of the benefit of growth stayed with localities and enterprises, and the central share of GDP in revenue slid down year after year — the essence of the 1994 tax-sharing reform was a surgical operation to "raise tax elasticity", and the VAT invoice-deduction chain made tax growth outrun nominal GDP, so that the "two shares" halted and recovered after that. This cause therefore carries a methodological meaning: fiscal imbalance is often not the simple problem of "spent too much" or "collected too little" but the failure of the tax system's "sensor" for economic growth.

**(3) The operating condition of state-owned enterprises.** In the planned economy and the early reform, remitted profits of state-owned enterprises were the largest source of fiscal revenue, and their operation decided fiscal balance directly. But the change must be read accurately on the timeline: the "profit-to-tax" reform of 1983–1984 replaced remitted profit with income tax, and after the 1994 tax-sharing reform state-owned enterprises for a time paid tax only and remitted no profit to the budget (retained profits supporting their rescue), so the SOEs' effect on fiscal balance changed from "a revenue pillar" to "an implicit contingent liability" — losses and debts of SOEs (above all local financing vehicles) may ultimately be backstopped by the budget, and this form of risk came to concentrated expression in the expansion of local debt after 2008 (chapter 6). After the state-owned capital operations budget was restored in 2007, central SOE profits were remitted in proportion again (current rates graded by industry, subject to the latest policy), and the effect returned to being explicit and institutionalised.

**(4) Unexpected events.** Earthquakes, floods and droughts, public health emergencies bring less revenue and more expenditure, affecting that year's and later years' balance: post-disaster reconstruction after the 2008 Wenchuan earthquake, the tax and fee cuts and epidemic spending of the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic, are both typical. The institutional tools for responding are the contingency reserve mentioned in chapter 7 (1–3% of expenditure) and the budget adjustment procedure — what an unexpected event tests is not really "balance" but the elastic reserve of the budget institution.

A fifth cause should be added, absent from the textbook list but the largest source of contemporary fiscal imbalance: **a deliberate choice of counter-cyclical policy**. In the Keynesian frame, expanding the deficit in a slump is not finance "having a problem" but finance performing its stabilising function — and separating such deficits from the four classes of "passive imbalance" above is precisely the point of the structural deficit concept.

## Synthetic essay

### The socio-economic constraints on the scale of the fiscal deficit

A deficit cannot be as large as you like — it has a set of socio-economic constraints, one at a time:

**(1) The degree of rise in the growth rate.** If the economy grows rapidly for long, a fairly large deficit does little harm, because growth in national income brings strong absorbing capacity and prices are not easily pushed up. The strict statement behind this is the debt dynamics (Domar 1944, told in chapter 6): whether the debt ratio converges depends on the difference between the interest rate and the growth rate (r − g), and when growth persistently exceeds the interest rate the debt ratio falls on its own even with the deficit continuing — America after the war and contemporary high-growth China are both cases; once r > g becomes the norm (the age of low growth), the same deficit ratio is far more dangerous. So "how large a deficit is safe" has no constant answer; it is a function of the growth scenario.

**(2) The degree of growth of the monetised sector.** Developing countries have a large non-monetised sector (self-sufficient production, transactions in kind), and marketisation continually moves them into the money economy, a process that keeps absorbing the new money supply. McKinnon's 1973 financial deepening theory is directly at stake here, and China is the best case: in the early reform the growth of money supply far outran growth without producing hyperinflation, the "excess money" being absorbed by the process of monetisation (which partly explains the long climb in M2/GDP). Monetisation is a buffer that can be drawn on only once — after the process is done, the same monetised deficit turns straight into inflationary pressure.

**(3) The degree of under-use of capacity across the national economy's sectors.** When agriculture and industry hold large idle resources and capacity, deficit spending mobilises them, the added output offsets the expansion of demand and prices do not rise; when resources are already fully employed, a deficit only lifts prices. This is exactly the boundary of the Keynesian multiplier — the multiplier is large in a slump and approaches zero, even negative, in an overheating (complete crowding out). Judging whether the deficit should be expanded now begins with judging how far the economy stands from the production possibility frontier.

**(4) The nature of the investment projects financed by the deficit.** If deficit funds go to construction projects with short cycles and quick results, the added supply offsets the expansion of purchasing power after a short lag and inflationary pressure dissolves itself; if they go to purely consumption-type or long-cycle inefficient projects, demand comes first and supply lags far behind, and the pressure persists. This brings "the structure of the deficit" into the discussion: the same 3% deficit ratio, directed at infrastructure and human capital, or at inefficient subsidies, has entirely different macro consequences — and it is the real measure for judging the merit of a large stimulus such as the "four trillion" (not the size, but the quality of the projects).

**(5) The degree of the deficit in international trade.** When there is a balance-of-payments deficit, part of the added domestic purchasing power flows abroad (the import leak) and the inflationary pressure of the deficit is "leaked off"; a surplus country lacks this discharge channel and all the demand stays domestic. This is the two sides of the twin deficits hypothesis: in an open economy part of the effect of fiscal expansion must spill over through the external account, and the domestic cost of a deficit therefore depends on the exchange rate regime and the state of external balance.

**(6) The government's own managerial capacity.** A deficit, once it appears, may turn inflationary, and whether that can be controlled depends on whether the government has adequate and forceful managerial capacity: first, the capacity to borrow and to tax — if it can issue at low cost by market means and has a sustainable tax base, there is no need to reach for the printing press (in countries where financial deepening is shallow and bond markets absent, a deficit goes more easily to monetary financing, and this is the fiscal root of Latin American inflation and hyperinflation); second, the capacity to manage wages and prices — keeping the expansion of the deficit from becoming a wage–price spiral. The People's Bank Law of 1995, mentioned in chapter 6, which forbids the treasury to overdraw on the central bank, is the institution-building that cuts the "deficit → printing" channel off by law.

**(7) The public's willingness to bear sacrifice.** If deficit financing is to add real output there is a lag between mobilising resources and supplying products, and society must keep patient for a period long or short: restrain expectations of income and living standards that run too high, and even endure a temporary fall in real income. This is the softest yet the most fundamental — the political foundation of macro stability is public trust in and cooperation with policy, and a failure of expectation management (the public rushing to build expected inflation into wages and prices) will make any technical control of the deficit fail.

The conclusion the seven factors give together: the safe scale of a deficit has no universal constant; it is a joint function of the growth stage, the financial structure, the industrial structure, the degree of openness, institutional capacity and social consensus — and a number like 3% is only the common divisor of a particular time and place, so treating it as a physical constant (in Maastricht practice, or in applying China's numbers by rote) will misjudge.

## Limitations and further work

The boundary of this chapter's frame is being continually rewritten in three directions by contemporary research and contemporary Chinese practice.

First, **the r − g dispute has reopened the crack in the balance view**. The debt dynamics of the synthetic essay's item (1) was brought to the front by Blanchard in 2019 (AEA presidential address): when the safe rate persistently below growth (r < g) becomes a long-run fact of the advanced economies, the budget cost of "rolling debt over" is near zero and the intergenerational cost of a moderate deficit has been overestimated — which gave theoretical cover to the mass global fiscal expansion of the 2020 pandemic, and revived "functional finance" in its contemporary variant, MMT (modern monetary theory). But the criticism is just as sharp: r < g does not rule out a sudden jump in rates on the debt ratio path and a market repricing (a euro-crisis-style shock), and the claim that a sovereign currency "can always afford it" ignores the constraints of inflation and the exchange rate — MMT's record of coming unstuck in emerging markets (Argentina, Turkey) shows the institutional preconditions (central bank credibility, a deep debt market) cannot be dropped. The pendulum of the balance view has stopped in the middle: the boldness of the high-growth, low-rate era cannot be extrapolated into a norm.

Second, **the agenda of making China's deficit calibre transparent**. "An official deficit ratio around 3%" and "a wider gap in broad fiscal revenue and expenditure" have coexisted long (special bonds, policy financial instruments, the pro-cyclical gap of land finance, all outside the deficit), and have been misread often in international comparison. The direction of reform is clear: government comprehensive financial reporting (accrual basis, told in chapter 7) makes implicit liabilities explicit, "penetrating monitoring" of special bonds and debt-relief policy compress the real deficit outside the calibre, and the next step of the "whole-calibre budget" is to bring broad fiscal activity into a ledger open to scrutiny. In essence this process is the institutional landing of this chapter's concepts: only when the calibre is unified do indicators such as "deficit ratio" and "dependency" recover metrological meaning.

Third, **from a "balance view" to a "sustainability frame"**. Contemporary public finance has largely given up "annual/cyclical balance" as the operating goal, replacing it with debt sustainability analysis: given the interest–growth differential and a basic deficit path, the convergence conditions of the debt ratio are computed, supported by generational accounting (future social security and health commitments discounted, checking the intergenerational burden) and fiscal reaction functions (whether the basic balance improves automatically as debt rises). The corresponding Chinese practice is the cross-year budgetary balance mechanism, the medium- and long-term fiscal plan, and the cap management of local debt. Once this line of evolution is understood, the chapter's position is clear: static and dynamic balance, deficit indicators and constraints are the underlying parts of the analytical toolkit, while the upper-layer "objective function" has changed from "books balanced" to "debt sustainability plus macro stability under intertemporal trade-offs" — the grail of public finance has passed from "living within your income" to "keeping solvency and policy space through time".
