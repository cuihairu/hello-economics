# Chapter 7 International movement of factors

> **Core question of this chapter**: when goods cannot move freely, capital moves in their place — so what determines capital movement itself? Why do firms choose to open a plant abroad rather than export or licence their technology? Borrowing to develop the economy: how much is too much?

## Thread of the chapter

The theory of the preceding chapters all rests on the assumption that "factors cannot move across borders", and this chapter overturns it — and the first paper to overturn it revealed at the same time the entanglement of the two assumptions: Mundell in 1957 proved that under standard assumptions factor movements and goods movements are perfectly substitutable for each other (trade barriers obstruct the movement of goods, and capital moves behind the barrier to produce directly — "tariff-induced investment"); Markusen in 1983 then proved that when the two countries differ in technology or taxation, the two are instead complementary (capital movement creates trade). Whether substitution or complementarity obtains depends on where the disturbance comes from, and that boundary is still the basis on which the interaction of trade and investment policy is assessed. The organizational subject of capital movement is the multinational corporation, and the relay race of theories explaining its existence has been running for over half a century:Hymer in 1960 put forward the theory of monopoly advantage, holding that a firm operating in a foreign country bears additional costs and institutional risk, so a firm bold enough to go abroad must possess ownership advantages that can be transferred across borders (technology, brand, management); Buckley and Casson in 1976 carried Coase's 1937 theory of the firm to the international level with the internalization theory — when the market transaction costs of intermediate goods (especially of knowledge) are too high, a firm substitutes an internal hierarchy for an external market, and the multinational corporation is exactly "the internalized market"; Dunning's eclectic theory of international production (the OLI paradigm, 1977) synthesizes the two and adds location factors, becoming the most widely used analytical framework, and in 1981 he made it dynamic with the investment development cycle hypothesis (a country's net outward investment evolves in a U-shape with per capita income). Another line connected with development is external resource constraint: Chenery and Strout's two-gap model (1966) derived from the identities the inevitable "saving gap" and "foreign exchange gap" of developing countries, providing the theoretical basis for importing foreign capital; while the Latin American debt crisis of 1982 (Mexico being first to announce its inability to pay) demonstrated the cliff edge of external debt accumulation and gave birth to the external debt indicator system of the second half of this chapter — the World Bank switched to the present-value method from 1995, so that concessional and commercial loans can be compared on the same scale.

## Core concepts

### Ownership-specific advantage

Ownership-specific advantages are advantages a firm possesses in organizational and managerial capacity, financial and financing strength, technical features, and firm scale and monopoly position. These advantages make the firm stronger than the local companies of the host country, enough to overcome the additional costs and institutional risks encountered in producing abroad. It is one of the basic contents of the eclectic theory of international production, and together with internalization advantages and location-specific advantages the three jointly determine whether a firm has an advantage in outward investment, or whether it can exploit that advantage through export or technology transfer.

"Additional costs and institutional risk" is the key to understanding this concept: a firm operating in its home country need not learn the local law, need not do cross-cultural management, need not face the "outsider" discount on trust, while in the host country all of these must be paid — so the very existence of FDI is a puzzle (why not produce at home and export? Why not simply sell the technology?), and ownership-specific advantage is the first layer of the answer: the firm must hold proprietary assets that competitors cannot obtain and that can be used across borders. The second layer, internalization advantage, answers "why plant rather than sell technology": intangible assets such as technology are extremely hard to price, and once sold they are beyond control (imitation, leakage), so market transactions fail and the transaction has to be brought inside the firm (build the plant and use your own technology) in order to protect the rent — this is why knowledge-intensive firms generally choose FDI rather than licensing. The third layer is location-specific advantage: the host country's labour cost, market size, tariff barriers and government policy determine where "that internalized market" is placed. The decision matrix the three combine into is concise and powerful: ownership advantage alone → choose export; ownership plus internalization advantage → choose technology transfer or licensing; all three present → outward foreign direct investment. The cases are everywhere: TSMC's US plant — process technology (ownership advantage), preventing the spillover of frontier technology and controlling yield (internalization advantage), *CHIPS Act* subsidies and proximity to large customers (location advantage), all three in place at once; Chinese auto firms building plants in Europe in the 2020s is likewise a combination of technological accumulation, quality control and the location motive of getting around tariffs.

### Present-value debt ratio

The present-value debt ratio is the ratio of a debtor country's outstanding debt at present values in the current year to its gross national product in the current year (or to its exports). It has two expressions: the economic present-value debt ratio (outstanding debt at present values as a share of current-year GNP, critical value 80%) and the export present-value debt ratio (outstanding debt at present values as a share of current-year exports, critical value 20%); a country whose actual indicator exceeds 60% of the critical value is a moderately indebted country, and below that a lightly indebted one.

Replacing nominal values with "present values" is the greatest institutional improvement in this set of indicators, and its motive comes from the practice of development financing in the 1970s and 1980s: advanced countries and multilateral institutions gave poor countries large volumes of concessional loans (low interest, ultra-long terms), the nominal balances look huge, but the real burden discounted at market rates is much smaller — measured by nominal values, recipient countries would be misjudged as heavily indebted; conversely, short-term high-interest commercial borrowing has small nominal balances but an extremely heavy real burden. The World Bank adopted the present-value method from 1995, making debt under different terms comparable on one scale. The critical values themselves are empirical calibrations rather than theoretical derivations (they come from statistical induction on samples of heavily indebted countries in the 1980s), and in use they must likewise be combined with debt structure (maturity, currency, interest rate, source) for a combined judgement.

### Debt service ratio

The debt service ratio is the ratio of a country's principal and interest payments on the external debt raised in a given period to the total of its various export revenues. It is the core indicator of whether external debt is excessive, with an empirical critical value of 20%: above 20-25% the external debt is excessive, with the danger of debt service difficulties and a debt crisis. The debt service ratio is not the only indicator for examining the size of external debt; debt structure and economic conditions must be examined in combination — for example, two countries with the same 20% debt service ratio, one with external debt mainly long-term low-interest loans and the other mainly short-term high-interest loans, the latter's situation is clearly more severe.

The logic of the debt service ratio is blunt and cruel: external debt must ultimately be repaid in foreign exchange, and developing countries' foreign exchange comes almost exclusively from exports — the higher the share of export revenue that must be spent on principal and interest, the lower the share available for importing equipment and technology and supporting development, and once export revenues slump or international interest rates spike (the US rate hikes of the 1980s made the interest burden of Latin America's floating-rate debt swell manifold), the debt service ratio breaks through the safety line in an instant. Mexico's default of 1982 was exactly the real form of that curve. This indicator is also key to understanding the paradox of "borrowing to develop": external debt can fill the saving and foreign exchange gaps in the short run (see the two-gap model below), but the obligation to repay makes the future gaps larger — the sustainability of borrowing depends on whether the borrowed resources raise the capacity to export itself, that is, "using borrowed money to learn to earn it back".

## Problems and analysis

### The main indicators for measuring a country's external debt burden

**(1) Static debt indicators (nominal values)**

Economic debt ratio: current-year external debt balance as a share of current-year gross national product, expressing the relation of the outstanding debt balance to the economy as a whole, $\text{Economic debt ratio}=\text{Outstanding external debt balance}/\text{Gross national product}$, with a safety line of 30%. Export debt ratio: current-year external debt balance as a ratio of current-year total exports, $\text{Export debt ratio}=\text{Current-year external debt balance}/\text{Current-year total export revenue}$, generally not to exceed 100%. Debt service ratio: current-year principal and interest payments as a share of current-year total export revenue, $\text{Debt service ratio}=\text{Current-year principal and interest payments}/\text{Current-year total export revenue}$, generally not to exceed 20%, with above 30% dangerous.

**(2) Present-value indicators (adopted by the World Bank from 1995)**

Economic present-value debt ratio: outstanding debt at present values divided by gross national product, with 80% as the critical value; export present-value debt ratio: outstanding debt at present values divided by exports, with 20% as the critical value; a country whose actual indicator exceeds 60% of the critical value is a moderately indebted country, and below that a lightly indebted one (the reasoning for replacing nominal values with present values is in the concept section).

**(3) Debt structure indicators**

Beyond the aggregate indicators, structure must be examined: currency structure (whether the currency of borrowing matches the currency of export receipts — when they are mismatched, exchange rate movements directly magnify the debt burden, and the "double mismatch" of the Asian financial crisis is the lesson), maturity structure (an excessive share of short-term debt means concentrated refinancing risk), source structure (the proportion of official concessional loans to commercial loans) and interest structure (the proportion of fixed to floating rates — the direct trigger of the Latin American crisis of the 1980s was floating-rate debt meeting a global rate hike).

### The derivation of the two-gap model and its theoretical and practical significance

**(1) Derivation of the model**

Starting from the national income identities: total supply $Y = C + S + T + M$, total demand $Y = C + I + G + X$. Setting tax revenue equal to government spending ($T = G$), the balance condition becomes

$$S + M = I + X$$

Rearranging gives the two-gap formula:

$$I - S = M - X$$

The left-hand side $I - S$ is the saving gap (the part by which investment exceeds domestic saving), the right-hand side $M - X$ the foreign exchange gap (the part by which imports exceed exports). The depth of the identity lies in "they must be equal": ex post national accounts ensure that the two gaps are naturally equal — the part by which investment exceeds saving must be filled by the part by which imports exceed exports (that is, by net capital inflow), and the abstract "gaps" are joined by a bridge of international capital movement.

**(2) Theoretical significance: why foreign capital is needed**

Developing countries at the early stage of development face two "gaps": wanting to invest while domestic saving is insufficient (the saving gap), and wanting to import machinery and equipment while the foreign exchange earned by exports is insufficient (the foreign exchange gap). The identity shows the two gaps are not two separate ledgers but two sides of the same gap. Importing external resources can fill both at once: foreign direct investment brings funds (filling the saving gap) and equipment and technology (imports not having to be paid for with current exports, filling the foreign exchange gap); external debt borrows foreign exchange (filling the foreign exchange gap) and then converts it into domestic investment (filling the saving gap). This framework gave the policy world in which Chenery and Strout proposed it in 1966 — post-war development aid and the introduction of foreign capital — its most direct theoretical basis.

**(3) Policy significance and practical testing**

The model's policy significance is four routes: importing external resources to fill both gaps at once; raising export capacity (earning foreign exchange to narrow the foreign exchange gap); raising the saving rate (narrowing the saving gap — the saving rates of the East Asian economies, generally above 30%, are exactly why the gap disappeared); and structural adjustment to fit the requirements of importing external resources. The practical test has two faces. The successful face: the East Asian model (the four Asian dragons, 1970s-1990s) drove itself with "imported foreign capital plus export orientation", foreign capital coming in as foreign exchange and equipment and going out as exports earning foreign exchange, the gap converging dynamically as export capacity rose, and China's large-scale import of FDI after reform and opening-up (first among developing countries continuously from 1993) likewise became the world's second largest economy along this path. The failing face: Latin America's borrowing of external debt in the 1970s and 1980s (mostly commercial syndicated floating-rate loans) supported import substitution rather than export expansion, and the gap did not narrow through borrowing but widened with the debt service obligations, into a full debt crisis from 1982. The lesson from the contrast is unmistakably clear: the key to using foreign capital is not its scale but whether it can be converted into export capacity and domestic saving — the borrowed money has to "generate" the capacity to repay it, and then the model closes.

**(4) Later development of the model**

The two-gap model was further extended: Bacha in 1990 proposed a "fiscal gap" (the constraint of the public sector's saving-investment gap) for fiscally weak countries, called together with the original two gaps the three-gap analysis; the model also became one of the intellectual sources of the later "growth diagnosis" framework (Hausmann-Rodrik-Velasco) for judging "where the binding constraint is". Its identity core is still used today in balance of payments and development financing analysis — no strict version of a "gap" narrative can avoid $I - S = M - X$.

## Extended discussion

### The basic content and policy significance of the eclectic theory of international production

**(1) Basic content**

The eclectic theory of international production was summed up by the British economist Dunning in 1977. Starting from the reality of the international economy, it touches on all aspects of cross-border capital movement, and is the systematic theory of the foreign direct investment of multinational corporations. Its content: the combination of ownership-specific advantages, internalization advantages and location-specific advantages determines whether a firm has an advantage in outward investment, or whether it turns to exploit that advantage through export or technology transfer.

Ownership-specific advantages: a firm's organizational and managerial capacity, financing advantages, technical features, scale and monopoly position, and the advantages obtained from product differentiation — they make the firm stronger than local companies in the host country, enough to overcome the additional costs and institutional risks of producing abroad. Internalization advantages: the capacity to allocate ownership-specific advantages inside the firm — external markets are incomplete (pricing technology is hard, controlling quality is hard, intermediate goods markets are missing), and ownership advantages may be impaired or lost in market transactions, so internalization carries out resource allocation within the firm to overcome the adverse conditions. Location-specific advantages: the sufficient condition for investment, including the host country's labour cost, market conditions and the state of demand, tariff and non-tariff barriers, the host country's government policy and the like. The decision rule the three combine into:

$$\text{Foreign direct investment}=\text{Ownership-specific advantage}+\text{Market internalization}+\text{Location-specific advantage}$$

Only when all three advantages are present does a firm take the form of outward foreign direct investment in its international economic activity; lacking location advantage it exports, lacking internalization advantage it transfers technology.

The theory was then further developed dynamically — the investment development cycle hypothesis (Dunning 1981): a country's cross-border capital movement (especially outward investment) is closely related to the degree of economic development, and as the stage of development advances the three advantages change dynamically, so the country's flows with external capital pass successively through stages of net inflow, mixed inflow and outflow, and net outflow. This U-shaped trajectory is fairly well borne out in the East Asian economies and in China: China moving from net capital inflow (the 1990s FDI peak) to "mixed inflow and outflow" (outward direct investment in the 2010s leaping to the front rank in the world) is climbing the latter half of the cycle.

**(2) Policy significance**

The eclectic theory reflects the new configuration of the international economy after the 1960s: it overcomes the defect of international trade theory in assuming capital immobility, and also overcomes the limitation of international investment research in explaining capital movement alone, bringing international trade, direct investment and technology transfer into a unified framework, with both explanatory power and practical relevance. For the host country: ownership and internalization advantages come with the firm and the government cannot supply them, what it can supply is location advantage — so the focus of investment promotion is clear (infrastructure, market size, policy stability, labour quality) and not merely tax concessions; for the home country: policy can help firms cultivate ownership advantages (research support, brand building, intellectual property protection — protecting intellectual property is exactly the institutional condition that makes firms dare to go abroad by direct investment rather than by selling technology). The theory's weakness in measurability (the three advantages are hard to measure) and its insufficient explanatory power for developing-country firms (multinationals from developing countries need not hold an obvious monopoly advantage, and the transfer of small-scale and appropriate technologies has its own logic — Wells' and Rao's theories of developing-country FDI can supplement it) indicate that the eclectic paradigm is a starting point of analysis, not an end.

## Limits and what follows

The centre of gravity of factor movement research has moved three ways in contemporary work. First, **from "why cross borders" to "what crossing borders looks like"**: Antràs from 2003 onwards brought incomplete contracts and property rights theory into the analysis of multinational corporations, explaining which stages of a global value chain stay inside the firm (capital-intensive, asset-specific intermediate inputs) and which are outsourced (labour-intensive, generic inputs) — the theory of the firm's boundary and trade theory have merged, and "the organization of global value chains" is the most active frontier of the field; China's rising position in global value chains (from assembly to exporting new energy vehicles as whole units) provides a dynamic sample. Second, **the distortion and re-estimation of FDI statistics**: investment routed by multinationals through tax havens has badly blurred bilateral FDI data (for long over half of the FDI China has absorbed has been routed through Hong Kong and offshore centres, with "round-trip investment"), and research on base erosion and profit shifting (BEPS) has revealed that the place where multinational profits are reported and the place where they are created diverge greatly — the OECD's BEPS Action Plan and the global minimum corporate tax (the "two-pillar" agreement of 2021, entering into force in legislation in several countries from 2024) are exactly patching the institutional holes in this distortion. Third, **sovereign debt governance after the debt indicators**: the present-value debt ratio and the debt service ratio draw the map of risk, but the sovereign debt predicaments of the 2010s (the restructuring standoffs of Greece, Argentina, Sri Lanka and Zambia) exposed a deeper institutional gap: there is no international bankruptcy court, and restructuring depends on creditor coordination — collective action clauses (included in newly issued sovereign bonds from 2003) and the G20 Common Framework (applicable to low-income country debt treatment from 2020) are the current institutional responses; China's role has shifted from debtor to a principal official creditor ("Belt and Road" financing), and its debt treatment practice is being written into this still-evolving set of rules. The textbook chapter on factor movement is thus joined to the renegotiation of the international economic order — which is exactly the theme of Chapters 9 and 10.
