# International economics

This set of chapters is not about memorizing a string of theory names. It answers one question throughout:

**Why do countries trade and move capital across borders, and how do prices, exchange rates and policy in an open economy act together on its internal and external equilibrium.**

Compressed into a chain of questions, the whole course falls into three layers:

1. Trade theory: why countries trade at all, and where comparative advantage comes from.
2. Trade policy and openness: why protection exists, and what tariffs and quotas do.
3. Open macroeconomics: how to read the balance of payments, exchange rates, capital flows and internal–external equilibrium.

## 1. The questions this set keeps circling back to

- Where the gains from international trade come from.
- Whether comparative advantage is really settled by technology or by factor endowments.
- Why protectionism and anti-dumping measures still exist in practice.
- Why the balance of payments goes out of balance, and why exchange rates move.
- Why an open economy finds it harder to hold internal stability and external balance at the same time.

## 2. The birth and testing of the theories

- **The first data check of comparative advantage**: Ricardo in 1817 held that export patterns are set by relative labour productivity. MacDougall (1951) tested it on export data from over twenty US and UK industries: the industries where US productivity led were exactly the ones exporting most to Britain — comparative advantage passed its first systematic test more than a century after it was proposed.
- **The Leontief paradox (1953)**: the H-O theorem predicted that capital-rich America should export capital-intensive goods. Leontief computed from his own input-output tables that US exports were instead more labour-intensive, and the paradox triggered a wave of revisions — human capital, demand reversal, factor-intensity reversal. The well-known case of a theory being challenged by its own testing instrument.
- **The testing history of purchasing power parity**: Cassel in 1918 held that the exchange rate is set by the two countries' price levels. The data split by horizon: it barely holds in the short run (exchange rate swings dwarf price-level gaps) and holds weakly in the long run. The verdict "useless short term, converging long term" led directly to the asset-market approach to exchange-rate determination.
- **The J-curve effect**: a currency devaluation improves the trade balance only after first worsening it, because import and export volumes react to a change in the exchange rate with a lag. Multi-country data support that shape — elasticity analysis moves from theory into a prediction with a shape.
