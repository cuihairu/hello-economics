# The historical progression of international economics

This is not a chapter index but the chain of theoretical advance in international economics. The point is not merely to memorise "who proposed what", but to see clearly: what reality did the earlier theory explain, why did it turn out to be insufficient, and where did the next theory push it forward and correct it.

## The question tree and the progress of finding answers

> How to read: record each step as "question advanced -> brief answer -> corresponding chapter"; if a step involves several places in the textbooks, list them all.

### Big question 1: why do countries trade, and where do the gains from trade come from

- Subquestion 1.1: what is the basic object of study in an open economy.
  Corresponding chapter: [Chapter 1 Introduction](/international/绪论)
- Subquestion 1.2: why do countries trade with one another.
  Corresponding chapter: [Chapter 2 Pure theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易纯理论)
- Subquestion 1.3: do the gains from trade come from absolute or from comparative advantage.
  Corresponding chapter: [Chapter 2 Pure theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易纯理论)
- The progress of finding the answer:
  1. The original mercantilist intuition was: international trade is more like a zero-sum contest.
     Brief answer: early views took trade to be a contest over wealth in which one country gains more and another gains less.
     Corresponding chapters: [Chapter 1 Introduction](/international/绪论) · [Chapter 2 Pure theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易纯理论)
  2. Smith corrected it: the division of labour can produce mutual gain, and the gains from trade come from absolute advantage.
     Brief answer: countries specialise in the goods they can produce more efficiently and exchange them, and both gain.
     Corresponding chapters: [Chapter 1 Introduction](/international/绪论) · [Chapter 2 Pure theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易纯理论)
  3. Ricardo corrected it further: even if one country is stronger in everything, comparative advantage can still generate trade.
     Brief answer: what determines trade is not absolute strength but differences in relative cost.
     Corresponding chapter: [Chapter 2 Pure theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易纯理论)
  4. The limit of this round: why trade occurs has been explained, but where comparative advantage itself comes from is still unclear.
     Brief answer: classical theory shows that trade will occur, but does not yet show why countries' advantages differ.
     Corresponding chapters: [Chapter 2 Pure theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易纯理论) · [Chapter 3 Modern and contemporary theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易的现代与当代理论)

### Big question 2: what determines comparative advantage, and why is actual trade more complicated than classical theory

- Subquestion 2.1: where exactly does comparative advantage come from.
  Corresponding chapter: [Chapter 2 Pure theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易纯理论)
- Subquestion 2.2: why do actual data and the H-O theory deviate.
  Corresponding chapter: [Chapter 3 Modern and contemporary theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易的现代与当代理论)
- Subquestion 2.3: how technology, economies of scale and intra-industry trade change the pattern of trade.
  Corresponding chapter: [Chapter 3 Modern and contemporary theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易的现代与当代理论)
- The progress of finding the answer:
  1. Ricardo explained: comparative advantage produces trade.
     Brief answer: as long as relative costs differ, countries can gain through specialisation and exchange.
     Corresponding chapter: [Chapter 2 Pure theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易纯理论)
  2. H-O advanced it to: comparative advantage comes from differences in factor endowments.
     Brief answer: a country exports the goods that intensively use its relatively abundant factor.
     Corresponding chapter: [Chapter 2 Pure theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易纯理论)
  3. The Leontief paradox exposed: actual data do not always support H-O.
     Brief answer: empirical fact shows that factor endowments alone cannot fully explain actual trade structures.
     Corresponding chapter: [Chapter 3 Modern and contemporary theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易的现代与当代理论)
  4. The technological gap theory and the product life-cycle theory corrected it: technology also determines the pattern of trade.
     Brief answer: technological leadership, the diffusion of innovation and changes in product stage also shape export structures.
     Corresponding chapter: [Chapter 3 Modern and contemporary theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易的现代与当代理论)
  5. New trade theory advanced it again to: economies of scale, product differentiation and intra-industry trade also matter.
     Brief answer: similar countries also engage in large volumes of two-way trade because of economies of scale and product differentiation.
     Corresponding chapter: [Chapter 3 Modern and contemporary theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易的现代与当代理论)
  6. The conclusion of this round: actual trade is far more complicated than classical theory.
     Brief answer: actual trade is jointly affected by factors, technology, economies of scale and market structure.
     Corresponding chapters: [Chapter 2 Pure theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易纯理论) · [Chapter 3 Modern and contemporary theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易的现代与当代理论)

### Big question 3: why do governments intervene in trade, and why does free trade turn into a question of policy choice

- Subquestion 3.1: if free trade is usually efficient, why does protection still exist in reality.
  Corresponding chapter: [Chapter 4 International trade policy analysis](/international/国际贸易政策分析)
- Subquestion 3.2: what consequences do tariffs, quotas, dumping and anti-dumping bring.
  Corresponding chapter: [Chapter 4 International trade policy analysis](/international/国际贸易政策分析)
- The progress of finding the answer:
  1. The general answer classical theory gave first was: free trade is usually more efficient.
     Brief answer: under standard conditions free trade raises the efficiency of resource allocation and total welfare.
     Corresponding chapter: [Chapter 4 International trade policy analysis](/international/国际贸易政策分析)
  2. The protectionist argument pushed back: if stages of development differ, should countries still be fully open.
     Brief answer: a backward country at a given stage of development may not be well suited to becoming fully open immediately.
     Corresponding chapter: [Chapter 4 International trade policy analysis](/international/国际贸易政策分析)
  3. The infant industry argument added: short-term protection can serve long-term development.
     Brief answer: for industries that are not yet mature but have potential, staged protection may help them grow.
     Corresponding chapter: [Chapter 4 International trade policy analysis](/international/国际贸易政策分析)
  4. Welfare analysis then advanced it to: what price and welfare consequences tariffs, quotas and dumping each bring.
     Brief answer: different policy instruments change prices, output, the distribution of welfare and the pattern of international interests.
     Corresponding chapter: [Chapter 4 International trade policy analysis](/international/国际贸易政策分析)
  5. The conclusion of this round: the question is no longer "whether we want free trade" but "which policy works under what conditions".
     Brief answer: the discussion of trade policy shifted from a fight over principles to the analysis of conditions, instruments and effects.
     Corresponding chapter: [Chapter 4 International trade policy analysis](/international/国际贸易政策分析)

- The progress of finding the answer:
  1. Trade theory originally explained only: how commodities are exchanged across borders.

## I. Question 1: why do countries trade, and where do the gains from trade come from

### 1776, Adam Smith, the theory of absolute advantage

- Representative work:
  *The Wealth of Nations: An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations*.
- Influenced by:
  Continuing the classical political economists' concern with the division of labour and market exchange.
- What was proposed:
  The theory of absolute advantage.
- What it was explaining:
  Why countries trade with one another.
- Core claim:
  If a country's production efficiency of a good is absolutely higher than another country's, it should specialise in that good and exchange.
- The advance over predecessors:
  Rewriting international trade from the mercantilist "zero-sum contest" into "the division of labour produces mutual gain".
- What was left unsolved:
  If a country is more efficient in every good, why does trade still occur — Smith could not explain this.
- Back to the textbooks:
  [Introduction](/international/绪论) · [Pure theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易纯理论)

### 1817, Ricardo, the theory of comparative advantage

- Representative work:
  *On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation*.
- Influenced by:
  Built directly on Smith's theory of absolute advantage, but proposing a correction to its inadequate scope of explanation.
- What was proposed:
  The theory of comparative advantage.
- What it was explaining:
  Why trade can still occur even if one country is more efficient in every good.
- Core claim:
  The gains from trade come from differences in relative cost, not from differences in absolute efficiency.
- The advance over Smith:
  Pushing the source of the gains from trade from "absolute advantage" to "comparative advantage" and greatly enlarging the theory's explanatory power.
- Whom it directly corrected:
  Directly correcting the gap in Smith's theory of absolute advantage over "why a country with all-round advantage still trades".
- What was left unsolved:
  Ricardo could explain why trade occurs, but did not say where comparative advantage itself comes from.
- Back to the textbooks:
  [Pure theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易纯理论)

### The logic of this stage

- Smith first overturned the mercantilist zero-sum view.
- Ricardo showed further that even if a strong country is stronger in every good, trade can still be mutually beneficial.
- But a new question arose at once:
  Why exactly do countries' comparative advantages differ.

## II. Question 2: what exactly determines comparative advantage, and why is actual trade not so simple as classical theory

### 1919 to the 1930s, Heckscher and Ohlin, the factor endowment theory

- Representative work:
  Heckscher's 1919 paper and Ohlin's *Interregional and International Trade*.
- Influenced by:
  Inheriting Ricardo's core idea that "comparative advantage determines trade", but attempting to explain the source of comparative advantage.
- What was proposed:
  The factor endowment theory.
- What it was explaining:
  Why different countries come to have different comparative advantages.
- Core claim:
  A country exports the goods that intensively use its relatively abundant factor and imports the goods that intensively use its relatively scarce factor.
- The advance over Ricardo:
  Ricardo showed "comparative advantage produces trade"; Heckscher–Ohlin showed "where comparative advantage comes from".
- What was left unsolved:
  The theory relies on assumptions such as identical technology and constant returns to scale, and its explanatory power in reality is limited.
- Back to the textbooks:
  [Pure theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易纯理论)

### 1953, Leontief, the Leontief paradox

- Representative work:
  The empirical input-output study of the structure of US imports and exports.
- Influenced by:
  Directly testing the factor endowment theory empirically.
- What was proposed:
  The Leontief paradox.
- What it was explaining:
  Why the trade structure of capital-rich countries such as the United States is inconsistent with the H-O prediction.
- Core claim:
  Actual data can run counter to the standard factor endowment theory.
- The advance over the H-O theory:
  It did not propose a complete new theory, but exposed the inadequacy of the old one through empirical fact.
- What was left unsolved:
  The paradox itself is not a complete alternative theory; it only forced the problem into the open.
- Back to the textbooks:
  [Modern and contemporary theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易的现代与当代理论)

### -1966, Posner and Vernon, the technological gap theory and the product life-cycle theory

- Representative work:
  Posner's paper on the technological gap, Vernon's paper on the product life cycle.
- Influenced by:
  Responding directly to the Leontief paradox and to the H-O theory's neglect of technological differences.
- What was proposed:
  The technological gap theory and the product life-cycle theory.
- What it was explaining:
  Why actual trade is affected by innovation, technological leadership and the product life cycle.
- Core claim:
  The pattern of trade is not determined by factor endowments alone; technological leadership and changes in product stage also determine export structures.
- The advance over the H-O theory:
  Bringing "technology" back into trade theory and correcting the strong assumption that "countries have identical technology".
- What was left unsolved:
  Its explanation of economies of scale, intra-industry trade and imperfect competition remained insufficient.
- Back to the textbooks:
  [Modern and contemporary theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易的现代与当代理论)

### -From the 1980s on, new trade theory

- Transitional figures:
  Krugman, Helpman and others systematised economies of scale, imperfect competition and intra-industry trade.
- Influenced by:
  Built on a long reflection on the limitations of classical and H-O theory, and absorbing the analysis of economies of scale and imperfect competition.
- What was proposed:
  An explanatory framework of economies of scale, product differentiation and intra-industry trade.
- What it was explaining:
  Why similar countries also trade heavily, and why like goods are traded in both directions.
- Core claim:
  Trade comes not only from differences, but also from economies of scale, product differentiation and market structure.
- The advance over predecessors:
  Pushing international trade from "determined by national differences" to "also determined by firms, scale and market structure".
- What was left unsolved:
  The policy implications are more complicated and more easily slip into the controversy over strategic trade policy.
- Back to the textbooks:
  [Modern and contemporary theory of international trade](/international/国际贸易的现代与当代理论)

### The logic of this stage

- Ricardo explained the role of comparative advantage.
- The H-O theory explained the source of comparative advantage.
- The Leontief paradox exposed the empirical gap in the H-O theory.
- The technological gap theory and the product life-cycle theory brought in technology.
- New trade theory further brought economies of scale, intra-industry trade and imperfect competition into the analysis.

## III. Question 3: why do governments intervene in trade, and why does free trade turn into a question of policy choice

### From the sixteenth century on, the protectionist tradition

- Transitional figures:
  List is the most typical figure in this line.
- Influenced by:
  Raising a realist critique against classical free trade theory.
- What was proposed:
  The argument for infant industry protection and other protectionist reasoning.
- What it was explaining:
  Why backward countries are not necessarily suited to becoming fully open immediately.
- Core claim:
  At a given stage of development, temporary protection may help industries grow.
- The advance over classical free trade:
  Distinguishing "long-run efficiency" from "stage of development".
- What was left unsolved:
  How to ensure that protection is temporary and effective rather than a long-run protection of inefficiency.
- Back to the textbooks:
  [International trade policy analysis](/international/国际贸易政策分析)

### The twentieth century, analysis of tariffs and non-tariff barriers

- Influenced by:
  Built on traditional trade theory, but turning further to the actual effects of policy instruments.
- What was proposed:
  Systematic analysis of tariffs, quotas, dumping, anti-dumping and export subsidies.
- What it was explaining:
  Why there is so much trade policy in the real world, and why the consequences of policy differ between countries.
- Core claim:
  Policy instruments change domestic prices, the distribution of welfare and the pattern of international interests.
- The advance over early protectionism:
  No longer making normative claims alone, but turning policy consequences into welfare analysis.
- What was left unsolved:
  Whether strategic protection is justified, and whether political-economy motives distort policy, remain difficult problems.
- Back to the textbooks:
  [International trade policy analysis](/international/国际贸易政策分析)

### The logic of this stage

- Classical theory advocated free trade.
- Actual development experience put forward "different stages of development, different policy choices".
- So international economics moved from "whether to have free trade" to "which policy works under what conditions".

## IV. Question 4: how to understand the balance of payments, exchange rates, capital flows and integration in an open economy

### -The nineteenth century, Hume and others, the idea of automatic adjustment of the balance of payments

- Representative work:
  Hume's writings on the price-specie-flow mechanism.
- Influenced by:
  The early quantity theory of money and the experience of the gold standard.
- What was proposed:
  The idea of automatic adjustment of the balance of payments.
- What it was explaining:
  Why a country's trade surplus or deficit does not widen indefinitely.
- Core claim:
  The flow of gold and silver and the adjustment of prices push the balance of payments back into balance.
- The advance over predecessors:
  Pushing the trade question up to the monetary and balance of payments level.
- What was left unsolved:
  The mechanism relies on the gold standard and price elasticity, and is not enough for the modern credit-money economy.
- Back to the textbooks:
  [Balance of payments analysis](/international/国际收支分析)

### The twentieth century, balance of payments theory with elasticity and absorption analysis

- Influenced by:
  Built on traditional adjustment ideas, but needing to explain imbalances in the modern open economy.
- What was proposed:
  Elasticity analysis, absorption analysis and the like.
- What it was explaining:
  How exchange-rate changes and changes in total expenditure affect the balance of payments.
- Core claim:
  The balance of payments depends not only on prices but also on income, absorption and structure.
- The advance over early adjustment ideas:
  From "prices adjust automatically" to "prices, income and expenditure acting together".
- What was left unsolved:
  Financial capital flows and expectations grew ever more important, and the trade balance framework alone was not enough.
- Back to the textbooks:
  [Balance of payments analysis](/international/国际收支分析) · [Internal and external equilibrium of the macroeconomy](/international/宏观经济的内外均衡)

### -The 1920s, the purchasing power parity theory; later interest rate parity and the development of exchange-rate theory

- Influenced by:
  Built on the quantity theory of money and on the analysis of an open price system.
- What was proposed:
  Exchange-rate theories such as purchasing power parity and interest rate parity.
- What it was explaining:
  Why the exchange rate changes.
- Core claim:
  The exchange rate may be jointly determined by price levels, interest differentials, capital flows and expectations.
- The advance over the old trade theory:
  Pushing international economics from the movement of commodities to monetary prices and the analysis of financial variables.
- What was left unsolved:
  A single exchange-rate theory often struggles to explain violent short-run fluctuations.
- Back to the textbooks:
  [The general theory of exchange rate determination](/international/汇率决定的一般理论)

### From the mid-twentieth century on, Mundell–Fleming and internal-external equilibrium theory

- Representative work:
  The classic papers by Mundell and Fleming on macroeconomic policy in an open economy.
- Influenced by:
  Influenced jointly by Keynesian macroeconomic analysis and balance of payments theory.
- What was proposed:
  A framework of internal and external equilibrium under open-economy conditions.
- What it was explaining:
  How a country can achieve full domestic employment and balance of payments equilibrium at the same time.
- Core claim:
  Fiscal policy, monetary policy, the exchange-rate regime and capital flows jointly determine the outcome of internal and external equilibrium.
- The advance over closed-economy macro theory:
  Extending the Keynesian macroeconomic model into an open-economy setting.
- What was left unsolved:
  With highly mobile global capital, the conflict between policy independence and exchange-rate stability is sharper.
- Back to the textbooks:
  [Internal and external equilibrium of the macroeconomy](/international/宏观经济的内外均衡)

### From the 1950s on, customs union and economic integration theory

- Influenced by:
  Built on trade theory and welfare analysis.
- What was proposed:
  Trade creation and trade diversion, and integration theory.
- What it was explaining:
  Why countries move from ordinary trade relations to institutional union.
- Core claim:
  Regional integration can raise efficiency, but it can also bring new exclusion and a redistribution of interests.
- The advance over traditional trade theory:
  Pushing the unit of analysis from "exchange between countries" to "institutional regional organisation".
- What was left unsolved:
  The tension between globalisation and regionalisation, and the problem of a diverging international order, continue.
- Back to the textbooks:
  [Economic integration and the international economic order](/international/经济一体化与国际经济秩序分析) · [The trend of economic globalization](/international/经济全球化趋势)

## V. Finally compressing the whole book into one sentence

- The order of theoretical advance in international economics is:
  First explaining why trade occurs, then explaining where comparative advantage comes from, then explaining why actual trade is more complicated than classical theory, then explaining why countries intervene in trade, and finally pushing the question on to exchange rates, capital flows, integration and globalisation.
