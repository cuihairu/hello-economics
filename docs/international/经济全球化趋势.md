# Chapter 10 Coda: the trend of economic globalization

> **Core question of this chapter**: how has the world economy been woven into a single web? What are the drivers and the costs of globalization? When the era of "efficiency first" ends, what kind of world will the theories discussed so far face?

## Thread of the chapter

Globalization is not new, it is a "second occurrence". The first globalization (roughly 1870-1914) was driven jointly by steamships, railways, telegraphy and the gold standard: the share of trade in each country's GDP climbed to a historic high, mass migration (tens of millions crossing the Atlantic) and outward capital flows (British foreign investment close to half of its saving) made the world economy's degree of integration in many dimensions a level not surpassed until the end of the twentieth century (O'Rourke and Williamson's quantitative work established this periodization). The two world wars and the Great Depression interrupted it: the Smoot-Hawley Tariff of 1930 and competitive devaluations shrank world trade by about two thirds, and the 1930s "beggar-thy-neighbour" era became the deepest memory of those who designed the post-war order. The institutional design of Bretton Woods (1944) and GATT (1947) answered that memory head-on: fixed but adjustable exchange rates, capital controls tacitly permitted, tariff bindings and most-favoured-nation — the goal of Keynes and White was not "maximized globalization" but "managed openness", rules in place of the jungle. The second globalization (from the 1950s) came in two waves: trade liberalization (the eight GATT rounds pushed the average tariff on industrial goods in the advanced economies from double digits down to the low single digits, per WTO statistics) and the transport-technology revolution (the commercialization of the container in 1956 cut the cost of sea freight by an order of magnitude — Levenson's *The Box: How the Shipping Container Made the World Smaller and the World Economy Bigger* calls it "the box that changed the world"); from the 1970s onwards capital-flow liberalization added the wing of financial globalization. In the 1990s the end of the Cold War, the integration of the transition economies and the compression of communication costs by information technology took globalization to its peak — China's real coordinate in international economics (its WTO accession in 2001) falls inside that wave. The 2008 financial crisis was the turning point: the ratio of global trade to GDP peaked and turned down around 2008 (per WTO and World Bank data), and "slowbalisation" became the keyword after 2016 — British exit from the EU, great-power trade wars, pandemic-era supply chain breakages, the alignment of the world into geopolitical camps all pushing the "efficiency first" logic of integration towards "rebalancing efficiency and security". I place this chapter at the end, using concept—mechanism—debate to draw the whole text together.

## Core concepts

### Economic globalization

The definition of the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development: globalization is the process by which countries become increasingly interlinked and interdependent across national boundaries, a process powerfully promoted by rapid advances in transport, communications and information technology. The definition of the International Monetary Fund: the increase in the scale and forms of cross-border trade in goods and services and of international capital flows, together with the wide and rapid diffusion of technology, strengthens the interdependence of the world economies. Economic globalization is a dynamic process of the modern economy and the product of social and economic development reaching a certain stage.

The common core of the two definitions is "interdependence", and interdependence is measurable: on the goods dimension look at the trade dependence ratio (world trade as a share of GDP, roughly 14% in 1913, falling to about 5% in 1950, peaking at about 30% in 2008 and then moving on a plateau, per World Bank data); on the financial dimension look at the stock of cross-border assets as a share of total output (the indicators of financial globalization stood high in 1914, bottomed out in the 1970s and climbed again since, a shape exactly parallel to the trade indicators); on the information dimension look at cross-border data flows — the fastest-growing "trade" of the twenty-first century is actually data that no longer passes through customs. The two-sidedness of interdependence is measurable by the same route: integration brings efficiency (comparative advantage, economies of scale, knowledge diffusion — the theories of Chapters 2 and 3), and it brings contagion (external shocks transmitted synchronously through the trade and financial channels — the mechanisms of Chapters 5 and 8), so choosing a "degree of globalization" is fundamentally a trade-off between "efficiency and fragility", not a one-way narrative of progress.

### The driving mechanisms of globalization

Economic globalization is driven by three groups of forces together; miss one and the pace slows or reverses.

Technology engine: transport costs (containerization, ultra-large vessels, port mechanization — unit sea freight down about nine tenths in half a century) and communication costs (submarine cable, the internet — cross-border communication costs approaching zero) set the "boundary of tradability": which goods, which services, which stages of production are worth moving across distance. Services were previously untradable (you cannot import a haircut); the communication revolution made remotely deliverable services (software, design, call centres) tradable — "the next globalization" is happening here.

Institutional engine: multilateral and regional agreements (tariff cuts under GATT/WTO, the network of regional free-trade areas) lower policy barriers; each country's openness policies (foreign investment access, capital account, exchange rate arrangements) lower institutional friction. During the "Washington consensus" period of the 1990s the institutional engine ran at full speed; after the 2010s it slowed markedly and in places inverted (export controls, investment screening, subsidy competition) — institutions are the slowest and most reversible of the three engines, and the first globalization was destroyed by institutions (war and beggar-thy-neighbour) rather than by technology.

Capital engine: profit-seeking international capital searches the whole world for cost hollows and markets, and multinational corporations package capital, technology and management for cross-border deployment (the OLI framework of Chapter 7); FDI and global value chains reorganize "trade" into "one product, an international relay". The three engines relate multiplicatively, not additively: technology decides feasibility, institutions decide permission, capital decides execution — in the 1920s technology had not retreated, it was the collapse of institutions that ended the first globalization.

### The gains and costs of globalization

Gains: consumers get cheaper and more varied goods (the consumer surplus from trade is empirically large — larger still for low-income groups, whose baskets hold a higher share of tradables); producers get larger markets and finer specialization (returns to scale and specialization); countries allocate resources according to comparative advantage and raise total output; technology and knowledge diffuse with trade and investment (learning by exporting, FDI technology spillovers). Costs: asymmetric distribution — trade creates winners and losers alike (the real-world version of the Stolper-Samuelson effect: low-skilled labour in the advanced economies bore wage and employment pressure from import competition, Rodrik's *Has Globalization Gone Too Far?* (1997) had already warned of this, and Autor and colleagues' "China shock" study (2013) confirmed with data the geographic concentration and durability of the shock), while compensation mechanisms (retraining, trade adjustment assistance) are widely under-delivered; macro contagion — financial crises transmit across borders through capital flows and bank channels (Asia 1997, the world 2008), and the more open an economy, the more imported volatility it takes; sovereignty constraints — international rules and capital-market discipline compress domestic policy space (Rodrik's "trilemma": super-globalization, national sovereignty and democratic politics cannot all be had together); the environment and the race to the bottom — worries about "downward competition" and carbon leakage are new cost items of contemporary globalization. The asymmetric distribution of gains and costs is exactly the source of political backlash: the total gain from globalization is positive, but if the distribution, compensation and governance of those gains fail, the bearers of the costs will use the ballot to close the door — the anti-globalization politics of the advanced economies since 2016 is, at bottom, the echo of a distribution problem.

## Problems and analysis

### China and globalization: a sample of "integration—upgrading—rebalancing"

China's path through globalization provides the text so far with a complete single-case test. First stage (1980-2000): inbound foreign investment and export orientation, using FDI to fill the saving and foreign exchange gaps on the logic of the two-gap model, and embedding labour-intensive industries in the international division of labour (the "inherent advantage" of Chapter 4 and the location advantage of Chapter 7 resonating). Second stage (2001-2013): joining the WTO in 2001 was the largest single event of the institutional engine, exports grew explosively, and in 2009 China became the world's largest goods exporter (remaining first in most years since), so that its position in intra-industry and intra-product division as the "world factory" was established; the "twin surpluses" of the same period (current account and capital account both in surplus) mirrored themselves in the accumulation of foreign exchange reserves (the bookkeeping logic of Chapter 5) and pushed the internal-external equilibrium problem (Chapter 8) to the centre of macro policy. Third stage (2013 to the present): the "Belt and Road" initiative marks a change of role — from a net importer of capital and production capacity to an important exporter of outward investment and construction (the later half of the investment development cycle hypothesis of Chapter 7); and the "dual circulation" proposed in 2020 (domestic circulation as the mainstay, domestic and international circulation mutually reinforcing) is a rebalancing statement answering external change and internal structural transition. This sample demonstrates both faces of globalization at once: the gains of integration (growth, employment, technology learning) and the necessity of rebalancing (external imbalance, critical dependence, geopolitical friction) — China's own evolution of attitude towards globalization (firmly maintaining the multilateral trading system while pursuing autonomy and controllability of supply chains) is itself part of the question of the "efficiency and security rebalancing" era.

### Will globalization continue? — three competing futures

**Scenario one: re-acceleration (digital and services globalization)**. Even as goods trade slows, digitizable service trade (software, professional services, entertainment) grows far faster than goods trade, and cross-border data flows become the new vehicle of globalization; if digital trade rules (the plurilateral e-commerce negotiations, data-flow provisions) are concluded, globalization may enter its next phase in a new form.

**Scenario two: slowbalisation (plateau and restructuring)**. Trade/GDP moves on a plateau, multinational corporations shorten supply chains ("China+1" positioning) without decoupling — the efficiency logic remains, only with resilience and political feasibility added as constraints. Most quantitative studies (IMF and WTO medium-term assessments) support this scenario as the dominant tone: globalization is not reversing, it is "re-wiring".

**Scenario three: forking (camps)**. If geopolitical confrontation keeps escalating, the world economy may split along security camps into several technology-trade-financial blocs (export control lists and sanctions lists already have their outlines), replaying the lesson of the 1930s. The cost of this scenario is hard to bear (estimates put the loss on global GDP at several percentage points), but "unbearable" was also mistaken for "impossible" before 1914 — which is exactly why the institutional engine (WTO reform, great-power coordination) has to keep running.

The three scenarios are not mutually exclusive; reality is most likely a regionalized combination: digital services keep integrating, strategic sectors (semiconductors, critical minerals, biotechnology) fork, general goods slowbalize. The leading indicators for judging which combination prevails are correspondingly three: the pace of digital trade rule negotiations (scenario one), the net change in tariffs and non-tariff barriers (scenario two), the coverage of technology controls (scenario three).

## Limits and what follows

This chapter's "limits and what follows" connects the discussion so far to the present. First, **the parameters of the textbook world model are being rewritten**: the core theories discussed above (comparative advantage, factor endowments, Mundell-Fleming, the trilemma) all embed an implicit premise of "efficiency first, stable rules", while security weights, resilience constraints and sanction instruments are entering the de facto objective functions of countries — the mathematical structure of the theories need not be overturned, but the parameters and the constraints are changing, and they must be recalibrated before application. Second, **a crisis of statistics and measurement**: global value chains distort gross-value trade statistics (noted in Chapter 1), data flows and free digital services sit outside customs, and profit shifting by multinational corporations distorts the balance of payments as defined — "can we measure globalization accurately" is itself an open research question (value-added accounting, digital trade measurement and BEPS statistical reform are all in progress). Third, **the convergence of climate and globalization**: the carbon border adjustment mechanism (the EU's CBAM, running in transition since 2023) writes environmental externalities into trade costs, and "carbon tariffs" are the largest incremental variable in the trade rule framework since 1947 — an extension of climate policy, and possibly the prototype of a new kind of trade barrier, whose rule design will test the updating capacity of the multilateral system. This course started from a numerical example more than two hundred years old (Ricardo's Britain and Portugal) and ends in a world where the rules are being rewritten — what does not change is the analytical tool: reduce every "what shall we do" to "who benefits, who pays, under what constraints, through what mechanism". That way of asking is the analytical method used repeatedly above, and it is the lens a reader can pick up at any time when facing whatever globalization news the next decade brings.
