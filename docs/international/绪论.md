# Chapter 1 Introduction

> **Core question of this chapter**: economics already has microeconomics and macroeconomics — why is there also an "international economics"? On this side and that side of a border, what exactly does the logic of economic activity gain, and what does it lose?

## Thread of the chapter

The history of international economics is almost the history of economics itself: the mercantilism Smith attacked in 1776 was the first adversary of international trade theory, Ricardo's comparative advantage was the first milestone at which economic analysis leapt from "common sense" to "science", and Keynes's 1936 revolution likewise took place inside an open economy (the multiplier theory received its earliest international test in the trade multiplier). But the discipline took shape as an independent branch only after the Second World War: the gold standard had already collapsed, the Bretton Woods system had just been established, and the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade was beginning to operate, so international trade and the balance of payments became for the first time objects a government had to manage systematically — Kindleberger's *International Economics* (1953) fixed the standard textbook form of the discipline, while China introduced it systematically during the 1980s as opening-up advanced (the rapid growth of foreign trade turned this "foreign learning" quickly into "the reality at hand"). Its reason for existing independently can be summed in three "border effects": first, money has a boundary — goods and capital crossing national borders have to cross an exchange rate and different monetary regimes, so the "money" of closed-economy macro analysis is no longer a single unit of account internationally; second, policy has a boundary — the fiscal and monetary authorities of one country are its only regulator at home, but internationally there are several sovereign policy actors, and the spillovers and coordination of policy become a problem of their own; third, factors do not move freely — labour and capital move freely within a country but are impeded between countries by institutions and borders, so international differences in productivity get "frozen" inside each country and become the root of trade patterns. These three effects fix the object structure of international economics: two large blocks, trade theory (why countries exchange goods with one another) and open macroeconomics (how money and payments interact between countries), plus extended themes such as factor movements, integration and globalization.

## Core concepts

### International economics

International economics is the branch of economics that, on the basis of general economic theory, studies international economic activity and international economic relations. It is the extension and application of general economic theory into the international sphere, an organic part of the whole economic system, whose main objects of study include international trade theory and policy, balance of payments theory, exchange rate theory, the international movement of factors, international investment theory, and macroeconomic equilibrium in the open economy.

The positioning "extension and application" is worth unpacking: international economics does not build a house on a brand-new set of axioms, it takes micro price theory, general equilibrium and welfare analysis and macro national-income determination and money theory and re-derives them in a "world with borders" — and the border is precisely what makes many closed-economy conclusions fail or need revising. For example: "market clearing" in a closed economy only requires one price system, whereas in an open economy the same good has two prices in the two countries, and arbitrage (buying low, selling high) ties them together, so the "law of one price" becomes the shared foundation of international trade and exchange-rate theory; in closed-economy macro the interest rate is set by domestic saving and investment, in open-economy macro it is set by the world capital market (a small open economy), and the effect of monetary policy is rewritten entirely. The contrast question I use most when reading this set of chapters is "what does this conclusion look like in a closed economy, and which step of the reasoning does the border change" — the whole interest of international economics lies in that "which step".

### Open economy

An open economy, also called an "open-type economy" and opposed to a "closed economy", is an economy in which the economic activity of a country or region is closely tied to the world market (for instance through international trade and international financial flows). In an open economy there exists a certain scale of import-export trade flow or capital flow, factors, goods and services can cross borders relatively freely, and so a better allocation of resources and a higher economic efficiency are achieved. Against the trend of economic globalization, developing an open-type economy has become the mainstream choice of countries.

"Openness" in economics is a variable on a scale rather than a switch: the usual measures of it are the trade dependence ratio (total imports and exports as a share of GDP) and the scale of capital flows. The two ends of the scale differ strikingly — small economies such as Singapore and Hong Kong can run trade dependence ratios above 300% (a high share of re-export trade), a continental economy such as the United States sits at around 25% (latest published figure), and China's roughly 35% used to be well above most major economies for a long time (peak of about 64% in 2006, falling since then as domestic demand expanded and the trade/GDP ratio declined naturally, official statistics). A low degree of openness for a large country does not mean "more closed": a domestic market large enough lets specialization and the division of labour be completed internally, and increasing returns at world scale substitute for international trade. A second layer of the scale separates goods openness from financial openness — many countries have very open goods markets while their capital account is regulated (China is the typical case); the costs and benefits of goods openness are in the trade theory of the textbooks, while the costs and benefits of financial openness only arrive with the trilemma of Chapter 8, and the two must not be confused.

### International division of labour

The international division of labour is the division of labour between countries, international specialization in production; it is the foundation of international trade and the result of the social division of labour extending from the domestic to the foreign. Each country's choice of divisional arrangement, and the evolution of that arrangement, reflect the differences in economic development between countries and the degree of economic linkage among them. The main types are inter-industry division, intra-industry division, vertical division, horizontal division, and division between differing factor intensities.

Taking "division of labour" as the root of trade is the first principle of this discipline since Smith: trade is not a zero-sum game between countries (the error of mercantilism), it is the deepening of the division of labour replayed at a larger scale. Smith opens *The Wealth of Nations* with the pin factory to show how division of labour multiplies productivity, and immediately afterwards argues that "the division of labour is limited by the extent of the market" — the function of international trade is exactly to expand that "extent of the market" without limit. The most contemporaneous item in the typology is the evolution from vertical division towards intra-industry and intra-product division: nineteenth-century international division of labour was the inter-industry division of "British textiles for Indian cotton" (countries of different technical level each doing their own industry), in the late twentieth century intra-industry division appeared in the form of "Germany exporting cars and importing cars" (similar countries exchanging different varieties of similar goods), and since the 1990s what has really dominated global production is intra-product division — the chips, screen and assembly of a phone completed across different countries, with intermediate goods crossing borders several times. Every step of this evolution corresponds to a body of theory below: inter-industry division corresponds to Ricardo and the H-O model, intra-industry division to Krugman's new trade theory, and intra-product division to the current research frontier (global value chain analysis).

## Extended discussion

### The origin, subject and methods of international economics

**(1) The origin of international economics**

International economics is the product of microeconomics and macroeconomics reaching a certain stage of development. Under open-economy conditions, economic exchanges between countries are frequent, and the country becomes an agent of international economic activity, which produces economic linkages between states; when national economic activity must be examined against an international background, an independent branch discipline is needed in order to explore the internal connections of international economic relations. As noted in "Thread of the chapter", this process of becoming independent happened after the war, and its driving force was half theoretical (the systematization of comparative advantage, factor endowments and Keynesian macro under open conditions) and half practical (Bretton Woods, GATT, and balance of payments adjustment becoming routine problems for each country).

**(2) The objects of study**

In the order from theory to policy, the main objects are ten. Pure theory of international trade: on the assumption that factors cannot move internationally and excluding government intervention and monetary factors, it studies the causes of trade, trade patterns and the distribution of gains (Chapters 2 and 3). International trade policy: it studies restrictive or encouraging measures such as tariffs and quotas and their economic effects (Chapter 4). Balance of payments: it studies the relations between the constituent parts, the adjustment mechanisms and the measures for disequilibrium (Chapter 5). Foreign exchange and exchange rates: the determination and variation of the exchange rate (Chapter 6, not dealing with the practice of foreign exchange transactions). International movement of factors: the cross-border movement of capital, labour and technology, and its substitution with and complementarity to goods flows (Chapter 7). Multinational corporations: the motives for and the effects of outward foreign direct investment. Economic development: international economic relations bearing on development. Economic integration: the economic effects and welfare analysis of customs unions (Chapter 9). Macro regulation of the open economy: the simultaneous achievement of internal and external equilibrium (Chapter 8). Economic globalization and the international economic order (Chapter 10).

**(3) The methods of study**

Six groups of methods correspond to six habits of analysis of the discipline. Combining macro and micro: the micro part of trade theory analyses prices, resource allocation and welfare, the macro part analyses the balance of payments and national income, and the combination of the two is the basic theoretical feature of the discipline. Combining static and dynamic: starting from comparative static analysis (comparing the difference between two equilibrium positions, before and after a change) and gradually moving towards dynamics (the product life-cycle theory, for example, sets the static comparative advantage "in motion"). Combining qualitative and quantitative: structural judgement and quantitative relations are given equal weight, with an emphasis on empirics. Combining partial and general equilibrium: partial-equilibrium analysis of a tariff looks only at a single market, general-equilibrium analysis examines all markets and aggregate welfare (the two sets of diagrams in Chapter 4 are arranged exactly in that way). Combining theory and policy: the policy content of this discipline is strong, and almost every theory connects directly to a policy prescription — this is the most distinctive character it has compared with general micro and macro textbooks. Combining absorption and evaluation: as a branch of western economics, international economics carries the common features of that system (it devotes little attention to problems at the level of the relations of production, and cannot reveal the inequality phenomena in international economic relations at their root); study should both master its analytical tools and be aware of the critiques of radical economics and others, keeping the theoretical horizon complete.

## Limits and what follows

The discipline map given by the introduction is drawn inside the "country-versus-country" frame, and that frame itself is being challenged in three directions. First, global value chains make "a country's exports" ambiguous: a phone export may draw more than half its value from imported components, so trade data recorded at gross value exaggerate bilateral imbalances — hence the OECD-WTO Trade in Value Added accounting (TiVA), where "how much value added did each party contribute to an export" is displacing "what did each party export" as the basic question of research. Second, the old map of a "developed-versus-developing" dichotomy has given way to a multipolar order: emerging economies are at once capital exporters and major manufacturers, and the traditional North-South analytical frame needs rewriting. Third, the "efficiency first" logic of globalization is being recalibrated against a national-security logic: the great-power tariff wars since 2018, the financial sanctions and export controls since 2022, and the competition in industrial subsidies all push the textbook reference point of "free trade is optimal" towards "a trade-off between efficiency and security" — that is the historical backdrop running through the whole text, and the question Chapter 10's reconsideration of globalization will come back to.
