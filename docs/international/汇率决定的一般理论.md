# Chapter 6 The general theory of exchange rate determination

> **Core question of this chapter**: where does the ratio between two paper currencies come from? Why do some exchange-rate theories govern the long run while others govern the short run? Why does the currency with the higher interest rate trade at a forward discount?

## The arc of this chapter

Exchange-rate doctrine has evolved in lockstep with the changing monetary regimes. Under the gold standard (1870–1914) there was no problem of "exchange rate determination": each country's currency was fixed at the ratio of its gold content (mint parity), the rate fluctuated only within the narrow band between the gold points, and the only theoretical question was short-run fluctuation — which is why Goschen's international indebtedness theory of 1861 needed to answer just one thing, "what determines the rate within the gold points" (the supply and demand for foreign exchange arising from debts and credits already due for settlement). The First World War destroyed the gold standard, the ratio between paper currencies lost its objective anchor, and the chaos of the 1920s — German hyperinflation, Britain's mistaken return to gold in 1925 at the prewar parity, the collapse of exports Keynes predicted in *The Economic Consequences of Mr. Churchill* arriving on schedule — bred a new generation of doctrine: Cassel's purchasing power parity theory, proposed in 1918 and systematised in 1922, which prices paper-currency rates by the ratio of the two countries' price levels, "the domestic value of money determines its external value", and so supplies an anchor for rebuilding postwar rates; Aftalion's psychology theory of exchange of 1927, standing on the Austrian subjective theory of value and explaining with the psychological appraisal of the marginal utility of money those violent swings in the franc crisis that fundamentals cannot account for; and the interest rate parity theory, set out systematically in Keynes's *Tract on Monetary Reform* of 1923 and supplemented by Einzig's *Theory of Forward Exchange* of 1937, answering the relation between spot and forward rates under arbitrage flows. After the Bretton Woods system collapsed in 1973 and the floating-rate era opened, exchange-rate volatility outran every traditional doctrine's predictions, and the theory turned over again: Dornbusch's overshooting model of 1976 (sticky commodity prices, elastic asset prices — the rate over-adjusts in the short run and then swings back), the monetarist exchange-rate model (Frankel 1976) and the portfolio balance model (Branson 1977) all cast the exchange rate as an asset price. This chapter arranges its concepts and theory in the order "gold-standard legacy → purchasing power parity → international indebtedness → exchange psychology → interest rate parity → scale of reserves", and closes with the Meese–Rogoff puzzle, which marks the common ceiling that all exchange-rate theories reach on short-run prediction.

## Core concepts

### Foreign exchange

Foreign exchange is the means of international payment, or the claim, held by a monetary authority (central bank, monetary agency, exchange stabilisation fund or treasury) in the form of bank deposits, treasury bills, long- and short-term government securities and so on, which can be used for payment when the balance of payments is in deficit. Foreign exchange carries both a dynamic and a static meaning: dynamic foreign exchange refers to the specialised business activity of exchanging the currency of one country for that of another in order to discharge international debts and credits; the ordinary use of the term usually means the static sense — means of payment for international settlement denominated in foreign currency. The IMF's definition also takes the static sense.

The split between the two meanings corresponds to two disciplinary viewpoints: the "exchange activity" belongs to practice and markets (foreign-exchange market operations, trading mechanisms), while "means of payment and claims" belongs to reserves and policy (that portion of foreign-currency assets in the central bank's hands) — this chapter, and the foreign exchange covered in the monetary and financial chapters, take the latter as the main object. The qualifier "held by a monetary authority" in the IMF definition sets it apart from ordinary foreign-currency assets: foreign-currency deposits held by residents are not foreign exchange in the sense of reserves. China's *Foreign Exchange Administration Regulations*, revised in 2008, adopt a broader functional definition (foreign-currency payment instruments, foreign-currency securities, special drawing rights and other foreign exchange assets) — keep the different scopes in mind when reading the statute and when reading a textbook.

### The exchange rate and methods of quotation

The exchange rate, also called the "rate of exchange", the "foreign-exchange rate" or the "foreign-exchange quotation", is the price of buying and selling foreign exchange — the relative ratio of two countries' currencies, or the price of one country's currency expressed in another. Converting between two currencies first requires fixing which country's currency is the standard, that is, the method of quoting the exchange rate. The direct quotation method, also called the "payable quotation", takes a fixed unit of foreign currency as the standard and converts it into a fixed amount of domestic currency — the foreign currency stays put and the domestic currency moves. The indirect quotation method, also called the "receivable quotation", takes a fixed unit of domestic currency as the standard and expresses the price of the domestic currency in foreign currency — the domestic currency stays put and the foreign currency moves.

The choice of quotation method is not a matter of doctrine but a residue of history and trading convenience: London, the world's financial centre in the nineteenth century, found it most convenient for merchants to quote prices worldwide in pounds sterling, so the pound has retained the indirect quotation to this day (together with the heirs of its colonial trading sphere — the Australian dollar, the New Zealand dollar, the now-defunct Irish pound, and the euro and US dollar after 1999), while most countries in the world, including China, use the direct quotation. The practical trap of the quotation method lies in the direction of "appreciation" and "depreciation": under a direct quotation a rising number means the domestic currency depreciates (buying one dollar costs more renminbi), whereas under an indirect quotation a rising number means the domestic currency appreciates — so the first step when comparing exchange-rate data across markets is always to confirm the direction of quotation.

### Fixed and liquid indebtedness

Fixed indebtedness refers to debts and credits formed in international borrowing but not yet entered into actual payment; liquid indebtedness refers to debts and credits that have been formed and have entered actual payment. The international indebtedness theory (Goschen 1861) holds that fixed indebtedness has no substantial determining effect on the course of the exchange rate (sometimes even the opposite — the domestic currency can strengthen when the debt is established), and only liquid indebtedness determines the actual supply and demand for foreign exchange and hence the exchange rate.

The elegance of this pair of concepts lies in the two words "come due": buying and selling in the foreign-exchange market springs from a demand for currency to be delivered now, and contracted debts not yet due generate no actual foreign-exchange trading before the maturity date — so what determines the spot rate is the part "being settled", not the part "on the books". The indebtedness theory was a fairly effective explanation of short-run exchange-rate fluctuation under the gold standard (the long-run anchor was supplied by mint parity); its limitation is that it speaks of supply and demand without speaking of the price mechanism behind them. Purchasing power parity supplies this link from the side of "quality" (the purchasing power of money), and the psychology theory of exchange supplies "expectations" — theories pass the baton to one another rather than replacing each other.

### The law of one price

The law of one price is a theory about the regularity of international pricing of commodities under free trade: under free trade, traders' arbitrage behaviour will make the price of the same tradeable commodity, sold anywhere, identical once transportation costs are deducted.

The law of one price is the micro-foundation of purchasing power parity: arbitrage on individual commodities (buy low and sell high when the price gap between two places exceeds freight) guarantees international convergence of tradeable-goods prices, and aggregating the law of one price across all commodities (weighted into a price index) yields purchasing power parity, in which the ratio of the purchasing powers of two countries' currencies determines the exchange rate. Note the boundary of the law's application: it constrains only "tradeable goods" (manufactures, bulk commodities), while non-tradeable items such as haircuts and rent cannot be arbitraged and so do not obey it — and this gap is precisely the point of entry for the Balassa–Samuelson effect (see "Limits and what follows") in explaining the systematic deviation of PPP; transportation costs and trade barriers determine the friction to arbitrage, and the container revolution of the second half of the twentieth century (transport costs down by an order of magnitude) greatly improved the law's fit with reality.

### Premium and discount

Premium and discount are symmetrical concepts: a premium refers to a rise in the rate of exchange when monetary funds are switched and exchanged between two different points in time, places or currencies; a discount refers to a reduction in the rate of exchange. Under a direct quotation a premium means the forward rate is above the spot rate, a discount the reverse, and the opposite holds under an indirect quotation. The premium and discount formula is:

$$E_t - E_0 = E_0(R_h - R_f)$$

where $E_t$ is the forward rate, $E_0$ the spot rate, $R_f$ the foreign interest rate and $R_h$ the domestic interest rate. In general, the currency with the higher interest rate is mostly at a forward discount, and the currency with the lower interest rate is mostly at a forward premium.

"The currency with the higher interest rate trades at a forward discount" looks counter-intuitive at first, but is the necessary outcome of riskless arbitrage equilibrium (the derivation is in Problems and analysis): if the high-interest currency did not trade at a forward discount, everybody could "borrow at the low rate, swap into high-interest deposits and then swap back at an unchanged forward price" and earn a riskless profit, and capital flows would immediately raise the spot price of the high-interest currency and push down its forward price, until the premium or discount exactly offsets the interest differential — the arbitrage opportunity is closed off. This relation of "forward premium or discount ≈ interest differential" is interest rate parity, and its staying power in practice exceeds a century in both theory and case: the forward rates banks quote to clients are still computed directly from the interest-rate difference between the two countries.

### Fixed exchange rates and floating exchange rates

A fixed exchange rate is a regime in which the government selects a basic reference by administrative or legal means and fixes, publishes and maintains a fixed ratio between its currency and that reference; the fixed rates under the gold standard and the Bretton Woods system were determined by the ratio of the currencies' gold contents, with fluctuation confined within the gold points or the agreed band. Fixed rates were abolished worldwide around 1973. A floating exchange rate is one at which a country's currency moves freely up and down according to market supply and demand, and the state has no obligation to maintain it; in practice there are two varieties, full free float and managed float.

The real-world "fixed versus floating" is a continuum rather than an either-or choice: the currency board (Hong Kong's linked exchange rate, the rate 100 per cent locked but monetary autonomy given up), crawling peg, target range and managed float (China: from 2005 a reference to a currency basket in adjustment, after the "8·11" reform of 2015 the central rate determined more by the previous day's close and a currency basket, and from 2017 a counter-cyclical factor) form the intermediate forms. The trade-offs of institutional choice are precisely the theme of the trilemma in Chapter 8 — here we first keep the facts: in the "floating-rate era" after 1973 the overwhelming majority of economies practise a managed float, and pure free float exists only in a handful of large advanced economies.

## Problems and analysis

### Deriving the forward exchange rate and the premium and discount formulae

The basic idea of interest rate parity: the return on a sum invested at home and the return on the same sum invested abroad should be equal, otherwise arbitrage capital flows will bring the two into line.

**(1) The forward exchange rate formula**

If the domestic currency is invested domestically, the amount plus interest after a year is $Y(1 + R_h)$. Invest the domestic currency abroad: convert it at the spot rate $E_0$ into $Y/E_0$ of foreign currency, deposit it in a bank for a year to receive $(Y / E_0)(1 + R_f)$, and at the same time sell one-year forward foreign exchange at the forward rate $E_t$ to lock in the domestic-currency return, so that at maturity it is converted back into domestic currency as $(Y \cdot E_t (1 + R_f))/E_0$. By equality of returns (the law of one price applied in financial markets):

$$Y(1 + R_h) = \frac{Y \cdot E_t (1 + R_f)}{E_0}$$

Simplifying gives the forward exchange rate formula:

$$E_t = E_0 \cdot \frac{1 + R_h}{1 + R_f}$$

**(2) The premium and discount formula**

Computing the difference between the forward and the spot rate:

$$E_t - E_0 = E_0 \cdot \frac{1 + R_h}{1 + R_f} - E_0 = E_0 \cdot \frac{R_h - R_f}{1 + R_f}$$

When interest rates are low, approximately $1 + R_f \approx 1$, giving the premium and discount formula:

$$E_t - E_0 = E_0 (R_h - R_f)$$

**(3) The direction of the premium and discount, and the logic of interest rate parity**

From the formula: when $R_f > R_h$ (the foreign interest rate is higher) $E_t < E_0$, the foreign currency is at a discount and the domestic currency at a premium; when $R_f < R_h$ the foreign currency is at a premium; if interest rates are equal the rate is at parity. The intuitive chain: the high-interest currency attracts capital inflows and appreciates at the spot — but arbitrage capital must be converted back into the original currency at the forward at maturity, and that forward selling pressure makes it depreciate at the forward (a discount); the low-interest currency is exactly the reverse. The premium and discount are not market sentiment but compensation: the forward movement exactly offsets the interest differential, so that the two investment paths yield the same return and no riskless arbitrage remains. Put the other way, if the quoted forward rate deviates noticeably from the level indicated by the interest differential, there is an uneliminated arbitrage opportunity — and this is exactly how interest rate parity is used to test the degree of capital market integration (after 2008, "covered interest rate parity deviations" between dollars, euros and yen in advanced markets became a central banks' thermometer of liquidity stress, while deviations in renminbi forwards reflect the shadow price of capital controls).

### Indicators of the appropriate scale of a country's foreign exchange reserves

How much in foreign exchange reserves is enough? Three indicators answer from three dimensions.

**Reserves/GDP**: the ratio of foreign exchange reserves to gross domestic product, with a reasonable standard of about 10 per cent — a measure of the capacity to cope with macroeconomic imbalances from the dimension of economic scale: the larger the GDP, the larger the economy's exposure to external shocks, and the greater the reserve buffer needed.

**Reserves/external debt**: the ratio of foreign exchange reserves to total external debt, with a reasonable standard of about 30 per cent — a measure of external repayment capacity and international credit from the dimension of debt service: the more external debt, the more reserves needed to safeguard principal and interest payments; in practice it is usually used together with the finer "short-term external debt coverage" ratio (reserves covering external debt maturing within a year, the Guidotti–Greenspan rule), which international institutions widely adopted after the 1997 Asian financial crisis (the crisis countries were precisely those whose short-term external debt far exceeded their reserves).

**Reserves/imports**: how many months' worth of imports the foreign exchange reserves can pay for, with the traditional standard of three months — a measure of the capacity to meet routine imports from the dimension of liquidity, deriving from Triffin's 1947 recommendation and still the starting point for IMF assessments of reserve adequacy.

The three indicators complement one another and correspond to shocks of different character: for an economic crisis look at reserves/GDP, for a debt crisis at reserves/external debt, for a break in trade at reserves/imports, and a comprehensive assessment must look at all three together. But they are all "floor-type" rules of thumb: the true appropriate scale of reserves also depends on the exchange-rate regime (fixed rates need more reserves to intervene in the market), the openness of the capital account (the higher the openness, the more needed to resist speculative shocks) and the convenience of financing (whether it is possible to draw funds at any time from markets or from other central banks). China is the extreme case in this dimension: after 2000 reserves surged from the hundred-billion-dollar scale to a historical peak of about US$3.99 trillion in June 2014 (as published by the State Administration of Foreign Exchange), far beyond any empirical standard, and afterwards retreated with market intervention following the "8·11" reform to fluctuate around US$3.2 trillion — beyond "how much is enough", "how much is too much" (the costs of holding, of exchange-rate risk and of monetary autonomy) is the other side Chinese researchers must answer.

## Synthetic essay

### The basic content and evaluation of the purchasing power parity theory

**(1) Basic content**

The purchasing power parity theory was proposed by the Swedish economist Cassel at the end of the First World War (1918) and systematised in 1922, and its theoretical basis is the quantity theory of money. Background: after the collapse of the gold standard the exchange rate fluctuated violently, and neither the mint parity theory nor the foreign-exchange supply-and-demand theory could explain the determination of paper-currency rates. Content: the value of a country's currency and the demand for it are determined by the amount of commodities and services that a unit of the currency can buy domestically (its purchasing power), and the exchange rate between two countries' currencies is determined mainly by the ratio of their purchasing powers. There are two forms: absolute purchasing power parity — at any point in time the exchange rate is determined by the ratio of the two countries' general price levels, $E={P_a}/{P_b}$; and relative purchasing power parity — when both countries experience inflation, the nominal exchange rate equals the base-period rate multiplied by the ratio of the two countries' price indexes:

$$E_{t}=E_{0}\left[\left(P_{a t} / P_{a 0}\right) /\left(P_{b t} / P_{b 0}\right)\right]$$

where $E_0$ is the base-period exchange rate, $P_{a0}$ and $P_{at}$ are country A's price indexes in the base period and in period $t$, and $P_{b0}$ and $P_{bt}$ are the corresponding indexes for country B. The exchange rate is determined by the ratio of the two countries' purchasing powers, purchasing power depends on inflation, and inflation depends on each country's quantity of money: with everything else unchanged, the rate of the domestic currency is directly proportional to the domestic price level and inversely proportional to the foreign price level — depreciation at home must bring depreciation abroad.

**(2) Evaluation**

Standing: the purchasing power parity theory occupies an important place in foreign-exchange theory. In a world where major countries have abandoned the gold standard it points to the comparison of domestic and foreign prices as the basis for exchange-rate determination and reveals the long-run causes of exchange-rate change; it is still today the benchmark instrument for estimating the "equilibrium exchange rate" (judgements of exchange-rate misalignment, real effective exchange rate assessments in IMF Article IV consultations).

There are six defects. First, its theoretical basis is the quantity theory of money — Cassel held that the exchange ratio of paper currencies depends on purchasing power, and critics hold this to be almost a putting of the cart before the horse: the value represented by paper money does not depend on purchasing power, but rather purchasing power depends on the value represented by paper money (this is a criticism from the standpoint of the labour theory of value; within the framework of the quantity theory the circularity does not exist, and how to regard it depends on one's theoretical stance). Second, it assumes all commodities are tradeable and ignores non-tradeable items — price differences in haircuts and housing are wrongly counted into exchange-rate determination. Third, it ignores trade costs and trade barriers; arbitrage is not as smooth as the theory supposes. Fourth, it over-emphasises the effect of prices on the exchange rate, when exchange-rate change reacts back on prices too (depreciation pushes up import prices and inflation). Fifth, it ignores the impact of international capital flows on the exchange rate — the short-run rate is determined mainly by capital account transactions (from the 1970s on this point became increasingly prominent). Sixth, it is essentially static or comparative-static analysis and does not analyse in detail the transmission mechanism by which prices affect the exchange rate.

**(3) Later development**

Against the non-tradeable-goods defect, Balassa and Samuelson in 1964 gave the classical correction: in a country with high productivity in the tradeable sector (usually a rich country) wages are pushed up, and the prices of non-tradeables (services) rise broadly with them, so when the nominal exchange rate is converted at prices the rich country "looks more expensive" — the deviation of PPP is systematic rather than noise, and the real exchange rate appreciates over the long run with per-capita income. The "Big Mac index" founded by *The Economist* in 1986 demonstrates this effect intuitively with the price of the same hamburger in different countries and has become the best-known economics popularisation indicator; for China, in the debate over the renminbi's continued appreciation after the 2005 reform, the Balassa–Samuelson effect (rapid productivity growth in the tradeable sector) was the core argument of those advocating appreciation, and the structural slowdown of productivity growth is one of the later explanations for the slowing appreciation.

### The basic content and analysis of the psychology theory of exchange

**(1) Basic content**

The psychology theory of exchange is the doctrine of exchange-rate determination of the 1920s (represented by Aftalion in 1927), and its theoretical basis is the Austrian subjective utility theory. Its basic content has four points: first, the international indebtedness theory and purchasing power parity explain exchange-rate determination respectively from "quantity" and "quality", and a complete doctrine must be a unity of quality and quantity (in the formulation of this theory, the order in the international indebtedness theory is "international indebtedness → purchasing power → exchange rate"); second, people demand foreign exchange because it can buy foreign commodities and services, and foreign commodities and services have the utility of satisfying demand, and this utility is the value basis of foreign exchange; third, the real value of foreign exchange lies in its marginal utility, marginal utility is determined by people's subjective psychological appraisal, and different appraisals mean different buying and selling, so the supply and demand for foreign exchange change accordingly and ultimately the exchange rate changes; fourth, the psychological appraisal is determined by factors of quality and quantity: the quality factors are the specific purchasing power of the currency over commodities and its uses in debt payment and speculation, and the quantity factors are the size and sign of the balance of payments, inflation, and capital inflows and outflows. Countless individual demands and supplies make up the market's, and their intersection is the price of foreign exchange.

**(2) Evaluation**

The psychology theory of exchange substitutes subjective judgement for the objective process and is, on its philosophical foundation, idealist — this is its fundamental limitation. But it captured a dimension that the other two doctrines ignored: under the paper-currency standard, and especially in an environment where international capital shocks the financial markets heavily, the influence of people's expectations and confidence on the course of the exchange rate grows ever greater — in the 1920s franc crisis, pessimistic expectations about the budget outlook drove capital flight and the exchange rate collapsed further than any purchasing power parity calculation predicted, and this is precisely the site where the psychology theory was born. From a contemporary viewpoint, the doctrine foreshadowed in crude form the direction that later became rigorous: the rational expectations school brought expectations into macroeconomic models (from the 1970s), the asset-market theory prices the exchange rate as "the discounted value of future fundamentals" — and what is being discounted is precisely expectations; and behavioural finance has rediscovered the explanatory power of irrational components (herding, overreaction) for the short-run exchange rate. "Psychological factors" have gone from a derided impurity to a standard component of exchange-rate models, only that today's psychology appears in the mathematical guise of "expectation formation mechanisms".

### The relation between purchasing power parity and the psychology theory of exchange

The two doctrines form a "long run — short run" complementarity in exchange-rate determination: purchasing power parity, resting on the objective quantity theory of money, explains the long-run trend of the exchange rate with price levels (purchasing power) — inflation differentials drive purchasing power differentials, and purchasing power differentials drive long-run exchange rates; it can explain inflation-driven depreciation but cannot explain the panic crashes in crises or the "confidence premium" of reserve currencies. The psychology theory of exchange, resting on the subjective utility theory, explains short-run fluctuation with people's psychological appraisals and expectations — expectations can be self-fulfilling (expect depreciation → capital flight → actual depreciation), it is strong on the short run and on crisis situations but lacks an objective anchor. The contemporary synthesis is "look at purchasing power for the long run, look at expectations for the short run": the famous test by Meese and Rogoff in 1983 drew the precise boundary of this division of labour — over horizons of a month to a year, no exchange-rate model based on macroeconomic fundamentals (including the monetary model and the portfolio model) predicts out of sample better than a naive random walk, and purchasing power parity shows explanatory power only on a scale of one to several years; the short-run dynamics of exchange rates are therefore mainly grasped through interest rate parity, order flow and microstructure research (Evans–Lyons 2002). A century of exchange-rate doctrine reached a candid maturity at this moment: we know where it anchors in the long run, why it cannot be measured in the short run, and why it cannot be measured (the asset-price character of expectations and information flow) — and that itself is important knowledge.

## Limits and what follows

The contemporary frontiers of this chapter's framework are concentrated in three places. First, **the asset-market view and the new open macroeconomics**: Dornbusch's overshooting model of 1976 explains why exchange-rate fluctuation in the floating-rate era far exceeds commodity price fluctuation (sticky commodity prices make the exchange rate bear all of the short-run adjustment), and the Redux model of Obstfeld and Rogoff in 1995 adds micro-foundations — the "asset-pricing" of exchange-rate theory is thereby complete, with interest rate parity and purchasing power parity embedded in the new framework as the no-arbitrage condition and the long-run anchor respectively. Second, **the empirical reversal of exchange-rate regimes**: the "exchange rates determined smoothly by fundamentals" predicted for the floating era did not materialise; frequent overshooting, misalignment and currency crises came instead (the 1992 European Exchange Rate Mechanism, Mexico 1994, Asia 1997, Argentina 2001), and three generations of currency crisis models (Krugman's 1979 fiscal-origin type, Obstfeld's 1994 multiple-equilibrium self-fulfilling type, and the moral-hazard type after the Asian crisis) bind exchange-rate determination to institutional fragility — what "determines the exchange rate" is increasingly not some parity but institutional credibility itself. Third, **the institutional experiment of the renminbi exchange rate**: basket reference adjustment in 2005, the "8·11" central rate reform in 2015, renminbi inclusion in the SDR basket in 2016, the counter-cyclical factor in 2017 — the process of a large economy seeking an "managed float" equilibrium point under limited capital mobility, providing a testing ground for almost every proposition of exchange-rate theory (the deviation of purchasing power parity and the Balassa–Samuelson effect, controlled deviations from interest rate parity, the appropriateness of reserve scale, the toolbox of expectation management); tracking its evolution is the best contemporary case for understanding all of this chapter's theory.
