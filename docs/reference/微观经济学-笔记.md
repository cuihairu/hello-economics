# Western Economics (Microeconomics)

## Chapter 1 Introduction

### Section 1 Human economic activity and the origin and evolution of economics

#### 1. Human economic activity

- First, economic activity is not carried out by individuals alone but by people acting together — it is a social activity, whether the society was called a tribe at the beginning or a state later on.
- Second, economic activity must be efficient, which requires a division of labour and cooperation, whether the starting point was the household division between men and women or its later evolution into the social division of labour across trades and professions.
- Third, human beings reproduce and want their lives to keep improving, so they always feel that the economic resources they live on are insufficient or scarce; this raises the questions of how to allocate limited resources to satisfy one's most important wants, and of creating new products.
- Fourth, for economic activity to continue without interruption, everyone must follow certain rules and institutions, whatever form those rules and institutions take.
- Fifth, human beings keep recognizing and recording the laws of economic activity — that is how human economic thought forms.

#### 2. What western economics is

- First, the methods and experience of business operation and management: market analysis, inventory management, product quality control, the layout of shop-floor production processes, and so on.
- Second, concentrated research on a particular economic sector, field or question: resource economics, commercial economics, agricultural economics, petroleum economics, discussions of taxation, public finance and inflation, and so on.
- Third, the study and examination of economic theory: microeconomics, macroeconomics, mathematical economics, dynamic economics, welfare economics, the history of economic thought, and so on.

#### 3. The origin and evolution of modern western economics

##### Four stages of bourgeois economics
- Mercantilism
- Classical economics
- Vulgar economics
- The period after vulgar economics

##### Traditional western economics underwent three major revisions and supplements

- The first revision and supplement concerned the problem of monopoly.
- The second revision: in 1936 Keynes published *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*. Keynes not only defended traditional western economics' praise of capitalism but also laid the theoretical foundation for government intervention in economic life in western countries.
- Hicks's *Value and Capital* (1939) brought the third revision and supplement to western economics: value theory and general equilibrium theory.

### Section 2 The object and methods of western economics

#### 1. The object of study of western economics

##### 1. What western economics studies

Economic goods: goods that can only be obtained by paying a price are called "economic goods".

Scarcity: relative to people's endless wants, the "economic goods" and the resources that produce them are always insufficient — this is scarcity.

##### 2. Resource allocation and opportunity cost

Resource allocation: to optimize the allocation of resources is to distribute limited resources according to the relative urgency of people's various wants, so that they are used sensibly.

Opportunity cost: when a given economic resource is used to produce a certain product, the largest return given up on producing other products is called the opportunity cost of producing this product.

##### 3. The economic system and the role of the market

The economic-system question: achieving the sensible allocation and use of resources and the development of new products depends on the right method, and this is usually called the economic-system question.

##### The evolution of human economic systems:
- The natural economy
- The market economy
- The planned economy
- The mixed economy

##### 4. Microeconomics and macroeconomics

Western economics:
- Microeconomics
- Macroeconomics

#### 2. The methods of western economics

##### 1. Positive analysis and normative analysis

##### Positive analysis
One approach studies only what an economic phenomenon is — what the current state of the economy is, why it is so, and how it might develop and change — without evaluating whether the phenomenon is good or whether it ought to be so. This is called positive analysis.

##### Normative analysis
The other approach makes judgements and evaluations of economic phenomena and their changes — whether they are good, whether they ought to be so. This is called normative analysis.

##### 2. The unity of opposites and equilibrium analysis
Every economic phenomenon contains contradictions within it; it is a unity of opposites.

Generally speaking, if the participants in an economic activity feel, on reaching a certain state of affairs, that they have obtained the best result, then that state of affairs is an equilibrium.

##### Equilibrium
Equilibrium as used in western economics refers to a state of no further change. "No further change" here does not mean that change is impossible, but that there is no need to change, because this state is the best and any change would only make things worse.

##### Stable equilibrium
If other forces can restore the equilibrium, it is called a stable equilibrium.

##### Unstable equilibrium
If no force can restore the equilibrium, it is called an unstable equilibrium.

##### Equilibrium analysis
- **Partial equilibrium analysis** analyses, under the assumption that other conditions are unchanged, how the price of a given commodity (or factor of production) in a given market at a given time is determined when its supply and demand reach equilibrium.
- **General equilibrium analysis** analyses the determination of the price of a commodity under the assumption that the supplies, demands and prices of all commodities and factors interact with one another, examining how the prices of all commodities and factors are determined when their supplies and demands are simultaneously in equilibrium.

##### 3. Static, comparative static and dynamic analysis

**Static analysis** analyses the equilibrium state of an economic phenomenon and the conditions the relevant economic variables must satisfy to reach it. It strips out the time element and the concrete process of change entirely — a method of examining economic phenomena in a frozen, isolated way.

**Comparative static analysis** analyses how the equilibrium state of an economic phenomenon changes after known conditions have changed, and how the relevant economic variables change in reaching the new equilibrium — that is, it compares the before and after of a one-time (not continuous) change in the relevant economic variables.

**Dynamic analysis** analyses the actual process of economic change, including the movement of the relevant variables over a period of time, their mutual influence and mutual constraint during that movement, and their rates of change at each point in time.

##### 4. The method of abstraction and economic models

An **economic model** is a theoretical structure describing the interdependencies among the economic variables related to the object under study.

##### 5. Mathematical methods and marginal analysis

A method of marginal analysis is often used in the mathematical treatment of economics, which is what makes calculus so useful in economic research. In fact, the "margin" in economics only says that people make economic decisions by choosing at the margin.

#### 3. The rational-agent assumption

The **rational-agent assumption** is a basic assumption about human economic behaviour in theoretical economic analysis. Many propositions of economics are derived under given assumptions. Under the rational-agent assumption, the basic driving force of the actors in economic activity is the maximization of benefit, so the rule of action is the optimization of a given goal. This of course does not mean the actors necessarily achieve the optimum in action, nor that the goal is necessarily a good one.

#### 4. A few principles of economics

Under the rational-agent assumption, some important and widely accepted principles of economics follow, mainly:
- (1) Since the fundamental purpose of human economic behaviour is the pursuit of happiness or benefit, people always respond to incentives in economic activity.
- (2) Since people's ultimate purpose is the pursuit of benefit, whatever they do, they compare costs and benefits.
- (3) Division of labour and specialization greatly raise people's labour productivity, make products better and better and output higher and higher; but the division of labour depends on exchange — otherwise people can neither obtain everything they need nor capture the gains specialization brings them. Conversely, exchange promotes the division of labour, making it finer and people's work more specialized, which in turn brings greater benefit. Division of labour and exchange thus reinforce one another.
- (4) The market is the bond that links and organizes human division of labour and exchange. It not only provides information for economic activity but also strengthens the vitality and drive of the actors in market competition. But exchange in markets must be free and voluntary, with transparent information, on equal and mutually beneficial terms. Otherwise transactions cannot proceed normally and the market cannot develop healthily.
- (5) Benefit maximization is always the actors' given goal, but since the past cannot be recalled and the future cannot be known, the outcome of any economic activity is always uncertain. This is risk.
- (6) Normal transactions and a healthy market depend on a sound and complete set of institutions. Institutions are the rules of behaviour without which people cannot carry out orderly market economic activity — nothing comes right without rules. Institutions consistent with the rational-agent assumption can effectively regulate people's market behaviour, reduce friction and cost in transactions, lower uncertainty and increase predictability. A good set of institutions can stir people's initiative in market activity and bring vitality to the market economy.
- (7) For the market to run and develop effectively, a capable government is indispensable. The government's position in the market economy must be reasonable, its intervention in economic activity moderate, and its supervision, inspection and handling of whether economic actors actually comply with the rules in place. Experience shows that the effective protection of property rights, the enforcement of institutions, the healthy running of the economy, the protection of the environment, and the formulation and implementation of economic development plans all depend on the government. The government must use good institutions to guide and regulate people's economic behaviour, so as to safeguard the healthy development of the social economy.

## Chapter 2 Demand, supply and equilibrium price

### Section 1 The characteristics of microeconomics

#### 1. The object of study of microeconomics

##### 1. Scarcity and choice

In economic activity human beings all face one basic fact: the scarcity of resources. Scarcity means that the resources available to an economy are limited, so it cannot produce all the goods and services people would like to have.

##### 2. Rational choice and trade-offs

The basic characteristic of the rational agent: everyone engaged in economic activity is self-interested.

Measuring cost by opportunity cost is the basis on which an economic agent maximizes its own benefit.

##### 3. The market economy: individual benefit and social benefit

In a market economy, each person's rational choice is only the pursuit of their own greatest benefit, realized in buying and selling. For the consumer, buying goods serves only to maximize their own utility; they do not pursue the producer's interest.

The spontaneous adjustment of the market price mechanism guides the behaviour of buyers and sellers, and under competitive market conditions brings about the maximization of social welfare.

##### 4. The role of government

Important as the role of the "invisible hand" is, it is not omnipotent; the market economy still needs government. The reasons:
- First, the market economy needs the government to protect property rights and to maintain the order and rules of the market.
- Second, the market economy has problems it cannot solve by itself — market failure — which also need government to solve.

The concept of demand:

The usual form of the linear demand function:

$$

$$

##### 3. The law of demand

With other factors held constant, when the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded of it falls;
when the price of a good falls, the quantity demanded of it rises.
In short, a good's price and its quantity demanded move in opposite directions.

#### 2. Movement along the demand curve versus a shift of the curve

##### 1. A change in quantity demanded: movement along the demand curve

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240413083944.png)

With other factors held constant, a change in the quantity demanded of a good caused only by a change in the good's own price is called a change in quantity demanded. Geometrically, it appears as movement along a given demand curve.

##### 2. A change in demand: a shift of the demand curve

In the market for a good, if changes in other factors increase the quantity demanded at every given price level, this is called an increase in demand. An increase in demand appears as a rightward shift of the demand curve. Conversely, if changes in other factors decrease the quantity demanded at every given price level, this is called a decrease in demand, appearing as a leftward shift. Clearly, a shift of the demand curve due to a change in demand represents a change in the entire state of demand.

### Section 4 The equilibrium price

#### 1. The meaning of equilibrium

**Equilibrium** is a widely used and important concept. In its most general sense, equilibrium refers to a relatively static state that the relevant variables of an economic matter reach under the interaction of given conditions.

**Market equilibrium**
- **Partial equilibrium** analyses the relationship between supply and demand and price, and the equilibrium state, for a single market or a group of markets.
- **General equilibrium** analyses the relationships between supply and demand and prices across all the markets of an economy, and their equilibrium state. General equilibrium assumes that the supplies, demands and prices of all goods interact; the equilibrium of any one market can be achieved only when all other markets are also in equilibrium.

#### 2. The determination of the equilibrium price

In western economics, the **equilibrium price** of a good is the price at which the market quantity demanded and the market quantity supplied of that good are equal.
The equal quantities supplied and demanded at the equilibrium price are called the **equilibrium quantity**.

### Section 5 Elasticity of demand and elasticity of supply

#### 1. The general meaning of elasticity

In economics, the general formula for elasticity is:

$$
\text{elasticity coefficient} = \frac{\text{proportional change in the dependent variable}}{\text{proportional change in the independent variable}}
$$

Elasticity formula:

$$
e = \frac{\frac{\Delta Y}{Y}}{\frac{\Delta X}{X}} = \frac{\Delta Y}{\Delta X} \cdot \frac{X}{Y}
$$

Point elasticity formula:

$$
e = \lim_{\Delta X \to 0} \frac{\frac{\Delta Y}{Y}}{\frac{\Delta X}{X}} = \frac{\frac{dY}{Y}}{\frac{dX}{X}} = \frac{dY}{dX}\cdot\frac{X}{Y}
$$

#### 2. The meaning of price elasticity of demand

The price elasticity of demand expresses how much the quantity demanded of a good responds, over a given period, to a change in that good's price.
Put differently, it expresses the percentage change in the quantity demanded brought about by a one-percent change in the good's price over a given period.

$$
\text{price elasticity of demand} = - \frac{\text{rate of change of quantity demanded}}{\text{rate of change of price}}
$$

The arc elasticity of demand expresses how much the quantity demanded between two points on a good's demand curve responds to a change in price over the arc between them.

$$
e_d = - \frac{\frac{\Delta Q}{Q}}{\frac{\Delta P}{P}} = - \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P}\cdot \frac{P}{Q}
$$

When the changes in price and the corresponding quantity demanded along the demand curve tend to the infinitesimally small, price elasticity must be expressed as point elasticity — that is, how much the quantity demanded at a point on the demand curve responds to a change in price at that point.

$$
e_d = \lim_{\Delta P \to 0} - \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P}\cdot \frac{P}{Q} 
$$

#### 5. Price elasticity of demand and the firm's sales revenue

First case: for an elastic good with $e_d > 1$, lowering the price raises the firm's sales revenue; conversely, raising the price lowers it — sales revenue moves opposite to price.
Second case: for an inelastic good with $e_d < 1$, lowering the price lowers sales revenue; conversely, raising the price raises it — sales revenue moves with price.
Third case: for a unit-elastic good with $e_d = 1$, neither lowering nor raising the price has any effect on sales revenue.

- First, the substitutability of the good.
- Second, the breadth of the good's uses.
- Third, how important the good is to the consumer's life.
- Fourth, the share of spending on the good in the consumer's total budget.
- Fifth, the time horizon over which consumers adjust the quantity demanded.

#### 7. Extending the concept of elasticity

##### 1. Price elasticity of supply

**Price elasticity of supply** expresses how much the quantity supplied of a good responds, over a given period, to a change in that good's price.
Put differently, it expresses the percentage change in the quantity supplied brought about by a one-percent change in price. It is the ratio of the rate of change of quantity supplied to the rate of change of price.

The **arc elasticity of supply** is the elasticity between two points on a good's supply curve. The point elasticity of supply is the elasticity at a point on the supply curve.

Arc elasticity of supply:

$$
e_s = \frac{\frac{\Delta Q}{Q}}{\frac{\Delta P}{P}} = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P} \cdot \frac{P}{Q}
$$

Point elasticity of supply:

$$
e_s = \lim_{\Delta P \to 0}\frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P}\cdot\frac{P}{Q} = \frac{dQ}{dP}\cdot\frac{P}{Q}
$$

Midpoint formula for the arc elasticity of supply:

$$
e_s = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta P}\cdot\frac{\frac{P_1 + P_2 }{2}}{\frac{Q_1 + Q_2}{2}}
$$

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240413173153.png)

##### 2. Cross-price elasticity of demand

The cross-price elasticity of demand expresses how much the quantity demanded of one good responds, over a given period, to a change in the price of a related good.
Put differently, it expresses the percentage change in the quantity demanded of one good brought about by a one-percent change in the price of another.

$$
e_XY = \frac{\frac{\Delta Q_X}{Q_X}}{\frac{\Delta P_Y}{P_Y}} = \frac{\Delta Q_X}{\Delta P_Y} \cdot \frac{P_Y}{Q_X}
$$

Point cross-price elasticity of demand for good X:

$$
e_XY = \lim_{\Delta P_Y \to 0}\frac{\Delta Q_X}{\Delta P_Y}\cdot\frac{P_Y}{Q_X} = \frac{dQ_X}{dP_Y}\cdot\frac{P_Y}{Q_X}
$$

##### Relations between goods
- Substitutes
	If either of two goods can replace the other in satisfying some want of the consumer, the two goods stand in a relation of substitution and are substitutes for each other.
- Complements
	If two goods must be used together to satisfy some want of the consumer, the two goods stand in a relation of complementarity and are complements of each other.

If two goods are substitutes, the price of one moves in the same direction as the quantity demanded of the other, and the cross-price elasticity of demand is positive.
If two goods are complements, the price of one moves opposite to the quantity demanded of the other, and the cross-price elasticity of demand is negative.
If two goods are unrelated, the quantity demanded of neither responds to the price of the other, and the cross-price elasticity of demand is zero.

##### 3. Income elasticity of demand

The income elasticity of demand expresses how much the quantity demanded of a good responds, over a given period, to a change in consumers' income.
Put differently, it expresses the percentage change in the quantity demanded brought about by a one-percent change in income.

Income elasticity of demand formula:

$$
e_M = \frac{\frac{\Delta Q}{Q}}{\frac{\Delta M}{M}} = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta M} \cdot \frac{M}{Q} 
$$

$$
e_M = \lim_{\Delta M \to 0}\frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta M} \cdot \frac{M}{Q} = \frac{dQ}{dM}\cdot\frac{M}{Q}
$$

Goods can be classified by the value of their income elasticity of demand:
- Normal goods, $e_M > 0$
	- Necessities: $e_M < 1$
	- Luxuries: $e_M > 1$
- Inferior goods: $e_M < 0$

**Engel's law** states: within a family or a country, the share of income spent on food falls as income rises.

In elasticity terms, Engel's law can be put as: the richer a family or a country, the smaller the income elasticity of spending on food; the poorer, the larger.

### Section 7 Concluding remarks

The main points of this chapter can be summed up as follows:
- (1) The demand for a good is the quantity consumers are willing and able to buy over a given period at each of various possible price levels. Market demand can be represented by a demand curve. The demand curve in general slopes down to the right, showing quantity demanded moving opposite to price. The supply of a good is the quantity producers are willing and able to offer for sale over a given period at each of various possible price levels. Market supply can be represented by a supply curve. The supply curve in general slopes up to the right, showing quantity supplied moving with price.
- (2) Changes in factors other than the good's own price shift the position of the demand curve or the supply curve. These are called, respectively, a change in demand and a change in supply.
- (3) The equilibrium price is the price that makes quantity demanded equal quantity supplied on the market for a good. It forms spontaneously under the market mechanism. A change in demand moves the equilibrium price in the same direction; a change in supply moves it in the opposite direction.
- (4) When a functional relation exists between two economic variables, elasticity can express how much the dependent variable responds to the independent one. Any elasticity can be expressed as either an arc elasticity or a point elasticity.
The price elasticity of demand expresses how much quantity demanded responds to price. The cross-price elasticity of demand expresses how much the quantity demanded of one good responds to the price of another. The income elasticity of demand expresses how much quantity demanded responds to income. The price elasticity of supply expresses how much quantity supplied responds to price.
- (5) With the elasticity formulas, the values of arc and point elasticities can be computed. In addition, the value of a point elasticity can be found geometrically. In general, elasticity coefficients fall into five classes by size: elastic, inelastic, unit elastic, perfectly elastic and perfectly inelastic.
- (6) As far as price elasticity of demand goes: for an elastic good, price and sales revenue move opposite to each other, so a firm can raise revenue by lowering price. For an inelastic good, price and sales revenue move together, so a firm can raise revenue by raising price. For a unit-elastic good, a change in price has no effect on sales revenue.
- (7) If two goods are substitutes, the cross-price elasticity of demand is greater than zero; if they are complements, it is less than zero; if they are unrelated, it equals zero.
- (8) For normal goods, the income elasticity of demand is greater than zero; for inferior goods, less than zero. Among normal goods, the income elasticity of necessities is less than 1, and that of luxuries greater than 1.
- (9) Engel's law: for a country or a family, as income rises, the share of total income spent on food keeps falling. In elasticity terms: the richer a family or country, the smaller the income elasticity of spending on food; the poorer, the larger.

## Chapter 3 Consumer choice

### Section 1 An overview of utility theory

#### 1. The concept of utility

**Utility** is an evaluation of a good's ability to satisfy human wants; or, more precisely, it is the degree of satisfaction a consumer experiences when consuming a good.

#### 2. Cardinal utility and ordinal utility

Analytical approaches:
- The marginal utility analysis of cardinal utility theory
- The indifference curve analysis of ordinal utility theory

#### 3. Cardinal utility theory and marginal utility analysis

##### 1. The law of diminishing marginal utility

##### (1) Total utility and marginal utility

**Total utility (TU)** is the sum of all the utility a consumer gets from consuming a given quantity of a good over a given period.

**Marginal utility (MU)** is the increment of utility a consumer gets from consuming one additional unit of a good over a given period.

Total utility function:

$$
TU=f(Q)
$$

Marginal utility function:

$$
MU = \frac{\Delta TU(Q)}{\Delta Q}
$$

$$
MU=\lim_{\Delta Q \to 0}\frac{\Delta TU(Q)}{\Delta Q} = \frac{dTU(Q)}{dQ}
$$

$$
\text{marginal amount} = \frac{\text{change in the dependent variable}}{\text{change in the independent variable}}
$$

##### (2) The law of diminishing marginal utility and its cause

**The law of diminishing marginal utility states**: within a given period, with the consumption of other goods held constant, as a consumer increases their consumption of a good, the increment of utility — the marginal utility — obtained from each successive unit of that good diminishes.

##### 2. The marginal utility of money

For a consumer, as monetary income keeps rising, the marginal utility of money diminishes.
That is, as a consumer's monetary income grows step by step, the marginal utility brought by each additional yuan generally becomes smaller.

##### 3. Consumer equilibrium

**Consumer equilibrium** studies how an individual consumer distributes limited monetary income among purchases of various goods in order to obtain the greatest utility.

Equilibrium condition for utility maximization:

$$
P_1 X_1 + P_2 X_2 + ...+P_n X_n = I
$$

$$
\frac{MU_1}{P_1} = \frac{MU_2}{P_2}=...=\frac{MU_n}{P_n}= \lambda 
$$

The consumer should choose the optimal bundle so that the marginal utility obtained from the last yuan spent on each good is equal, and equal to the marginal utility of money.

### Section 2 Indifference curves

#### 1. Assumptions about preferences

A preference is an ordering a consumer makes of any two bundles of goods.

Ordinal utility theory puts forward three basic assumptions about consumer preferences:
- The first is completeness of preference.
	Completeness means the consumer can always compare and order any two given bundles of goods.
- The second is transitivity of preference.
	Transitivity means that for any three bundles A, B and C, if the consumer prefers A to B and B to C, then, between A and C, the consumer must prefer A to C. The assumption of transitivity guarantees that consumer preferences are consistent, and therefore rational.
- The third is non-satiation.
	This assumption says that if the only difference between two bundles is the quantity of one good, the consumer always prefers the bundle containing more of it — that is, the consumer has not reached a point of satiation for any good.

#### 2. Indifference curves and their properties

An indifference curve represents all the bundles of two goods that give the consumer the same preference. Or: all the bundles of two goods that bring the same level of utility, or the same degree of satisfaction.

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240420185600.png)

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240420185613.png)

The **utility function** represents the level of utility a bundle brings the consumer.

$$
U=f(X_1,X_2) = U^0
$$

$U^0$ is a constant, representing an unchanging level of utility. This utility function is sometimes also called an **iso-utility function**.

##### An indifference curve has three basic properties:

- The first: since the utility function is usually assumed continuous, infinitely many indifference curves can lie between any two indifference curves in the same plane.
- The second: any two indifference curves in the same plane do not intersect.
- The third: indifference curves are convex toward the origin. That is, they not only slope down to the right — their slope is negative — but slope down convexly toward the origin, so the absolute value of their slope is diminishing.

### Section 3 Utility maximization and consumer choice

##### 1. The budget line

##### 1. The meaning of the budget line

The budget line is also called the budget constraint, the line of consumption possibilities and the price line.
The **budget line** represents all the combinations of two goods that a consumer can buy with their entire income, given the income and the goods' prices.

##### 2. Utility maximization and consumer choice

The consumer's optimal purchase behaviour must satisfy two conditions:
- First, the optimal bundle must be the one the consumer prefers most. That is, the optimal bundle must bring the consumer the greatest utility.
- Second, the optimal bundle must lie on the given budget line.

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240420204605.png)

Equilibrium point:

$$
MRS_{12}= \frac{P_1}{P_2}
$$

This is the equilibrium condition for consumer utility maximization.
It says: under a given budget constraint, to obtain the greatest utility the consumer should choose the bundle so that the marginal rate of substitution of the two goods equals the ratio of their prices.
It can also be read as: at the consumer's equilibrium point, the quantity of the other good the consumer is willing to give up for one unit of a good (that is, $MRS_{12}$) must equal the quantity of the other good the consumer can actually get for one unit of that good in the market (that is, $\frac{P_1}{P_2}$).

### Section 4 The effect of price and income changes on consumer equilibrium

##### 1. A price change: the price-consumption curve

The price-consumption curve is the locus of the consumer's utility-maximizing equilibrium points corresponding to different price levels of a good, with preferences, income and the prices of other goods held constant.

The demand curve derived by ordinal utility theory in general slopes down to the right, showing price and quantity demanded moving in opposite directions. In particular, the quantity demanded at every price level on the demand curve is the equilibrium quantity that brings the consumer the greatest utility.

##### 3. An income change: the income-consumption curve

The income-consumption curve is the locus of the consumer's utility-maximizing equilibrium points corresponding to different income levels, with preferences and prices held constant.

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240420232722.png)

##### 4. The Engel curve

The Engel curve represents the quantity demanded of a good at each income level.

### Section 5 The substitution effect and the income effect

#### 1. The meaning of the substitution effect and the income effect

When the price of a good changes, two effects are produced for the consumer:
- One is a change in the consumer's real income. Here, a change in real income is defined as a change in the level of utility.
- The other is a change in the relative prices of goods. Both changes alter the quantity demanded of the good in question.

**Total effect = substitution effect + income effect.**
The change in the relative prices of goods caused by the change in the price of a good, and through it the change in the quantity demanded of the good, is called the substitution effect. The change in real income caused by the change in price, and through it the change in the quantity demanded, is called the income effect. The substitution effect leaves the consumer's level of utility unchanged; the income effect reflects a change in the level of utility.

#### 2. The substitution and income effects for a normal good

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240421153806.png)

The compensating budget line is used to show: when a change in price alters the consumer's real income, imagine a certain monetary compensation given to the consumer so as to bring them back to their original real income, that is, to their original level of utility.

For a normal good, the substitution effect moves opposite to price, and the income effect also moves opposite to price; together, the total effect must move opposite to price. This is why the demand curve of a normal good slopes down to the right.

#### 3. The distinction between normal and inferior goods, and the income effect

Normal and inferior goods differ as follows:
The quantity demanded of a normal good moves with the consumer's income — it rises as income rises and falls as income falls.
The quantity demanded of an inferior good moves opposite to income — it falls as income rises and rises as income falls.

For an inferior good, the income effect moves with price.

#### 4. The substitution and income effects for an inferior good

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240421160442.png)

For an inferior good, the substitution effect moves opposite to price and the income effect moves with it; and in most cases the substitution effect is stronger than the income effect, so the total effect moves opposite to price and the demand curve slopes down to the right.

#### 5. A special case: the substitution and income effects for a Giffen good

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240421160704.png)

A Giffen good is a special kind of inferior good.
As an inferior good, the substitution effect for a Giffen good moves opposite to price and the income effect moves with it.

What is special about a Giffen good is that its income effect is so large that it outweighs the substitution effect, so the total effect moves with price. This is why the demand curve of a Giffen good has the unusual shape of sloping up to the right.

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240421160806.png)

### Section 6 The market demand curve

Market demand for a good is the quantity of it demanded by all consumers in the market, over a given period, at each of various price levels.

The quantity of market demand for a good is the sum of all consumers' quantities demanded at each price level.

Since the market demand curve is the horizontal summation of individual consumers' demand curves, it in general slopes down to the right, like an individual's.

Every point on the market demand curve represents the quantity of market demand that, at the corresponding price level, brings all consumers the greatest utility, or the greatest satisfaction.

### Section 7 Uncertainty and choice

#### 1. Uncertainty and risk

Uncertainty means that an economic actor cannot know in advance the exact outcome of a decision, or that the possible outcomes of a decision are more than one.

If a consumer knows the possible outcomes of a decision, and also knows the probabilities of those outcomes, this uncertain situation is called risk.

#### 2. Uncertainty and lotteries

Since any prize a consumer wins can always be expressed as a certain sum of money, in economic analysis every possible outcome of a lottery is abstracted as a monetary amount.

#### 3. Expected utility and the utility of the expected value

##### 1. Expected utility

For a lottery $L=(p;W_1，W_2)$, the expected utility function of the lottery is:

$$
E(U(W_1,W_2))=pU(W_1) + (1-p)U(W_2)
$$

A consumer's expected utility is the weighted average of the utilities of the various outcomes they may get under uncertainty.

##### 2. The utility of the expected value

For a lottery $L=(p；W_1，W_2）$, the expected value of the lottery is:

$$
pW_1 + (1-p)W_2
$$

The utility of the lottery's expected value is:

$$
U(pW_1 + (1-p)W_2)
$$

#### 4. The consumer's attitude to risk

Lottery $L=(p;W_1,W_2)$: one could instead hold a certain amount of wealth equal to the lottery's expected value:

$$
pW_1 + (1-p)W_2 
$$

Western economists divide consumers' attitudes to risk into three kinds:
- Risk averter: $U(pW_1 +(1-p)W_2) > pU(W_1) + (1-p)U(W_2)$
- Risk lover: $U(pW_1 +(1-p)W_2) < pU(W_1) + (1-p)U(W_2)$
- Risk neutral: $U(pW_1 +(1-p)W_2) = pU(W_1) + (1-p)U(W_2)$

### Section 8 Concluding remarks

The main points of this chapter can be summed up as follows:
- (1) The pursuit of utility maximization is the consumer's behavioural goal. Consumer choice theory divides into cardinal utility theory and ordinal utility theory. Cardinal utility theorists use marginal utility analysis to study consumer behaviour; ordinal utility theorists use indifference curve analysis. In contemporary western economics, the dominant approach is that of ordinal utility theory. This chapter mainly presents the indifference curve analysis of ordinal utility theory.
- (2) The law of diminishing marginal utility in cardinal utility theory says: within a given period, with other conditions unchanged, as a consumer continuously increases consumption of a good, the marginal utility obtained from each successive unit diminishes. Based on this law, the equilibrium condition for consumer utility maximization is: the consumer should make the marginal utility of the last yuan spent on each good equal, and equal to the marginal utility of money. The consumer's demand curve derived from this equilibrium condition slopes down to the right.
- (3) Ordinal utility theory uses indifference curve analysis to examine consumer behaviour. Given preferences, income and prices, the unique tangency between the consumer's budget line and one indifference curve in the family represents consumer equilibrium. At the equilibrium point of utility maximization the budget line and the indifference curve have equal slopes — the marginal rate of substitution of the two goods equals the ratio of their prices. The equilibrium condition of ordinal utility theory and that of cardinal utility theory are essentially the same.
- (4) Starting from the equilibrium point of consumer utility maximization one obtains the locus of equilibrium points corresponding to different price levels of a good — the price-consumption curve. From it one can further derive the consumer's demand curve, which in general slopes down to the right. The demand curve says: the quantity at every price level on it is the optimal consumption level that brings the consumer the greatest utility. Horizontally summing the demand curves of all consumers in a market gives the market demand curve for that good.
- (5) Starting from the equilibrium point of consumer utility maximization one obtains the locus of equilibrium points corresponding to different income levels — the income-consumption curve. From it one can further derive the Engel curve. The Engel curve of a normal good has a positive slope; that of an inferior good, a negative one.
- (6) Consumer surplus is the difference between the maximum total price a consumer is willing to pay for a given quantity and the total price actually paid. An individual's consumer surplus is the area below their demand curve and above the market price line. Market consumer surplus is the sum of all consumers' surpluses, the area below the market demand curve and above the market price line. A change in the price of a good brings a change in consumer surplus.
- (7) The total effect of a good equals the substitution effect plus the income effect. Any good's price moves opposite to the substitution effect. For a normal good, price moves opposite to the income effect; for an inferior good, price moves with it. Therefore, for a normal good, price and the total effect move in opposite directions — its demand curve slopes down to the right. For an inferior good, in most cases the substitution effect outweighs the income effect, so for most inferior goods price and the total effect also move in opposite directions and the demand curve slopes down to the right. For the special class of Giffen goods among inferior goods, the substitution effect is weaker than the income effect, so price and the total effect move in the same direction — the demand curve slopes up to the right.
- (8) A consumer's attitude to risk divides into three kinds: risk aversion, risk loving and risk neutrality. The test is: assume the certain income the consumer would have without buying the lottery equals the lottery's expected value — if the utility of the expected value is respectively greater than, less than or equal to the expected utility of the lottery, the consumer is respectively risk averse, risk loving or risk neutral.

## Chapter 4 Production technology

### Section 1 Basic concepts of the firm and of production

#### 1. The firm

In microeconomics, the producer is also called a firm or an enterprise, meaning a single economic unit that can make production decisions as one.

##### 1. Forms of organization of the firm

There are mainly three forms of organization:
- The sole proprietorship
- The partnership
- The corporation

A sole proprietorship is a firm owned and run by a single individual.

A partnership is a firm jointly owned and run by two or more people.

A corporation is a firm established and run under company law, with legal personality.

##### 2. The firm's objective

Achieving profit maximization is the basic rule for any enterprise competing for survival.

#### 2. Production

##### 1. The production function

The production function expresses the relationship, over a given period and under given technology, between the quantities of all the factors used in production and the **maximum output** that can be produced.

$$
Q = f(L,K)
$$

$L$ denotes the quantity of labour input
$K$ denotes the quantity of capital input

##### 2. The short run and the long run in production

The **short run in production** is a period in which the producer does not have time to adjust the quantities of all factors — at least one factor is fixed.

The **long run in production** is a period in which the producer can adjust all factors.

### Section 2 The short-run production function

$$
Q=f(L,\bar{K}) 
$$

$\bar{K}$ denotes that capital input is fixed
$L$ is the variable input

#### 1. Total, average and marginal product

- Total product of labour: $TP_L = f(L,\bar{K})=Q$
- Average product of labour: $AP_L = \frac{TP_L(L,\bar{K})}{L}$
- Marginal product of labour: $MP_L=\frac{\Delta TP_L(L,\bar{K})}{\Delta L}=\frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta L}$

The total product of labour, $TP_L$, is the maximum output corresponding to each quantity of the variable factor labour.

The average product of labour, $AP_L$, is the output per unit of the variable labour input on average.

The marginal product of labour, $MP_L$, is the increase in output from one additional unit of the variable labour input.

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240421175515.png)

#### 2. The law of diminishing marginal returns

##### 1. The law of diminishing marginal returns

A phenomenon occurs in all short-run production: with technology and other factors unchanged, as a variable factor is added in equal increments to one or more factors held fixed, the marginal product of that variable factor first increases, and once its input passes a certain point it begins to diminish. This is the law of diminishing marginal returns in short-run production, also called the law of diminishing marginal product or the law of diminishing returns.

##### 2. Technological progress and the marginal product curves

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240422092309.png)

#### 3. The relationship among total, average and marginal product

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240422092413.png)

##### 1. Marginal product and total product

Their general relationship can be put simply:
as long as marginal product is positive, total product always rises;
as long as marginal product is negative, total product always falls;
when marginal product is zero, total product is at its maximum.

##### 2. Marginal product and average product

For marginal product and average product, the formula says:
when $MP_L > AP_L$, $AP_L$ is rising;
when $MP_L < AP_L$, $AP_L$ is falling;
when $MP_L = AP_L$, $AP_L$ is at its maximum.

##### 3. Average product and total product

The slope of the line from the origin to any point on the $TP_L$ curve represents the corresponding average product.

#### 4. The three stages of short-run production

The second stage is the decision region for a rational firm's short-run production — also called the reasonable range of factor input.

### Section 3 The long-run production function

#### 1. Isoquants

##### 1. Isoquants and their properties

Long-run production function:

$$
Q = f(L,K) = Q^0
$$

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240422095112.png)

An isoquant is the locus of all combinations of two factors that produce the same level of output, with technology held constant.

##### 2. The marginal rate of technical substitution

Holding the output level constant, the amount of one factor that must be given up when one unit of another factor is added is called the marginal rate of technical substitution between the two factors.

$$
MRTS_{LK} = - \frac{\Delta K}{\Delta L}
$$

$$
MRTS_{LK} = \lim_{\Delta L \to 0}-\frac{\Delta K}{\Delta L} = -\frac{dK}{dL}
$$

##### 3. The diminishing marginal rate of technical substitution

A phenomenon occurs in all long-run production:
holding output constant, as the input of one variable factor keeps increasing, the amount of the other variable factor that each additional unit of the first can substitute diminishes.

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240422100903.png)

##### 4. Technological progress and isoquants

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240422102252.png)

#### 2. Isoquants of different shapes

##### 1. A fixed-proportions-substitution production function (also called a linear production function)

A fixed-proportions-substitution production function expresses that the rate of substitution between any two factors is fixed at every output level.

$$
Q = aL +bK
$$

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240422102649.png)

##### 2. A fixed-input-proportions production function (also called a Leontief production function)

A fixed-input-proportions production function expresses that the ratio between the inputs of any pair of factors is fixed at every output level.

$$
Q=min\left\lbrace \frac{L}{\mu } ,\frac{K}{\upsilon } \right\rbrace 
$$

Output $Q$ depends on the smaller of the two ratios $\frac{L}{\mu}$ and $\frac{K}{\upsilon}$. It is usually assumed that $L$ and $K$ in the expression both meet the efficiency requirement of the minimum factor bundle:

$$
Q=\frac{L}{\mu} = \frac{K}{\upsilon}
$$

$$
\frac{K}{L} = \frac{\upsilon}{\mu}
$$

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240422104423.png)

##### 3. The Cobb-Douglas production function

The general form of the Cobb-Douglas production function is:

$$
Q=AL^{\alpha }K^{\beta }
$$

where the constants $A$, $\alpha$, $\beta> 0$

Isoquants:

$$
Q=AL^{\alpha }K^{\beta } = Q^0
$$

#### 3. Returns to scale

In **long-run production**, changes in the scale of production naturally change output.

Changes in returns to scale come in three kinds: constant, increasing and diminishing returns to scale.
- Constant returns to scale: the proportionate increase in output equals the proportionate increase in all factor inputs.
- Increasing returns to scale: the proportionate increase in output exceeds the proportionate increase in all factor inputs.
- Diminishing returns to scale: the proportionate increase in output falls short of the proportionate increase in all factor inputs.

### Section 4 Concluding remarks

The main points of this chapter can be summed up as follows:
- (1) A producer or firm is an individual economic unit that can make production decisions as one. The objective a firm pursues in production is profit maximization.
- (2) A firm's production can be divided into short-run and long-run. The short run is a period in which at least one factor cannot be adjusted; the long run is a period in which all factors can be adjusted. Correspondingly, we discuss the technical characteristics of the short run and the long run with the short-run production function and the long-run production function respectively.
- (3) The basic law of short-run production is the law of diminishing marginal returns. The law says: in short-run production of any good, with technology and other conditions unchanged, the marginal product of any variable factor will necessarily pass from an increasing stage to a diminishing stage. That is, any variable factor's marginal product curve has an inverted-U shape — rising first, then falling. From the shape of the short-run marginal product curve, one can derive the short-run total and average product curves and see how these curves relate to one another. Short-run production divides into three stages, and the reasonable range for a firm's factor inputs is the second stage, also called the firm's decision region.
- (4) From the long-run production function one obtains isoquants. An isoquant represents all combinations of two factors that produce the same output with technology unchanged. The slope of an isoquant is the marginal rate of technical substitution, which is diminishing. This is why isoquants in general are convex toward the origin.
- (5) With other conditions unchanged, technological progress shifts the short-run marginal product curve upward and shifts the long-run isoquants toward the origin.
- (6) Returns to scale belong to the long run. Increasing, constant and diminishing returns to scale mean respectively that in long-run production the proportionate increase in all factors is less than, equal to or greater than the proportionate increase in output it causes. As a firm expands in the long run, it generally passes through increasing returns to scale, then constant, then diminishing.

## Chapter 5 Cost

### Section 1 Basic concepts of cost

#### 1. Opportunity cost

##### 1. What is opportunity cost

Opportunity cost is the basic concept expressing the relationship between scarcity and choice.

A firm's opportunity cost is the highest revenue given up by not using the same factors of production for other purposes.

##### 2. Explicit and implicit costs

A firm's **explicit costs** of production are the actual outlays paid for factors of production bought or rented from others in the factor market.

A firm's implicit costs of production are the total value of the factors it owns and uses in its own production process. Put differently, implicit costs are input costs that require no monetary outlay from the firm.

#### 2. Accounting profit and economic profit

The cost accountants focus on is usually accounting cost, covering only explicit costs. The cost economists focus on is economic cost, considered from the standpoint of opportunity cost and made up of both explicit and implicit costs — all opportunity cost.

Accounting profit equals total revenue minus accounting cost (that is, minus explicit cost);
economic profit is accounting profit minus implicit cost, that is, total revenue minus economic cost (explicit plus implicit cost).
Evidently accounting profit is larger than economic profit, and the difference is:
accounting profit includes implicit cost, while economic profit deducts it.

In economics one must also distinguish economic profit from normal profit. From the standpoint of opportunity cost, when the owner of a firm also supplies entrepreneurial ability, the owner should pay themselves for that ability — this payment is usually called **normal profit**.

#### 3. Sunk cost

A firm needs to take opportunity cost into account in production decisions, but not sunk cost.
What is sunk cost? Sunk cost is cost already paid and impossible to recover.

### Section 2 Cost minimization

#### 1. Isocost lines

An isocost line is the locus of all combinations of two factors that a producer can buy, given cost and factor prices.

With the wage rate $w$, the price of capital, that is the interest rate, $r$, and the firm's expenditure $C$, the cost equation is:

$$
C = wL + rK
$$

$$
K=-\frac{w}{r}L +\frac{C}{r}
$$

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240422141538.png)

#### 2. Cost minimization

##### 1. Cost minimization

$$
MRTS_{LK} = - \frac{dK}{dL} = \frac{w}{r}
$$

The firm should choose the optimal bundle of factors so that the marginal rate of technical substitution of the two factors equals the ratio of their prices, achieving minimum cost for a given output.

Since the marginal rate of technical substitution can be expressed as the ratio of the marginal products of the two factors, the equation can be written as:

$$
MRTS_{LK} = \frac{MP_L}{MP_K} = \frac{w}{r}
$$

$$
\frac{MP_L}{w} = \frac{MP_K}{r}
$$

To achieve minimum cost for a given output, the firm should keep adjusting the inputs of the two factors until the marginal product of the last unit of expenditure on each factor is equal.

##### 2. Cost minimization: further thought

#### 3. The dual problem: output maximization

A producer achieves production equilibrium only when the marginal rate of technical substitution of the two factors equals the ratio of their prices.

$$
MRTS_{LK} = \frac{MP_L}{MP_K} = \frac{w}{r}
$$

$$
\frac{MP_L}{w} = \frac{MP_K}{r}
$$

By keeping adjustments of the two factors' inputs, the firm can make the marginal product of the last unit of expenditure equal whichever factor it buys with, achieving maximum output for a given cost.

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240422163737.png)

#### 4. The expansion path and total cost of production

##### 1. The expansion path

When factor prices, technology and other conditions are unchanged, changing cost shifts the isocost line in parallel; changing output shifts the isoquant in parallel. Different isoquants then touch different isocost lines, forming a series of production equilibrium points — the locus of these points is the expansion path.

It realizes either the minimum cost for a given output or the maximum output for a given cost. The expansion path is the optimal course a firm must follow when expanding or contracting production in the long run.

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240422164437.png)

##### 2. The expansion path and long-run total cost

Long-run total cost is the minimum cost of production at each output level in the long run.

##### 2. From the short-run total product curve to the short-run total cost curve

#### 2. Classification of short-run cost

There are seven kinds of short-run cost:
- Total fixed cost ($TFC$) is the total cost paid for fixed factors to produce a given quantity in the short run.
- Total variable cost ($TVC$) is the total cost paid for variable factors to produce a given quantity in the short run.
- Total cost ($TC$) is the total cost paid for all factors to produce a given quantity in the short run. $TC(Q) = TFC + TVC(Q)$
- Average fixed cost ($AFC$) is the fixed cost per unit of output on average in the short run. $AFC(Q) = \frac{TFC}{Q}$
- Average variable cost ($AVC$) is the variable cost per unit of output on average in the short run. $AVC(Q)=\frac{TVC(Q)}{Q}$
- Average total cost ($AC$) is the total cost per unit of output on average in the short run. $AC(Q) = \frac{TC(Q)}{Q}=AFC(Q)+AVC(Q)$
- Marginal cost ($MC$) is the increase in total cost from one additional unit of output in the short run. $MC(Q)=\frac{\Delta TC(Q)}{\Delta Q}=\lim_{\Delta Q \to 0}\frac{\Delta TC(Q)}{\Delta Q}=\frac{dTC}{dQ}$

#### 4. What determines short-run cost changes: the law of diminishing marginal returns

In short-run production, the increasing stage of marginal product corresponds to the diminishing stage of marginal cost, the diminishing stage of marginal product to the increasing stage of marginal cost, and the maximum of marginal product to the minimum of marginal cost. This is why, under the law of diminishing marginal returns, the marginal cost $MC$ curve has the U-shaped character of falling first and then rising.

- First, on the relationship between the $TC$, $TVC$ and $MC$ curves.
	The $MC$ value at each output level is the slope of the corresponding $TC$ curve, and since the slopes of the $TC$ and $TVC$ curves are equal at each output, the $MC$ value at each level is simultaneously the slope of the corresponding $TC$ and $TVC$ curves.
- Second, on the relationship between the $AC$, $AVC$ and $MC$ curves.
	As we know, for any pair of marginal and average quantities: as long as the marginal is below the average, it pulls the average down; as long as the marginal is above the average, it pulls the average up; and when the marginal equals the average, the average must be at its own extreme point.

### Section 4 Long-run cost curves

#### 1. The long-run total cost function and curve

Long-run total cost (LTC) is the lowest total cost a firm can reach at each output level in the long run by choosing the optimal scale of production.

$$
LTC = LTC(Q)
$$

![](../img/note/Pasted%20image%2020240423173107.png)

The long-run total cost curve is the envelope of infinitely many short-run total cost curves.

#### 2. The long-run average cost function and curve

Long-run average cost (LAC) is the lowest total cost averaged over output that a firm achieves in the long run.

$$
LAC(Q) = \frac{LTC(Q)}{Q}
$$

##### 2. The shape of the long-run average cost curve

In the early stage of a firm's expansion, the firm improves economic efficiency by enlarging the scale of production — this is economies of scale. Once expansion has reached a certain scale, further expansion lowers efficiency — this is diseconomies of scale.

If the multiplier on a firm's increase in output exceeds the multiplier on its increase in cost, that is **economies of scale**;
conversely, if the multiplier on output is smaller than the multiplier on cost, that is **diseconomies of scale**.

Since both economies and diseconomies of scale arise from a firm's own changes in the scale of production, they are also called internal economies and internal diseconomies.

In general, as a firm's scale expands from small to large, economies of scale appear first and diseconomies later. It is precisely the effects of long-run economies and diseconomies of scale that give the long-run average cost $LAC$ curve its U-shaped character of falling first and then rising.

##### 3. Shifts in the position of the long-run average cost curve

A firm's external economies arise from improvements in the outside environment on which its production depends.

If the outside environment on which the firm's production depends worsens, that is an external diseconomy for the firm.

External economies and external diseconomies are caused by factors outside the firm, and they affect the position of the firm's long-run average cost $LAC$ curve.
